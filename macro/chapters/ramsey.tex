\section{Ramsey Model}\label{sec:ramsey}

Solow determines accumulation from a given saving share. Ramsey asks how much
to consume and save when households value consumption at different dates.
The change adds a choice, a price for postponing consumption, and a boundary
condition that selects an admissible path.

\subsection{From a saving rule to a saving decision}

Suppose two economies have the same current income. In one, income is expected
to rise; in the other, a disruption has temporarily reduced production. A rule
$S=sY$ makes saving move proportionally with current output in both cases.
It cannot tell us whether households want to smooth a temporary loss, prepare
for lower future income, or invest in a newly productive opportunity.

One response would be to make $s$ a function of income or the interest rate.
But then we would still need to explain that function and how it changes when
expectations or policy change. Instead, specify what consumption is worth,
what saving buys, and which paths the household can finance. A saving rate
will emerge from those choices rather than being the starting rule.

Start with the experiment of consuming one unit less today. Saving that unit
provides a return tomorrow. The household compares the utility lost now with
the discounted utility of that future consumption. This suggests an optimality
condition across adjacent dates. It cannot yet determine the level of
consumption: a household with little wealth cannot finance every path with
the right slope. We will need feasibility and a terminal restriction as well.

The planner and household formulations then answer related questions. The
planner allocates real resources; a household takes wages and returns as given.
Their allocations coincide in the competitive benchmark under the conditions
stated below. Taxes can separate the private return from the social product,
which is why decentralization matters for the final section.

Ramsey's original contribution was a saving problem; the presentation here is
the modern discounted Ramsey--Cass--Koopmans benchmark, rather than a literal
reproduction of the 1928 objective.\footnote{Frank P. Ramsey (1928), ``A
Mathematical Theory of Saving,'' \textit{Economic Journal}, 38(152), 543--559.
\url{https://doi.org/10.2307/2224098}.}

\subsection{Assumptions}\label{assumptions}

The conditions below state the technology, ownership, choices, and market
structure explicitly. The subsequent notation and solvency sections complete
the specification before the optimization problem is solved.

\begin{modelsetup}[1]
\begin{modelcondition}{01}{Economy and production technology}
A closed, one-good economy contains households and firms, without government
in the baseline. Output can be consumed or invested:
\[
Y=F(K,AL),\qquad Y=C+I,\qquad \dot K=I-\delta K.
\]
Technology is labor-augmenting (Harrod-neutral), freely and publicly available.
$F$ is continuous, twice differentiable for positive inputs, positive there,
with positive and diminishing marginal products of capital and effective
labor. Constant returns means
$F(\lambda K,\lambda AL)=\lambda F(K,AL)$ for $\lambda>0$.
With $k=K/(AL)$ and $f(k)=F(k,1)$, assume
\[
\begin{gathered}
f(0)=0,\qquad f'(k)>0,\qquad f''(k)<0\quad(k>0),\\
\lim_{k\downarrow0}f'(k)=\infty,\qquad
\lim_{k\to\infty}f'(k)=0.
\end{gathered}
\]
These are the explicit Solow production restrictions inherited here.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{02}{Given stocks and exogenous growth}
Initial $K(0),A(0),L(0)$ are positive and given. Technology and population follow
\[
A(t)=A(0)e^{gt},\qquad L(t)=L(0)e^{nt}.
\]
The population of each dynasty grows at the same rate $n$, so fixed dynasty
shares aggregate to total population growth $n$. Each member supplies one unit
of labor. Growth rates $n,g$ and depreciation $\delta$ are exogenous.
The course benchmark sets $\delta=0$; general formulas keep $\delta$ visible.
The original notation $a$ for technology growth is written consistently as $g$.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{03}{Households, ownership, and income}
There are many identical, price-taking, infinitely lived dynasties. Households
own capital and firms, supply labor inelastically, and choose consumption and
saving over time. Household income consists of wages, capital income, and its
share of firm profits. In aggregate, before imposing zero profits,
\[
\dot B=rB+wL+\Pi-C,\qquad r=q-\delta.
\]
Here $B$ is household financial wealth, $q$ the rental price before depreciation,
$w$ the wage per worker, and $\Pi$ distributed economic profit. Under the
competitive constant-returns benchmark below, $\Pi=0$ and $B=K$ in equilibrium.
Ownership remains an assumption even when profits vanish.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{04}{Preferences and the household objective}
Each dynasty maximizes the discounted sum of its members' utility, subject to
its budget and solvency constraints. With consumption per member $x=C/L$,
\[
\max_{\{C(t)\}}\int_0^\infty e^{-\rho t}L(t)u(x(t))\,dt,
\qquad u'(x)=x^{-\theta},\quad\theta>0.
\]
Using aggregate $L$ here changes the objective only by a positive constant
relative to a dynasty of fixed population share. Patience is measured by
$\rho$; in the separate discrete-time formulation the discount factor is
$\beta=1/(1+\rho)$, with $0<\beta<1$. Continuous-time discounting uses
$e^{-\rho t}$. The parameter restrictions used below are
\[
\rho>n\geq0,\qquad g\geq0,\qquad
\eta\equiv\rho-n-(1-\theta)g>0.
\]
Saving is an optimal choice rather than Solow's imposed share of income.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{05}{Firms and competitive factor markets}
There are many firms with a common production technology. They take prices
as given, hire labor and rent capital in competitive markets, and maximize
profits at each date:
\[
\max_{K,L}\;F(K,AL)-qK-wL.
\]
Common technology and constant returns permit the representative-firm
formulation. Homogeneous technology is not itself the definition of perfect
competition. Interior factor demands satisfy
\[
q=F_1(K,AL)=f'(k),\qquad
w=AF_2(K,AL)=A[f(k)-kf'(k)].
\]
Factor payments exhaust output, $Y=qK+wL$, and profits are zero. Households
receive the factor payments and would receive any profits through ownership.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{06}{Market clearing and equilibrium}
All supplied labor and capital are employed. With bars denoting aggregate
endowments,
\[
L^d=L^s=\bar L,\qquad K^d=K^s=\bar K,\qquad
Y=C+I,\qquad B=K.
\]
Household optimality, firm optimality, and these clearing conditions hold
jointly. There are no taxes, externalities, market power, income uncertainty,
or borrowing frictions beyond solvency in the baseline. These conditions
underlie the planner--competitive allocation equivalence used below.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{07}{Feasibility and terminal restrictions}
Capital is inherited and consumption is chosen. Feasible paths obey the
resource constraint with $K\geq0$ and $C>0$. Household debt must satisfy a
no-Ponzi restriction; optimal accumulation must also satisfy a transversality
condition. They serve different purposes and are written explicitly in the
solvency subsection below. An Euler equation alone does not select the path,
and refinancing debt is not by itself a Ponzi scheme.
\end{modelcondition}
\end{modelsetup}

\subsubsection{Notation and the course simplifications}

Use $K,C,Y$ for aggregates and $x=C/L$ for consumption per person. Reserve
$k=K/(AL)$ and $c=C/(AL)$ for quantities per effective worker, so $x=Ac$.
Technology growth is denoted by $g$ throughout. We retain the course's
zero-depreciation benchmark; $\delta$ remains visible in the formulas so that
setting $\delta=0$ is explicit. Full depreciation, $\delta=1$, will be used
only in a separately labeled discrete-time example.

Preferences have marginal utility $u'(x)=x^{-\theta}$, $\theta>0$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ramsey-utility}
u(x)=\begin{cases}
 x^{1-\theta}/(1-\theta),&\theta\ne1,\\
 \log x,&\theta=1.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
In this deterministic model, $1/\theta$ measures willingness to substitute
consumption across time. Higher $\rho$ reduces the weight on future utility.
Assume $\rho>n\geq0$, $g\geq0$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ramsey-effective-discount}
\eta\equiv\rho-n-(1-\theta)g>0.
\end{equation}
These are sufficient restrictions for finite utility on the balanced growth
path used below. Weighting by $L(t)$ means the dynasty values the utility of
all its members; choosing a different population welfare criterion would change
the normalization. The original lists are retained in comments immediately
above this section for comparison.

\subsubsection{No-Ponzi restrictions and transversality}

\begin{blueblock}
\textbf{Solvency and optimality are different restrictions.}
A household's total wealth $B$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
\dot B=rB+wL-C,
\end{equation}
where $r$ is the net real return and $w$ the wage per person. A no-Ponzi
restriction requires
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ramsey-npg}
\liminf_{T\to\infty}e^{-\int_0^T r(v)\,dv}B(T)\geq0.
\end{equation}
Borrowing must ultimately be supported by resources rather than by a negative
terminal present value of wealth. Refinancing a loan is not, by itself, a
Ponzi scheme: future income can support repayment.

A transversality condition instead concerns optimality. In the planner's
normalized problem below it takes the standard form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ramsey-tvc}
\lim_{t\to\infty}e^{-\eta t}u'(c(t))k(t)=0.
\end{equation}
It rules out leaving resources with positive limiting discounted utility value
when consuming more would improve the allocation. It does not require the
capital stock itself to approach zero. Feasibility and an Euler equation alone
need not select the optimal infinite-horizon path.
\end{blueblock}

\subsection{Solving the consumption--saving problem}

\subsubsection{The planner's problem and decentralization}\label{central-planners-problem}

For continuous time, the planner chooses consumption with inherited $K(0)$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ramsey-planner}
\begin{split}
\max_{\{C(t)\}}\;&\int_0^\infty e^{-\rho t}L(t)u(C(t)/L(t))\,dt,\\
\text{s.t. }&\dot K=F(K,AL)-\delta K-C,\qquad K\geq0,\ C>0.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
Consumption uses current output; the residual, net of depreciation, changes
the capital stock. In a decentralized economy, a household uses its budget
constraint instead. Competitive factor pricing gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ramsey-factor-prices}
q=f'(k),\qquad r=q-\delta,\qquad
w=A\left[f(k)-kf'(k)\right].
\end{equation}
$q$ is the rental payment before depreciation. The net return $r=q-\delta$
and a discrete-time gross return $R=1+q-\delta=1+r$ are different objects.

With constant returns, factor incomes exhaust output. Firms' dividends are
not an additional source of aggregate goods on top of $Y$. In equilibrium,
aggregate household wealth equals capital, $B=K$, so the household budgets
consolidate into \eqref{eq:ramsey-planner}'s resource constraint. With identical
preferences, competitive prices, and no taxes, externalities, or financing
frictions, the planner allocation can be supported as a competitive allocation.
This equivalence must be checked again when the environment changes.

\begin{redblock}
\textbf{Question: why do three methods give the same two-period Euler equation?}
Before solving for a whole path, isolate two dates with $A=L=1$, known incomes
$y_0,y_1$, and a given gross return $R=1+r>0$. There are no borrowing frictions
or terminal bequests. With zero initial and terminal assets, the budgets give
\begin{align*}
c_0+a_1&=y_0,\qquad c_1=y_1+Ra_1,\\
a_1&=y_0-c_0,\\
c_1&=y_1+R(y_0-c_0),\\
c_0+c_1/R&=y_0+y_1/R\equiv W.
\end{align*}
For an interior choice of positive consumption, solve
\[
\max_{c_0,c_1}\;u(c_0)+\beta u(c_1),\qquad
c_0+\frac{c_1}{R}=y_0+\frac{y_1}{R}\equiv W.
\]
\end{redblock}

\textit{Elimination.} Substitute $c_1=R(W-c_0)$ into utility and differentiate:
\begin{align*}
\frac{d}{dc_0}[u(c_0)+\beta u(R(W-c_0))]
 &=u'(c_0)+\beta u'(c_1)(-R)=0,\\
u'(c_0)&=\beta R u'(c_1).
\end{align*}
\textit{Geometry.} Along an indifference curve,
$u'(c_0)dc_0+\beta u'(c_1)dc_1=0$, so its slope is
$-u'(c_0)/[\beta u'(c_1)]$. The budget line has slope $-R$. Tangency therefore
requires $u'(c_0)=\beta R u'(c_1)$.

\Needspace{12\baselineskip}
\textit{Lagrangian.} Write
\[
\mathscr L=u(c_0)+\beta u(c_1)
 +\lambda\left(W-c_0-\frac{c_1}{R}\right).
\]
Differentiate each choice:
\begin{align*}
\mathscr L_{c_0}&=u'(c_0)-\lambda=0,\\
\mathscr L_{c_1}&=\beta u'(c_1)-\lambda/R=0,\\
\mathscr L_\lambda&=W-c_0-c_1/R=0,\\
u'(c_0)&=\lambda=\beta R u'(c_1).
\end{align*}

All three methods price the same marginal transfer: giving up one unit today
buys $R$ units tomorrow. The Euler condition fixes the preferred trade-off;
the budget fixes the affordable consumption levels. In infinite time the
adjacent-date logic survives, but the full budget and terminal conditions
still have to be imposed. A binding borrowing constraint would replace the
interior tangency by the appropriate constrained condition.


\subsubsection{Lagrangian: the adjacent-date trade-off}\label{method-of-lagrangian}

\textbf{From two dates to a finite horizon.}
First keep the household exercise separate from production. Dates are
$t=1,\ldots,T$, with $T\geq2$, known incomes $y_t$, a constant gross return
$R>0$, $0<\beta<1$, and no borrowing limit. Let $a_t$ be assets after date
$t$ consumption. Set $a_0=a_T=0$, assume an interior positive-consumption
solution, and write
\begin{align}
\max\;&\sum_{t=1}^T\beta^{t-1}u(c_t),\\
c_1+a_1&=Ra_0+y_1=y_1,\\
c_2+a_2&=Ra_1+y_2,\\
&\ \vdots\nonumber\\
c_T+a_T&=Ra_{T-1}+y_T.
\end{align}
Divide each date-$t$ budget by $R^{t-1}$ and sum. The asset terms telescope:
\begin{align}
\sum_{t=1}^T\frac{c_t}{R^{t-1}}
&=\sum_{t=1}^T\frac{y_t}{R^{t-1}}
 +\sum_{t=1}^T\left(\frac{a_{t-1}}{R^{t-2}}
                         -\frac{a_t}{R^{t-1}}\right),\\
&=\sum_{t=1}^T\frac{y_t}{R^{t-1}}+Ra_0-\frac{a_T}{R^{T-1}},\\
&=\sum_{t=1}^T\frac{y_t}{R^{t-1}}\equiv W_T.
\end{align}
The single present-value constraint gives the Lagrangian
\[
\mathscr L_T=\sum_{t=1}^T\beta^{t-1}u(c_t)
 +\Lambda\left(W_T-\sum_{t=1}^T\frac{c_t}{R^{t-1}}\right).
\]
Differentiate separately at adjacent dates:
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial\mathscr L_T}{\partial c_t}
 &=\beta^{t-1}u'(c_t)-\frac{\Lambda}{R^{t-1}}=0,\\
\frac{\partial\mathscr L_T}{\partial c_{t+1}}
 &=\beta^tu'(c_{t+1})-\frac{\Lambda}{R^t}=0,\\
\Lambda&=(\beta R)^{t-1}u'(c_t)
       =(\beta R)^tu'(c_{t+1}),\\
u'(c_t)&=\beta R u'(c_{t+1}),\qquad t=1,\ldots,T-1.
\end{align}
The last Euler comparison is between $T-1$ and $T$; there is no consumption
date $T+1$ in this problem.

We can obtain the same condition without first collapsing the budgets:
\[
\mathscr L_T=\sum_{t=1}^T\beta^{t-1}
 \{u(c_t)+\lambda_t[Ra_{t-1}+y_t-c_t-a_t]\}.
\]
For $t<T$, $a_t$ enters two adjacent budgets. Its derivative shows both terms:
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial\mathscr L_T}{\partial c_t}
 &=\beta^{t-1}[u'(c_t)-\lambda_t]=0,\\
\frac{\partial\mathscr L_T}{\partial a_t}
 &=-\beta^{t-1}\lambda_t+\beta^tR\lambda_{t+1}=0,\\
\lambda_t&=u'(c_t),\qquad
\lambda_t=\beta R\lambda_{t+1},\\
u'(c_t)&=\beta R u'(c_{t+1}).
\end{align}
The finite endpoint $a_T=0$ is given here, not another interior asset choice.
In an infinite horizon, adjacent-date conditions remain, but the endpoint
must be replaced by the appropriate solvency and transversality restrictions.
Taking $T\to\infty$ does not mean requiring the economy's physical capital
to approach zero.

\textbf{The infinite-horizon production economy.}
First isolate discrete time with $A=L=1$ and $n=g=0$. This makes timing visible
without carrying growing denominators; it is a restricted version of the model.
The planner solves
\begin{equation}
\max_{\{c_t,k_{t+1}\}}\sum_{t=0}^\infty\beta^tu(c_t),\qquad
c_t+k_{t+1}=f(k_t)+(1-\delta)k_t,
\end{equation}
where $k_0$ is given, $0<\beta<1$, and $0\leq\delta\leq1$. Writing
$\beta=1/(1+\rho)$ defines a discrete-time rate of time preference; the
continuous-time convention uses $e^{-\rho t}$.

Use current-utility multipliers $\lambda_t$:
\begin{equation}
\mathscr L=\sum_{t=0}^\infty\beta^t
\left\{u(c_t)+\lambda_t[f(k_t)+(1-\delta)k_t-c_t-k_{t+1}]\right\}.
\end{equation}
At an interior solution, differentiate with respect to $c_t$ and $k_{t+1}$:
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial\mathscr L}{\partial c_t}
 &=\beta^t[u'(c_t)-\lambda_t]=0,\\
\frac{\partial\mathscr L}{\partial k_{t+1}}
 &=-\beta^t\lambda_t
   +\beta^{t+1}\lambda_{t+1}[f'(k_{t+1})+1-\delta]=0,\\
\lambda_t&=u'(c_t),\\
\lambda_t&=\beta\lambda_{t+1}[f'(k_{t+1})+1-\delta].
\end{align}
Substitution gives the Euler equation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ramsey-discrete-euler}
u'(c_t)=\beta R_{t+1}u'(c_{t+1}),\qquad
R_{t+1}=f'(k_{t+1})+1-\delta.
\end{equation}
One unit saved at $t$ earns its return at $t+1$, when the new capital is used.
The utility cost today equals the discounted marginal gain tomorrow. For CRRA,
\begin{align}
c_t^{-\theta}&=\beta R_{t+1}c_{t+1}^{-\theta},\\
\frac{c_t^{-\theta}}{c_{t+1}^{-\theta}}&=\beta R_{t+1},\\
\left(\frac{c_{t+1}}{c_t}\right)^\theta&=\beta R_{t+1},\\
\frac{c_{t+1}}{c_t}&=(\beta R_{t+1})^{1/\theta}.
\end{align}
This determines consumption growth given the return, not the entire consumption
path. With no bequest motive and the usual concavity conditions, the terminal
condition is $\lim_{t\to\infty}\beta^tu'(c_t)k_{t+1}=0$. In a finite-horizon
exercise a terminal stock may be set to zero; that is not the same as imposing
$k_t\to0$ in this infinite-horizon economy.

\subsubsection{Hamiltonian: saving with continuous adjustment}\label{method-of-hamiltonian-equation-optimal-control}

Return to continuous time with population and technology growth. From
$k=K/(AL)$ and $c=C/(AL)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ramsey-capital-motion}
\dot k=f(k)-c-bk,\qquad b\equiv n+g+\delta.
\end{equation}
For $\theta\ne1$, substitute $x=Ac$ and $L=L_0e^{nt}$ into utility:
\begin{align}
e^{-\rho t}Lu(Ac)
 &=e^{-\rho t}L_0e^{nt}\frac{(A_0e^{gt}c)^{1-\theta}}{1-\theta},\\
 &=L_0A_0^{1-\theta}e^{[-\rho+n+(1-\theta)g]t}
       \frac{c^{1-\theta}}{1-\theta},\\
 &=L_0A_0^{1-\theta}e^{-\eta t}\frac{c^{1-\theta}}{1-\theta},\\
\eta&=\rho-n-(1-\theta)g.
\end{align}
The positive constant in front does not change choices. For log utility,
$\log(Ac)=\log A+\log c$; the $\log A$ term is independent of the allocation.
The normalized problem therefore discounts $u(c)$ at $\eta$ in either case.

\textbf{Where the costate equation comes from.}
Write $h(k,c)=f(k)-c-bk$. To derive interior conditions, first use a finite
interval $[0,T]$ with both capital endpoints held fixed. Let $p(t)$ be a
present-value multiplier. The augmented objective is
\begin{align}
\mathscr A
 &=\int_0^T\{e^{-\eta t}u(c)+p[h(k,c)-\dot k]\}\,dt,\\
 &=\int_0^T\mathcal H^P\,dt-\int_0^Tp\dot k\,dt,\\
\mathcal H^P&=e^{-\eta t}u(c)+ph(k,c).
\end{align}
Integration by parts keeps the boundary term visible:
\begin{align}
\int_0^Tp\dot k\,dt
 &=[pk]_0^T-\int_0^T\dot p\,k\,dt,\\
\mathscr A
 &=\int_0^T[\mathcal H^P+\dot p\,k]\,dt-[pk]_0^T.
\end{align}
Let $v_c(t)$ and $v_k(t)$ be small variations, with
$v_k(0)=v_k(T)=0$. The boundary variation vanishes, leaving
\[
\delta\mathscr A=\int_0^T
 [\mathcal H^P_c v_c+(\mathcal H^P_k+\dot p)v_k]\,dt.
\]
For arbitrary interior variations, the two coefficients must vanish.
Variation of $p$ also imposes the original resource equation:
\begin{align}
\mathcal H^P_c&=e^{-\eta t}u'(c)-p=0,\\
\mathcal H^P_k+\dot p&=p[f'(k)-b]+\dot p=0,\\
\mathcal H^P_p&=h(k,c)=\dot k.
\end{align}
Thus $p=e^{-\eta t}u'(c)$ and $\dot p=-p[f'(k)-b]$.
Holding endpoints fixed was used only to derive the interior conditions.
The infinite-horizon optimum still needs its own feasibility and
transversality condition; a boundary term must not be discarded by asserting
that the capital stock tends to zero.

Now convert units from present utility to date-$t$ utility. Define
$\mu=e^{\eta t}p$ and multiply the Hamiltonian by $e^{\eta t}$:
\begin{align}
e^{\eta t}\mathcal H^P
 &=u(c)+e^{\eta t}p\,h(k,c)
  =u(c)+\mu h(k,c)\equiv\mathcal H,\\
\dot\mu
 &=\eta e^{\eta t}p+e^{\eta t}\dot p,\\
 &=\eta\mu-e^{\eta t}p[f'(k)-b],\\
 &=[\eta+b-f'(k)]\mu.
\end{align}
With zero discounting on this finite interval, $\eta=0$ makes $p=\mu$ and
$\dot\mu=-\mathcal H_k$. With discounting, the additional $\eta\mu$ term
comes from differentiating $e^{\eta t}$; it is not an optional convention.

The current-value Hamiltonian is therefore
\begin{equation}
\mathcal H=u(c)+\mu[f(k)-c-bk].
\end{equation}
The control condition and current-value costate equation are
\begin{align}
\mathcal H_c=0&\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mu=u'(c),\\
\dot\mu=\eta\mu-\mathcal H_k
&=\left[\eta+b-f'(k)\right]\mu.
\end{align}
$\mu$ is the current utility value of an additional unit of normalized capital.
The factor $\eta$ belongs in the current-value costate equation; a
present-value costate would instead absorb the discount factor. These are two
representations of the same problem, not an assertion that patience is irrelevant.

Differentiate $\mu=c^{-\theta}$ and use
$\eta+b=\rho+\delta+\theta g$:
\begin{align}
\dot\mu&=-\theta c^{-\theta-1}\dot c,\\
\frac{\dot\mu}{\mu}
 &=-\theta\frac{c^{-\theta-1}}{c^{-\theta}}\dot c
  =-\theta\frac{\dot c}{c},\\
\frac{\dot\mu}{\mu}&=-\theta\frac{\dot c}{c}
 =\eta+b-f'(k),\\
\theta\frac{\dot c}{c}&=f'(k)-\eta-b,\\
\eta+b&=\rho-n-(1-\theta)g+n+g+\delta
        =\rho+\theta g+\delta,\\
\frac{\dot c}{c}
&=\frac{f'(k)-\delta-\rho-\theta g}{\theta}.
\label{eq:ramsey-normalized-euler}
\end{align}
Consumption per person is $x=Ac$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ramsey-person-euler}
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\dot x}{x}
 &=\frac{\dot A}{A}+\frac{\dot c}{c}\\
 &=g+\frac{f'(k)-\delta-\rho-\theta g}{\theta}\\
 &=\frac{\theta g+f'(k)-\delta-\rho-\theta g}{\theta}
  =\frac{r-\rho}{\theta}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
A high return makes future consumption relatively attractive; impatience favors
consumption now. Curvature determines how strongly consumption growth responds.
Population growth affects resources and welfare weights, but cancels from this
per-person Euler equation under the dynasty objective specified above.

Setting $\delta=0$ recovers the course benchmark. Setting $g=n=0$ also removes
the growth normalization. At $\rho=0$ the intertemporal trade-off changes, and
an infinite-horizon undiscounted problem needs its own welfare criterion; it is
not obtained simply by deleting discounting from every infinite integral.

\subsubsection{Bellman and HJB: valuing the remaining opportunities}\label{method-of-bellman-equation-dynamic-programming}

For the stationary discrete-time version, let $V(k)$ be the value of starting
with capital $k$. Write $k_0=k$ and
$c_t=f(k_t)+(1-\delta)k_t-k_{t+1}>0$, with feasible $k_{t+1}\geq0$.
Splitting off the first date gives
\begin{align}
V(k)
 &=\max_{\{k_{t+1}\}}\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\beta^tu(c_t),\\
 &=\max_{\{k_{t+1}\}}
    \left\{u(c_0)+\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\beta^tu(c_t)\right\},\\
 &=\max_{k_1}\left\{u(c_0)+\beta
       \max_{\{k_{t+1}\}_{t\geq1}}
        \sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\beta^ju(c_{j+1})\right\},\\
 &=\max_{k_1}\{u(f(k)+(1-\delta)k-k_1)+\beta V(k_1)\}.
\end{align}
The continuation problem has the same technology and preferences, starting
from $k_1$. Write $k'=k_1$. Its first-order condition is
\begin{align}
0&=u'(f(k)+(1-\delta)k-k')(-1)+\beta V'(k'),\\
u'(c)&=\beta V'(k').
\end{align}

\textbf{Show the policy derivative before canceling it.}
Let the optimal next-capital policy be $k'=\psi(k)$, assumed differentiable
at the interior point being considered. On that policy,
\[
V(k)=u(f(k)+(1-\delta)k-\psi(k))+\beta V(\psi(k)).
\]
Differentiate the entire expression, including the response of the choice:
\begin{align}
V'(k)
 &=u'(c)[f'(k)+1-\delta-\psi'(k)]
      +\beta V'(\psi(k))\psi'(k),\\
 &=u'(c)[f'(k)+1-\delta]
   +[\beta V'(\psi(k))-u'(c)]\psi'(k),\\
 &=u'(c)[f'(k)+1-\delta]+0\cdot\psi'(k),\\
 &=u'(c)[f'(k)+1-\delta].
\end{align}
The bracket is zero by the first-order condition. This cancellation is the
envelope theorem in this calculation; it is not an instruction to ignore
$\psi'(k)$ before differentiating.

Now write the two conditions at their actual dates:
\begin{align}
u'(c_t)&=\beta V'(k_{t+1}),\\
V'(k_{t+1})&=u'(c_{t+1})[f'(k_{t+1})+1-\delta],\\
u'(c_t)&=\beta u'(c_{t+1})[f'(k_{t+1})+1-\delta],\\
\frac{u'(c_t)}{u'(c_{t+1})}
 &=\beta[f'(k_{t+1})+1-\delta]=\beta R_{t+1}.
\end{align}
This recovers \eqref{eq:ramsey-discrete-euler}. The Lagrangian and Bellman
methods price the same marginal transfer of resources between dates.

\textbf{A case with an exact saving rule.}
Take $u(c)=\log c$, $f(k)=k^\alpha$, $0<\alpha<1$, and full depreciation
$\delta=1$ only for this example. Guess $V(k)=a+d\log k$. Substitute the
guess before differentiating:
\begin{align}
a+d\log k
 &=\max_{k'}\{\log(k^\alpha-k')+\beta[a+d\log k']\},\\
0&=-\frac{1}{k^\alpha-k'}+\frac{\beta d}{k'},\\
k'&=\beta d(k^\alpha-k'),\\
(1+\beta d)k'&=\beta d k^\alpha,\\
k'&=s k^\alpha,\qquad
s\equiv\frac{\beta d}{1+\beta d},\qquad c=(1-s)k^\alpha.
\end{align}
Insert that policy into the Bellman equation and expand both logarithms:
\begin{align}
a+d\log k
 &=\log[(1-s)k^\alpha]+\beta[a+d\log(sk^\alpha)],\\
 &=\log(1-s)+\alpha\log k+\beta a
    +\beta d\log s+\alpha\beta d\log k,\\
 &=\log(1-s)+\beta a+\beta d\log s
    +\alpha(1+\beta d)\log k.
\end{align}
Equality for every $k>0$ requires equal coefficients of $\log k$:
\begin{align}
d&=\alpha(1+\beta d),\\
d-\alpha\beta d&=\alpha,\\
d&=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha\beta},\\
s&=\frac{\beta\alpha/(1-\alpha\beta)}
        {1+\beta\alpha/(1-\alpha\beta)}=\alpha\beta.
\end{align}
The constant term also has to match:
\begin{align}
a&=\log(1-s)+\beta a+\beta d\log s,\\
a&=\frac{\log(1-\alpha\beta)
     +\dfrac{\alpha\beta}{1-\alpha\beta}\log(\alpha\beta)}{1-\beta}.
\end{align}
Thus the complete policy is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ramsey-exact-saving}
d=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha\beta},\qquad
k'=\alpha\beta k^\alpha,\qquad c=(1-\alpha\beta)k^\alpha.
\end{equation}
A constant saving share can emerge from optimization in this special case.
Its value depends on preferences and technology; the result does not make
a constant saving share general once depreciation or utility changes.

\textbf{Build the continuous-time HJB from a short interval.}
In effective-worker units let $h(k,c)=f(k)-c-bk$. Over a short interval
$\Delta t$, hold the chosen control fixed to first order. Then
\begin{align}
k(t+\Delta t)&=k+h(k,c)\Delta t+o(\Delta t),\\
e^{-\eta\Delta t}&=1-\eta\Delta t+o(\Delta t),\\
V(k(t+\Delta t))&=V(k)+V'(k)h(k,c)\Delta t+o(\Delta t).
\end{align}
The dynamic-programming identity over this interval becomes
\begin{align}
V(k)&=\max_c\{u(c)\Delta t
              +e^{-\eta\Delta t}V(k(t+\Delta t))+o(\Delta t)\},\\
&=\max_c\{V(k)+[u(c)+V'(k)h(k,c)-\eta V(k)]\Delta t
                    +o(\Delta t)\}.
\end{align}
Subtract $V(k)$, divide by $\Delta t$, and take the limit:
\begin{align}
0&=\max_c\{u(c)+V'(k)[f(k)-c-bk]-\eta V(k)\},\\
\eta V(k)&=\max_c\{u(c)+V'(k)[f(k)-c-bk]\}.
\end{align}
The control derivative is $u'(c)-V'(k)=0$. To obtain the costate equation,
write the optimal control as $c=\gamma(k)$ and differentiate the HJB:
\begin{align}
\eta V'(k)
 &=u'(c)\gamma'(k)+V''(k)h(k,c)
    +V'(k)[f'(k)-b-\gamma'(k)],\\
 &=V''(k)h(k,c)+V'(k)[f'(k)-b]
    +[u'(c)-V'(k)]\gamma'(k),\\
 &=V''(k)\dot k+V'(k)[f'(k)-b].
\end{align}
Finally, with $\mu=V'(k)$, the chain rule along the path gives
\begin{align}
\dot\mu&=V''(k)\dot k,\\
\dot\mu&=[\eta+b-f'(k)]\mu,\\
\mu=c^{-\theta}
&\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
-\theta\frac{\dot c}{c}=\eta+b-f'(k).
\end{align}
The value function therefore yields the same costate and consumption
conditions as the Hamiltonian. Feasibility and terminal restrictions remain
part of the underlying problem.

\subsection{Intertemporal equilibrium and economic adjustment}

\subsubsection{Steady state and the two loci}

The continuous-time equilibrium combines
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ramsey-system}
\begin{split}
\dot k&=f(k)-c-bk,\\
\dot c&=\frac{c}{\theta}[f'(k)-\delta-\rho-\theta g],
\end{split}
\end{equation}
with given $k(0)$ and \eqref{eq:ramsey-tvc}. A positive normalized steady state
satisfies
\begin{align}
0&=\frac{c^*}{\theta}[f'(k^*)-\delta-\rho-\theta g],\\
0&=f'(k^*)-\delta-\rho-\theta g\qquad(c^*>0),\\
f'(k^*)&=\delta+\rho+\theta g,\\
0&=f(k^*)-c^*-bk^*,\\
c^*&=f(k^*)-bk^*.
\end{align}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ramsey-steady}
f'(k^*)=\delta+\rho+\theta g,\qquad c^*=f(k^*)-bk^*.
\end{equation}
The production assumptions give a unique positive $k^*$. Strict concavity gives
$f(k^*)/k^*>f'(k^*)>b$, where the second inequality is $\eta>0$, so $c^*>0$.
On this balanced growth path, consumption per person grows at $g$, aggregate
consumption at $n+g$, and the net return is $r^*=\rho+\theta g$.

For $c>0$, the $\dot c=0$ locus is vertical at $k^*$. Consumption grows to its
left and falls to its right. The $\dot k=0$ locus is $c=f(k)-bk$; capital grows
below it and falls above it. If $b>0$, its maximum is the Golden Rule stock,
where $f'(k_{\mathrm{GR}})=b$. Neither locus, by itself, is an equilibrium path.
The shape of the resource locus follows by differentiation:
\[
\frac{dc}{dk}=f'(k)-b,\qquad
\frac{d^2c}{dk^2}=f''(k)<0.
\]
Its slope is positive below the Golden Rule stock and negative above it.
The $\dot c=0$ locus fixes $k=k^*$ for every $c>0$, which is why it is
vertical; no division by a zero derivative is needed.

\subsubsection{Why consumption can jump while capital cannot}

\begin{blueblock}
\textbf{A state is inherited; a control is chosen.}
Capital is a state inherited from previous investment. Consumption is a control:
a household can change spending immediately, but cannot instantly create a
new stock of machines. For a given $k(0)$, different initial consumption choices
produce different candidate paths under \eqref{eq:ramsey-system}. Most fail
feasibility or the terminal condition. The saddle path selects initial
consumption as well as the later transition; it does not require every variable
to start at its steady state.
\end{blueblock}

A one-time destruction of physical capital, with all other fundamentals
unchanged, moves the state leftward. Consumption adjusts onto the stable path.
The low stock supports a high return and rebuilding, while the loss of resources
constrains current spending. Actual disasters may also damage skills or
technology, which would change the destination rather than just the initial state.

A credible announcement about future productivity or taxation can change
consumption before it changes current output: the household evaluates future
income and returns when choosing today's spending. This model isolates that
intertemporal mechanism. It omits borrowing constraints, income risk, and
different marginal propensities to consume, so its representative consumption
response is not an empirical claim about every household.

\subsection{Linearization and the saddle path}

\subsubsection{The local system and its eigenvalues}

Let $\Delta k=k-k^*$ and $\Delta c=c-c^*$. In the order $(k,c)$, the first-order
system follows by differentiating both right-hand sides. Write
$P=f(k)-c-bk$ and $Q=c[f'(k)-\delta-\rho-\theta g]/\theta$:
\begin{align}
P_k^*&=f'(k^*)-b
 =(\delta+\rho+\theta g)-(n+g+\delta)=\eta,\\
P_c^*&=-1,\\
Q_k^*&=\frac{c^*}{\theta}f''(k^*)\equiv z<0,\\
Q_c^*&=\frac{f'(k^*)-\delta-\rho-\theta g}{\theta}=0,\\
\dot{\Delta k}&\approx P_k^*\Delta k+P_c^*\Delta c
                 =\eta\Delta k-\Delta c,\\
\dot{\Delta c}&\approx Q_k^*\Delta k+Q_c^*\Delta c=z\Delta k.
\end{align}
The constant terms vanish because $P(k^*,c^*)=Q(k^*,c^*)=0$.
In matrix form, the system is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ramsey-jacobian}
\begin{pmatrix}\dot{\Delta k}\\\dot{\Delta c}\end{pmatrix}
\approx
J\begin{pmatrix}\Delta k\\\Delta c\end{pmatrix},\qquad
J=\begin{pmatrix}\eta&-1\\ z&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
z\equiv\frac{c^*f''(k^*)}{\theta}<0.
\end{equation}
The top-left entry is $f'(k^*)-b=\eta$; the bottom-right entry vanishes because
the consumption growth bracket is zero at the steady state. Thus
\begin{align}
\det(\lambda I-J)
 &=\det\begin{pmatrix}\lambda-\eta&1\\-z&\lambda\end{pmatrix},\\
 &=(\lambda-\eta)\lambda-(1)(-z),\\
 &=\lambda^2-\eta\lambda+z=0,\\
\lambda_{\pm}&=\frac{\eta\pm\sqrt{(-\eta)^2-4z}}{2}
              =\frac{\eta\pm\sqrt{\eta^2-4z}}{2}.
\end{align}
Since $z<0$, $\lambda_-<0<\lambda_+$. The local system has one stable and one
unstable direction. This is the mathematical reason a boundary condition is
needed to select consumption for the inherited capital stock.

For a stable eigenvector $(1,m)$, multiply the matrix explicitly:
\begin{align}
J\begin{pmatrix}1\\m\end{pmatrix}
 &=\begin{pmatrix}\eta-m\\z\end{pmatrix}
  =\lambda_-\begin{pmatrix}1\\m\end{pmatrix},\\
\eta-m&=\lambda_-,\qquad z=\lambda_-m,\\
m&=\eta-\lambda_->0.
\end{align}
In the linearized system, the two modes have
$m_\pm=\eta-\lambda_\pm$ and constants $A_-,A_+$:
\begin{align}
\Delta k(t)&=A_-e^{\lambda_-t}+A_+e^{\lambda_+t},\\
\Delta c(t)&=m_-A_-e^{\lambda_-t}+m_+A_+e^{\lambda_+t}.
\end{align}
Convergence to this steady state requires $A_+=0$, because $\lambda_+>0$.
Then $A_-=\Delta k(0)$ and $\Delta c(0)=m_-\Delta k(0)$.
On the stable direction, separate the scalar differential equation:
\begin{align}
\dot{\Delta k}&=\lambda_-\Delta k,\\
\int_{\Delta k(0)}^{\Delta k(t)}\frac{dv}{v}
 &=\int_0^t\lambda_-\,ds,\\
\log\left|\frac{\Delta k(t)}{\Delta k(0)}\right|&=\lambda_-t,\\
\Delta k(t)&=\Delta k(0)e^{\lambda_-t}.
\end{align}
The integration assumes $\Delta k(0)\ne0$; if it is zero, the stable
linearized path remains at zero. Translating back to the nonlinear model,
the local stable path is therefore
\begin{align}
\Delta c(0)&\approx m\Delta k(0),\\
\Delta k(t)&\approx\Delta k(0)e^{\lambda_-t},\\
\Delta c(t)&\approx m\Delta k(0)e^{\lambda_-t}.
\end{align}
To halve the absolute deviation, solve
\begin{align}
e^{\lambda_-T_{1/2}}&=\tfrac12,\\
\lambda_-T_{1/2}&=-\log2,\\
T_{1/2}&=\frac{\log2}{-\lambda_-}.
\end{align}
The positive convergence speed is $-\lambda_-$. The exponential solution is exact for the linearized
system, and generally approximate for the original nonlinear transition.

\begin{greenblock}
\textbf{Comparing convergence in Solow and Ramsey.}
Both local stable paths have exponential decay in effective-worker capital:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{Solow:}\quad k(t)-k^*&\approx[k(0)-k^*]e^{-\lambda_Ct},\\
\text{Ramsey:}\quad k(t)-k^*&\approx[k(0)-k^*]e^{\lambda_-t}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Here $\lambda_C=b-sf'(k^*)>0$ is defined in
\eqref{eq:solow-continuous-speed}, while $\lambda_-<0$ is Ramsey's stable
eigenvalue. The positive speeds are therefore $\lambda_C$ and $-\lambda_-$,
and a negative stable eigenvalue is not a negative convergence speed. Each steady state belongs to
its own model. In Ramsey, initial consumption must also lie on the stable
arm; arbitrary consumption does not produce this convergent path. These are
local approximations to the nonlinear models.
\end{greenblock}

\subsubsection{The same restriction by undetermined coefficients}

\textbf{Recover the original second-order route.}
In this calculation all equalities refer to the linearized system. Differentiate
its first equation and substitute its second:
\begin{align}
\dot{\Delta k}&=\eta\Delta k-\Delta c,\\
\ddot{\Delta k}&=\eta\dot{\Delta k}-\dot{\Delta c},\\
 &=\eta\dot{\Delta k}-z\Delta k,\\
\ddot{\Delta k}-\eta\dot{\Delta k}+z\Delta k&=0.
\end{align}
Conjecture a nonzero mode $\Delta k(t)=Be^{\lambda t}$. Its derivatives are
$\dot{\Delta k}=\lambda Be^{\lambda t}$ and
$\ddot{\Delta k}=\lambda^2Be^{\lambda t}$. Substitution gives
\begin{align}
\lambda^2Be^{\lambda t}-\eta\lambda Be^{\lambda t}
      +zBe^{\lambda t}&=0,\\
Be^{\lambda t}(\lambda^2-\eta\lambda+z)&=0,\\
\lambda^2-\eta\lambda+z&=0.
\end{align}
The roots are exactly $\lambda_-$ and $\lambda_+$ above. Keeping only the
decaying mode and using $k(0)$ gives
$k(t)=k^*+[k(0)-k^*]e^{\lambda_-t}$ for the linearized transition.

\textbf{The equivalent policy-slope route.}
Alternatively, conjecture a local policy $\Delta c=m\Delta k$. From the first
linearized equation, $\dot{\Delta k}=(\eta-m)\Delta k$. Differentiating the
conjecture and using the second equation gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\dot{\Delta c}&=m\dot{\Delta k}=m(\eta-m)\Delta k=z\Delta k,\\
m(\eta-m)&=z,\\
m\eta-m^2-z&=0,\\
m^2-\eta m+z&=0.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Choose the positive root $m=\lambda_+$, so $\eta-m=\lambda_-<0$. This recovers
the same stable policy. Choosing the decaying direction is not a free change
to initial capital; it is the restriction on the consumption choice implied
by admissibility and optimality.

\subsection{Maximizing welfare}\label{maximizing-welfare}

\subsubsection{The object being maximized is a consumption path}

A higher future consumption level is attractive, but reaching it may require
less consumption today. Welfare makes that trade-off explicit. Compare two
feasible paths starting from the same $k(0)$, with the same technology,
population, preferences, and information. In effective-worker units, the
allocation-dependent objective is
\begin{equation}
W[c]=\int_0^\infty e^{-\eta t}u(c(t))\,dt.
\end{equation}
For CRRA utility this differs from the original dynasty objective only by a
positive constant factor; for log utility there is also an additive term
independent of the allocation. Thus the ordering of feasible paths is the same.

The Euler equation balances utility across adjacent dates. Feasibility limits
how much consumption can be financed, and the transversality condition rules
out leaving valuable resources unused forever. Together, under the concavity
conditions of this chapter, they select the welfare-maximizing path. An Euler
equation by itself does not establish that a proposed path is optimal.

\subsubsection{Golden Rule versus the optimal steady state}

The Golden Rule answers a narrower question: which constant $k$ supports the
highest steady consumption? With $b=n+g+\delta>0$,
\begin{equation}
\bar c(k)=f(k)-bk,\qquad
\bar c'(k)=f'(k)-b,\qquad f'(k_{\mathrm{GR}})=b.
\end{equation}
The Ramsey steady state instead satisfies
\begin{equation}
f'(k^*)=\delta+\rho+\theta g=b+\eta>b,
\qquad k^*<k_{\mathrm{GR}}.
\end{equation}
Diminishing marginal product gives the last inequality. If $b=0$, there is no
finite interior Golden Rule stock under $f'(k)>0$; the Ramsey steady-state
condition can still determine a finite $k^*$.

\begin{greenblock}
\textbf{Higher eventual consumption need not mean higher welfare.}
Why stop before the consumption-maximizing stock? Building extra capital
requires forgone consumption during the transition. The Golden Rule compares
steady states without pricing that sacrifice. Ramsey values the sacrifice
and the later benefit along a common time path. Starting at $k^*$, a policy
that forces more saving may eventually raise consumption, yet reduce welfare.
The later gain alone does not settle the comparison.
\end{greenblock}

This also clarifies the benchmark's policy meaning. With no externalities,
market power, borrowing frictions, or tax wedges, the competitive allocation
implements the planner's optimum. That statement uses identical dynasties and
the specified welfare weights; it does not settle distributional questions
when households differ.

\subsubsection{Measuring a welfare difference in consumption units}

A difference in utility units is hard to interpret. Instead, ask what permanent
proportional change to baseline consumption would make a household indifferent
between the baseline path $c_B$ and an alternative $c_A$. Define $\lambda$ by
\begin{equation}
W[(1+\lambda)c_B]=W[c_A].
\end{equation}
A positive $\lambda$ means the alternative is better. This is a welfare index;
the scaled baseline need not itself be feasible under the original resources.

For $\theta\ne1$, let
$J_i=\int_0^\infty e^{-\eta t}c_i(t)^{1-\theta}\,dt$, for $i=A,B$.
Substitution into CRRA utility gives
\begin{align}
W[(1+\lambda)c_B]
 &=\frac{1}{1-\theta}\int_0^\infty e^{-\eta t}
                 [(1+\lambda)c_B(t)]^{1-\theta}\,dt,\\
 &=\frac{(1+\lambda)^{1-\theta}}{1-\theta}J_B
  =\frac{1}{1-\theta}J_A,\\
(1+\lambda)^{1-\theta}&=J_A/J_B,\\
1+\lambda&=(J_A/J_B)^{1/(1-\theta)},\\
\lambda&=(J_A/J_B)^{1/(1-\theta)}-1.
\end{align}
For $\theta=1$, use $\int_0^\infty e^{-\eta t}dt=1/\eta$ to obtain
\begin{align}
W[(1+\lambda)c_B]
 &=\int_0^\infty e^{-\eta t}[\log(1+\lambda)+\log c_B(t)]\,dt,\\
 &=\frac{\log(1+\lambda)}{\eta}+W[c_B]=W[c_A],\\
\log(1+\lambda)&=\eta\{W[c_A]-W[c_B]\},\\
\lambda&=\exp\!\left(\eta\{W[c_A]-W[c_B]\}\right)-1.
\end{align}
For example, if consumption is 1\% higher at every date, both formulas give
$\lambda=0.01$. A 1\% difference only in eventual steady consumption generally
does not: the transition also enters the integral.

\Needspace{10\baselineskip}
\subsection{Taxation and capital accumulation}\label{sec:ramsey-taxation}

\subsubsection{Separate financial transfers from real resource use}

A tax can change who receives income, how many goods remain for private use,
and the return to saving. These are different mechanisms. To separate them,
introduce a balanced-budget government without debt. Let $T_L$ be aggregate
lump-sum taxes, $\mathcal R$ aggregate transfers, and $G$ government purchases.
A constant rate $0\le\tau<1$ taxes \emph{net} capital income $rK$, where
$r=f'(k)-\delta$. Depreciation is deductible under this convention.

The household budget and government budget are
\begin{align}
\dot B&=(1-\tau)rB+wL-C-T_L+\mathcal R,\\
\tau rK+T_L&=G+\mathcal R.
\end{align}
In equilibrium $B=K$ and $rK+wL=Y-\delta K$. Substitute the government budget
into the household budget:
\begin{align}
\mathcal R&=\tau rK+T_L-G,\\
\dot K&=(1-\tau)rK+wL-C-T_L+(\tau rK+T_L-G),\\
 &=rK-\tau rK+wL-C-T_L+\tau rK+T_L-G,\\
 &=rK+wL-C-G=Y-\delta K-C-G,\\
\dot k&=\frac{\dot K}{AL}-(n+g)k,\\
 &=f(k)-\delta k-c-\mathcal G-(n+g)k.
\end{align}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ramsey-government-resources}
\dot K=Y-\delta K-C-G,
\qquad
\dot k=f(k)-c-bk-\mathcal G,
\quad \mathcal G\equiv\frac{G}{AL}.
\end{equation}
Taxes and transfers cancel when consolidating budgets. Government purchases
use goods and therefore remain. Here $G$ does not enter production or household
utility; public investment or valued public services would require an explicit
additional mechanism.

\subsubsection{Lump-sum taxation: a resource and wealth effect}

Set $\tau=0$. A lump-sum tax does not depend on an individual household's saving,
so it leaves the marginal return unchanged:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dot c}{c}=\frac{f'(k)-\delta-\rho-\theta g}{\theta}.
\end{equation}
This does not mean that financing $G$ has no effect. Private consumption and
investment must share fewer resources, and households adjust spending when
they learn about future taxes and purchases.

For constant $\mathcal G$, an interior steady state has the same $k^*$ as the
untaxed benchmark, but
\begin{equation}
c^*_{G}=f(k^*)-bk^*-\mathcal G>0.
\end{equation}
The unchanged long-run stock follows from the unchanged Euler condition;
consumption absorbs the steady resource cost. The transition can still differ.
If $G=0$ and lump-sum taxes are returned to the same identical dynasties, the
net payment cancels and the benchmark allocation is unchanged.

\subsubsection{Capital income taxation: the private saving wedge}

Now set $T_L=0$ and rebate capital tax revenue as lump-sum transfers, with
$G=0$. There are many identical price-taking dynasties. Each takes the transfer
as given: saving an extra unit does not give that household the entire
increase in aggregate tax rebates. Its marginal return is $(1-\tau)r$.
To obtain the household Euler condition, use consumption per person $x$
and private assets per person $a_h$. The private budget has
$\dot a_h=[(1-\tau)r-n]a_h+w-x+\mathcal R/L$.
The dynasty discounts per-person utility at $\rho-n$. With marginal value
$\nu=u'(x)=x^{-\theta}$, its costate condition gives
\begin{align}
\frac{\dot\nu}{\nu}
 &=(\rho-n)-[(1-\tau)r-n]=\rho-(1-\tau)r,\\
\dot\nu&=-\theta x^{-\theta-1}\dot x,\\
-\theta\frac{\dot x}{x}&=\rho-(1-\tau)r,\\
\frac{\dot x}{x}&=\frac{(1-\tau)r-\rho}{\theta}.
\end{align}
Since $x=Ac$, $\dot c/c=\dot x/x-g$. Substitute $r=f'(k)-\delta$:
\begin{align}
\frac{\dot x}{x}&=\frac{(1-\tau)r-\rho}{\theta},\\
\frac{\dot c}{c}&=
\frac{(1-\tau)[f'(k)-\delta]-\rho-\theta g}{\theta}.
\label{eq:ramsey-tax-euler}
\end{align}
The consolidated resource constraint is still the original one. The tax changes
the private incentive even when its revenue is fully rebated. Treating the
representative household as if it chose aggregate rebates would erase this
wedge and would solve a different problem.

For a permanent constant tax, the interior steady state satisfies
\begin{align}
0&=(1-\tau)[f'(k_\tau)-\delta]-\rho-\theta g,\\
(1-\tau)[f'(k_\tau)-\delta]&=\rho+\theta g,\\
f'(k_\tau)-\delta&=\frac{\rho+\theta g}{1-\tau}.
\end{align}
\begin{equation}
f'(k_\tau)=\delta+\frac{\rho+\theta g}{1-\tau},
\qquad c_\tau=f(k_\tau)-bk_\tau.
\end{equation}
Differentiate the first equation:
\begin{equation}
f''(k_\tau)\frac{dk_\tau}{d\tau}
=\frac{\rho+\theta g}{(1-\tau)^2},
\qquad \frac{dk_\tau}{d\tau}<0.
\end{equation}
Explicitly, division by the negative marginal-product derivative gives
\[
\frac{dk_\tau}{d\tau}
 =\frac{\rho+\theta g}{(1-\tau)^2f''(k_\tau)}<0.
\]
A lower after-tax share requires a higher pre-tax marginal product to support
zero growth of $c$. With diminishing returns, that means less capital. Since
$k_\tau<k^*<k_{\mathrm{GR}}$ when $b>0$, steady private consumption also falls.
For $b=0$, $f(k)$ is increasing and the same consumption ranking follows.
Per-person output still grows at $g$ on the balanced growth path: this tax
changes its level, while technology growth remains exogenous.

The tax base matters. A tax on gross rental income $f'(k)K$, without depreciation
deductibility, would leave the net return $(1-\tau)f'(k)-\delta$ instead. Its
steady-state formula would therefore differ when $\delta>0$.

\subsubsection{The transition need not resemble the long-run comparison}

\begin{blueblock}
\textbf{A tax can raise consumption initially and lower it eventually.}
Suppose the economy starts at the untaxed steady state and an unexpected small
permanent capital tax is introduced, with full rebates and $G=0$. The
$\dot c=0$ line shifts left, while the $\dot k=0$ curve is unchanged. Capital
cannot jump. Consumption can, and locally it jumps \emph{up}: households now
want a smaller eventual capital stock and begin consuming part of the inherited
wealth. Then capital and consumption decline along the new stable path.
The initial consumption increase is consistent with lower long-run consumption.
\end{blueblock}

To see the local logic, write the new stable path as
$c-c_\tau\approx m_\tau(k-k_\tau)$, with $m_\tau>0$. At zero tax its slope is
$m=\eta-\lambda_->\eta$, whereas the resource curve has slope $\eta$.
A small fall in the target stock therefore places the new stable path above
the old steady-state consumption at the inherited $k^*$. This argument is
local and uses the stated rebate rule; government spending, announcements,
or other tax bases require their own transition analysis.
Write $\Delta k_\tau=k_\tau-k^*<0$ for a small tax. Expanding the resource
curve and the new local policy around zero tax makes the sign calculation
explicit:
\begin{align}
c_\tau-c^*&=[f'(k^*)-b]\Delta k_\tau+O(\tau^2)
             =\eta\Delta k_\tau+O(\tau^2),\\
c(0^+)&=c_\tau+m_\tau(k^*-k_\tau)+O(\tau^2),\\
c(0^+)-c^*&=\eta\Delta k_\tau-m\Delta k_\tau+O(\tau^2),\\
 &=(\eta-m)\Delta k_\tau+O(\tau^2)
  =\lambda_-\Delta k_\tau+O(\tau^2)>0.
\end{align}
Here $m_\tau=m+O(\tau)$, so multiplying its change by
$\Delta k_\tau=O(\tau)$ contributes only at second order. Both
$\lambda_-$ and the first-order $\Delta k_\tau$ are negative.

For a numerical level comparison, take $f(k)=k^{1/3}$, $\delta=0$,
$\rho=0.04$, $\theta=2$, $n=0.01$, and $g=0.02$. Then $b=0.03$ and
$\eta=0.05$. The steady-state formulas give
\begin{align}
f'(k)&=\tfrac13k^{-2/3}=q,\\
k^{-2/3}&=3q,\qquad k=(3q)^{-3/2},\\
q_0&=0.04+2(0.02)=0.08,\\
q_{0.20}&=\frac{0.04+2(0.02)}{1-0.20}=0.10,\\
c&=k^{1/3}-0.03k.
\end{align}

In this frictionless benchmark, forcing a capital tax wedge with no valued
public use of revenue moves the allocation away from the planner optimum.
This is a comparison of given policies, not a solution for optimal fiscal
policy. Redistribution, productive public expenditure, or market failures
would change the policy problem and must be modeled before drawing a broader
conclusion.
