\section{Solow Model}\label{sec:solow}

Chapter~\ref{sec:harrod-domar} separated the consistency of a growth path from
the behavior that might support or destabilize it. Solow changes one part of
the experiment: firms can vary capital intensity. We ask how this permits
capital adjustment, while maintaining full employment and a given saving rule.

\subsection{From the growth question to the model}\label{sec:solow-motivation}

\subsubsection{What if production can adjust?}

The fixed-proportions benchmark in \eqref{eq:hd-compatibility} requires
$s/v-\delta=n+\gamma$. Its parameters need not satisfy that equality. Before
concluding that sustained full employment requires a coincidence, ask what
made $v$ fixed. We described production as a single recipe: additional machines
could not economize on workers, and additional workers could not use a less
capital-intensive technique.

Solow's 1956 paper made this restriction central to its response to the
Harrod--Domar framework. His later account also questioned treating saving,
labor-force growth, and the capital-output ratio as independently given
constants.\footnote{Robert M. Solow (1956), ``A Contribution to the Theory of
Economic Growth,'' \textit{Quarterly Journal of Economics}, 70(1), 65--94,
Sections I--III, \url{https://doi.org/10.2307/1884513}; and Solow (1987),
``Growth Theory and After,'' Nobel lecture,
\url{https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/1987/solow/lecture/}.}
This addresses the production-side compatibility problem. Harrod's investment
responses and Domar's demand problem remain distinct questions, as the previous
chapter showed.

\subsubsection{Choose one adjustment mechanism}

Several responses are possible. Saving might adjust; labor supply or employment
might change; firms might use different factor proportions. Each response
would produce a different model. To isolate the last one, keep a simple saving
rule and an exogenous labor supply, maintain full employment, and allow
substitution in production. The sequence here reconstructs the published
argument as a modeling exercise, not the author's private thought process.

Start without technical change and replace the fixed recipe by $Y=F(K,L)$.
More machines per worker can raise output, while diminishing returns reduce
output per unit of capital. In
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dot K}{K}=s\frac{Y}{K}-\delta,
\end{equation}
$Y/K$ can now fall as capital accumulates. Capital growth can slow toward
labor-force growth without a change in $s$ or $n$. This is a candidate
adjustment mechanism; the properties of $F$ and the dynamics below must still
establish whether it works. Maintaining full employment makes that experiment
possible, rather than proving that actual economies always achieve it.

\subsubsection{Close the system and choose a useful intensity}

Choosing a flexible $F$ is not enough. In $\dot K=sF(K,L)-\delta K$, both
$K$ and $L$ could vary. To study capital adjustment first, specify
$L(t)=L_0e^{nt}$ and give $K(0)$. This leaves one inherited stock to solve for.
An alternative with wage-sensitive labor supply would require another decision
rule; it is a possible extension, not an algebraic rearrangement.

Next, a large economy has more capital partly because it has more workers.
The relevant object for this question is $K/L$. Constant returns allows
$Y/L=F(K/L,1)$, reducing the accumulation problem to a single capital intensity.
The ratio removes scale while preserving the mechanism we want to study.

Finally, if labor efficiency rises, even constant equipment per worker may
fail to keep up with productive opportunities. Introduce labor-augmenting
technology $A$ and use $K/(AL)$ instead. We can distinguish accumulating capital
relative to effective labor from growth in output per worker. We take $A$ as
given here; explaining innovation would add a different question.

The assumptions below collect these choices: a resource constraint, a
production experiment, a saving rule, exogenous labor and technology paths,
and a normalization. The full-employment assumption removes demand failures
from the dynamics being studied; the resulting convergence claim must be read
within that experiment.

\subsection{Economic environment and assumptions}\label{economic-environment-and-specific-assumptions}

The conditions are grouped by their economic role. The cards give the full
specification; the following paragraphs explain the notation and implications.

\begin{modelsetup}[1]
\begin{modelcondition}{01}{Economy and sectors}
A closed economy contains households and firms, with no government or foreign
trade. One good can be consumed or invested. Goods-market clearing requires
$Y_t=C_t+I_t$; in this two-sector economy, aggregate saving equals investment.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{02}{Households and the saving rule}
There are many homogeneous households. They own capital and firms, receive
factor incomes and any distributed profits, and supply labor inelastically.
Each worker supplies one unit of labor. Consumption and saving follow an
imposed rule rather than an intertemporal utility-maximization problem:
\[
S_t=sY_t,\qquad C_t=(1-s)Y_t,\qquad I_t=S_t.
\]
The rule permits $s\in[0,1]$. The positive-capital, positive-consumption
benchmark below uses $0<s<1$; the endpoints are separate limiting cases.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{03}{Firms and competitive markets}
There are many price-taking firms using the same production technology.
They hire labor and rent capital in competitive factor markets. A
representative firm's problem is
\[
\max_{K,L}\;F(K,A_tL)-q_tK-w_tL.
\]
Common technology and constant returns allow aggregation into the production
function below. Household saving is imposed; firms still maximize profits.
Homogeneity, competition, and optimization are distinct assumptions.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{04}{Endowments, ownership, and depreciation}
Initial stocks $K_0,A_0,L_0$ are given and positive. Households provide labor
at wage $w_t$ and rent their capital to firms at rental price $q_t$.
Depreciation removes a fraction $\delta$ per discrete period, with
$0\leq\delta\leq1$; investment is usable next period:
\[
K_{t+1}=(1-\delta)K_t+I_t.
\]
In continuous time, $\dot K=I-\delta K$ and the net capital return is
$r=q-\delta$. The original course notation $R$ for the rental price corresponds
to $q$ here. With a common saving share applied to all income and no
household-specific constraints, the distribution of ownership does not change
aggregate saving; its distributional consequences are outside this model.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{05}{Technology and exogenous paths}
Technology is freely and publicly available to all firms. There are no
adoption costs, patents, or research choices in the benchmark. Labor and
labor efficiency grow exogenously:
\[
A_{t+1}=(1+g)A_t,\qquad L_{t+1}=(1+n)L_t,\qquad g,n\geq0.
\]
The corresponding continuous-time paths satisfy $\dot A/A=g$ and $\dot L/L=n$;
rates in the two time conventions need not be numerically identical. The
interior steady-state analysis assumes $\delta+n+g>0$.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{06}{Production and its restrictions}
Technology is labor-augmenting (Harrod-neutral): $Y_t=F(K_t,A_tL_t)$.
$F$ is continuous, twice differentiable for positive inputs, positive there,
and has positive, diminishing marginal products of each input. It has constant
returns to capital and effective labor:
\[
F(\lambda K,\lambda AL)=\lambda F(K,AL),\qquad \lambda>0.
\]
Write $f(k)=F(k,1)$. The intensive-form restrictions used below are
\[
\begin{gathered}
f(0)=0,\qquad f'(k)>0,\qquad f''(k)<0\quad(k>0),\\
\lim_{k\downarrow0}f'(k)=\infty,\qquad
\lim_{k\to\infty}f'(k)=0.
\end{gathered}
\]
The last two are Inada restrictions on marginal products. They are sufficient
benchmark assumptions, rather than consequences of competition.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{07}{Market clearing and factor incomes}
All supplied labor and capital are employed. With bars denoting supplied
endowments and superscripts denoting supply and demand,
\[
L_t^d=L_t^s=\bar L_t,\qquad K_t^d=K_t^s=\bar K_t.
\]
Competitive prices equal marginal products, so constant returns implies
\[
q_t=F_1(K_t,A_tL_t),\qquad w_t=A_tF_2(K_t,A_tL_t),
\qquad Y_t=q_tK_t+w_tL_t.
\]
Firms are owned by households. Their economic profits are zero after paying
both factors; the owners still receive capital rental income.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{08}{Normalization and the closed system}
Use quantities per effective worker:
\[
k_t=\frac{K_t}{A_tL_t},\qquad y_t=\frac{Y_t}{A_tL_t}=f(k_t),
\qquad c_t=\frac{C_t}{A_tL_t}=(1-s)f(k_t).
\]
Given initial stocks and the paths of $A_t,L_t$, the three main equations are
\[
Y_t=F(K_t,A_tL_t),\qquad I_t=S_t=sY_t,\qquad
K_{t+1}=(1-\delta)K_t+I_t.
\]
The resource constraint, full employment, and competitive factor prices make
this a closed aggregate system. They are maintained assumptions of the
experiment, not conclusions about demand adjustment in actual economies.
\end{modelcondition}
\end{modelsetup}

\modelsequence{K_t,A_t,L_t}{Y_t\;\to\; C_t,I_t}{K_{t+1}}

\subsubsection{Resources, timing, and behavior}

The economy is closed, has no government, and produces one good. Households own
capital and supply labor inelastically. Each worker supplies one unit of labor;
changes in hours, participation, and employment rates are omitted. Firms hire
labor and rent capital in competitive markets.

In discrete time, $K_t$ is available at the start of period $t$. Given $K_t$,
$A_t$, and $L_t$, firms produce $Y_t$. Households consume part of output and
save the fraction $s\in(0,1)$. Investment becomes available next period:
\begin{align}
Y_t&=C_t+I_t,\qquad I_t=S_t=sY_t,\label{eq:solow-resources}\\
K_{t+1}&=(1-\delta)K_t+sY_t,\qquad 0\leq\delta\leq1.\label{eq:solow-capital}
\end{align}
The saving rule is imposed. Firms still maximize profits; the absence of a
household optimization problem does not imply that firms are non-optimizing.

Technology and labor follow
\begin{equation}\label{eq:solow-exogenous}
A_{t+1}=(1+g)A_t,\qquad L_{t+1}=(1+n)L_t,
\qquad g,n\geq0.
\end{equation}
Initial $K_0,A_0,L_0$ are positive. We assume $\delta+n+g>0$ to obtain a finite
positive steady state below. Technology is labor-augmenting: an increase in
$A$ makes a worker provide more effective labor. Its availability and growth
are given, rather than explained by research or adoption decisions.

\subsubsection{Production and normalization}

Let
\begin{equation}\label{eq:solow-production}
Y_t=F(K_t,A_tL_t).
\end{equation}
$F$ has constant returns in capital and effective labor:
\begin{equation}
F(\lambda K,\lambda AL)=\lambda F(K,AL),\qquad \lambda>0.
\end{equation}
Replicating all inputs replicates output. This rules out a fixed factor, such
as land, that becomes scarcer as the economy expands. It also rules out
aggregate increasing returns in these two inputs.

One motivation for constant returns is to treat any gains from organizing
production at a larger scale as already exhausted at the scale being modeled.
Another is to abstract from scarce inputs other than capital and effective
labor. These are ways to motivate the assumption, not proofs that it holds.

Divide by effective labor and define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:solow-normalization}
k_t\equiv\frac{K_t}{A_tL_t},\qquad
 y_t\equiv\frac{Y_t}{A_tL_t}=F(k_t,1)\equiv f(k_t),\qquad
 c_t\equiv\frac{C_t}{A_tL_t}=(1-s)f(k_t).
\end{equation}
Here lower-case variables are \emph{per effective worker}. Output per worker is
$Y_t/L_t=A_ty_t$, and capital per worker is $K_t/L_t=A_tk_t$. This differs from
the per-hour normalization used for level accounting in Chapter~\ref{sec:growth-facts}.
With $A_t=1$, the two capital normalizations coincide.

Labor-augmenting technology also makes a balanced growth path convenient to
describe. If $k$ settles at a positive constant, then
$K/Y=k/f(k)$ is constant. The functional form alone does not make that ratio
constant during a transition. Hicks-neutral technology instead takes the form
$Y=A_H\widetilde F(K,L)$. It is a different placement of productivity; with
Cobb--Douglas, $A_H=A^{1-\alpha}$ converts between the two representations.
The exogenous growth rate must be converted as well.

Assume $f(0)=0$ and, for $k>0$,
\begin{equation}
f'(k)>0,\qquad f''(k)<0,\qquad
\lim_{k\downarrow0}f'(k)=\infty,\qquad
\lim_{k\to\infty}f'(k)=0.
\end{equation}
The last two are the Inada conditions on \emph{marginal products}, not output
levels. With the other assumptions, they make capital sufficiently productive
near zero and insufficiently productive at very high intensity to guarantee
an interior crossing of investment and replacement needs. They are sufficient
benchmark restrictions, not empirical laws for every technology.

Our running example is Cobb--Douglas:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:solow-cd}
F(K,AL)=K^\alpha(AL)^{1-\alpha},\qquad f(k)=k^\alpha,
\qquad 0<\alpha<1.
\end{equation}
Diminishing returns here means adding machines to a fixed amount of effective
labor produces progressively smaller additions to output. It does not mean
that output falls when capital rises.

Cobb--Douglas is a convenient specification satisfying these restrictions;
it is not deduced uniquely from economic behavior. Its constant factor shares
are an additional restriction. Likewise, Inada conditions and an exogenous
technology path delimit the experiment: the former restrict behavior at
extreme capital intensities, while the latter leaves innovation unexplained.
Relaxing either calls for a new argument about the resulting dynamics.

\subsubsection{Prices and market clearing}

At each date, let $q_t$ be the real rental price of capital and $w_t$ the wage
per worker. A price-taking firm solves
\begin{equation}
\max_{K,L}\;F(K,A_tL)-q_tK-w_tL.
\end{equation}
At an interior solution, denoting derivatives with respect to the two arguments
of $F$ by $F_1,F_2$,
\begin{equation}
q_t=F_1(K_t,A_tL_t),\qquad w_t=A_tF_2(K_t,A_tL_t).
\end{equation}
Constant returns and Euler's theorem for homogeneous functions give
\begin{equation}
Y_t=F_1K_t+F_2A_tL_t=q_tK_t+w_tL_t.
\end{equation}
\begin{blueblock}
\textbf{Zero profits do not mean zero capital income.}
Economic profits are zero after paying both factors, including capital owned
by households. This is a property of this competitive constant-returns
benchmark, not a claim that owners receive no capital income.
\end{blueblock}

For Cobb--Douglas, $q_t=\alpha k_t^{\alpha-1}$ and
$w_t=(1-\alpha)A_tk_t^\alpha$. Capital deepening lowers the rental return and
raises the wage per effective worker. Prices support full use of the supplied
capital and labor. Goods-market clearing and the saving rule then determine
investment. Given the initial stocks and exogenous paths, the model is closed.

\begin{greenblock}
\textbf{The mechanism in one loop.}
Capital and effective labor determine output; the saving rule turns some output
into investment; investment and depreciation determine next period's capital.
Factor prices describe the returns along this path. The saving rate does not
respond to those returns until we change the household side of the model.
\end{greenblock}

\subsection{Solow model in discrete time}\label{solow-model-in-discrete-time}

\subsubsection{Deriving the law of motion}

\begin{redblock}
\textbf{Question: which capital variable makes the problem stationary?}
Start from $K_{t+1}=(1-\delta)K_t+sY_t$. Dividing by $L_{t+1}$ describes
capital per worker, but growing labor efficiency still changes production
at each date. Dividing by $A_{t+1}L_{t+1}$ instead gives a stationary map in
capital per effective worker when $s,n,g,\delta$ are constant. The derivation
below keeps the exact growth denominator; the per-worker special case follows
in the blue note. Choosing a normalization is part of solving the economic
problem, rather than merely shortening its notation.
\end{redblock}

Define $D\equiv(1+n)(1+g)$, the gross growth factor of effective labor. Divide
\eqref{eq:solow-capital} by $A_{t+1}L_{t+1}$:
\begin{align}
k_{t+1}
&=\frac{(1-\delta)K_t+sY_t}{A_{t+1}L_{t+1}},\\
&=\frac{(1-\delta)K_t/(A_tL_t)+sY_t/(A_tL_t)}{D},\\
&=\frac{(1-\delta)k_t+sf(k_t)}{D}\equiv\Phi(k_t).
\label{eq:solow-discrete-map}
\end{align}
The numerator adds surviving capital and investment per current effective
worker. The denominator spreads it across next period's effective labor. New
workers need equipment, and increasing labor efficiency requires more equipment
per worker to keep $k$ constant.

Subtract $k_t$ and define $d_D\equiv D-1+\delta$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:solow-discrete-change}
k_{t+1}-k_t=\frac{sf(k_t)-d_Dk_t}{D},\qquad
 d_D=\delta+n+g+ng.
\end{equation}
Break-even investment $d_Dk_t$ keeps capital per effective worker unchanged.
The $ng$ term is part of the exact discrete-time expression; dropping it is a
small-rate approximation. Setting $g=0$ gives $d_D=\delta+n$; setting $g=n=0$
gives $d_D=\delta$.

\begin{blueblock}
\textbf{Reading the per-worker version.}
Let $\bar k_t=K_t/L_t$. Dividing the aggregate accumulation equation by
$L_{t+1}$ instead gives the exact law
\[
\bar k_{t+1}=\frac{(1-\delta)\bar k_t+sF(\bar k_t,A_t)}{1+n}.
\]
There is no $1+g$ in this denominator: technology remains inside production.
With constant $A_t=\bar A$, define $\bar f(\bar k)=F(\bar k,\bar A)$.
The map is then stationary; setting $n=0$ also removes the population-growth
denominator. These are valid restricted cases. When technology grows, a
stationary map in $k=K/(AL)$ requires the effective-labor normalization above.
\end{blueblock}

\subsubsection{A steady state and the path toward it}

A positive normalized steady state satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:solow-discrete-steady}
k^*=\Phi(k^*),\qquad sf(k^*)=d_Dk^*,\qquad
\frac{K}{Y}=\frac{k^*}{f(k^*)}=\frac{s}{d_D}.
\end{equation}
A stable capital-output ratio is a conclusion \emph{on this path}, not something
that follows from labor-augmenting notation alone.

For existence and uniqueness, divide the middle equation by $k^*>0$. Strict
concavity and $f(0)=0$ imply $f'(k)<f(k)/k$, so
\begin{equation}
\frac{d}{dk}\frac{f(k)}{k}=\frac{kf'(k)-f(k)}{k^2}<0.
\end{equation}
The Inada conditions imply $f(k)/k$ falls from infinity to zero. Hence
$sf(k)/k=d_D>0$ has exactly one positive solution. Zero is also an absorbing
boundary under $f(0)=0$; our convergence result starts from $k_0>0$.

Below $k^*$, saving exceeds break-even investment and $k$ rises. Above $k^*$,
saving is insufficient to maintain that intensity and $k$ falls. Moreover,
\begin{equation}
\Phi'(k)=\frac{1-\delta+sf'(k)}{D}>0.
\end{equation}
Since $\Phi$ is increasing and $\Phi(k^*)=k^*$, an initial $k<k^*$ maps below
$k^*$, while an initial $k>k^*$ maps above it. The sequence is therefore
monotone and bounded toward $k^*$, and continuity makes its limit a fixed point.
The unique positive fixed point is the limit for every $k_0>0$.

\begin{blueblock}
\textbf{A steady state is not the definition of equilibrium.}
A steady state is one particular equilibrium path. A transition from an
arbitrary initial stock is also an equilibrium path under the imposed saving
rule. Convergence does not make the transition cease to be an equilibrium.
\end{blueblock}

\subsubsection{What grows on a balanced growth path?}

If $k_t=k^*$, then $y_t=f(k^*)$ and $c_t=(1-s)f(k^*)$ are constant. Yet
\begin{equation}\label{eq:solow-bgp}
\frac{Y_t}{L_t}=A_tf(k^*),\qquad
\frac{K_t}{L_t}=A_tk^*,\qquad
Y_t=A_tL_tf(k^*).
\end{equation}
Output and capital per worker grow at gross rate $1+g$; their aggregates grow
at $D$. With no technical progress, there is no sustained growth per worker on
this path, even if the population and total output keep growing.

For Cobb--Douglas, solve $s(k^*)^\alpha=d_Dk^*$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:solow-cd-steady}
k^*=\left(\frac{s}{d_D}\right)^{1/(1-\alpha)},\qquad
y^*=\left(\frac{s}{d_D}\right)^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)}.
\end{equation}
A higher saving rate raises the long-run level of output per effective worker.
Holding the technology path fixed, it does not change the long-run growth rate
of output per worker. Its steady-state level elasticity is
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial\log y^*}{\partial\log s}=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}.
\end{equation}
This comparison holds other determinants fixed. It is not a causal estimate
from a cross-country correlation between saving and income.

\subsubsection{Saving, rebuilding, and conditional convergence}

Suppose an economy starts on its old balanced growth path and permanently
raises $s$. At the time of the change, $K$, $A$, and $L$ are inherited, so output
is unchanged and consumption falls. Higher investment raises future $K$; output
per worker then grows faster during the transition. As diminishing returns
reduce the extra investment generated by another unit of capital, $k$ approaches
its new, higher steady state. Long-run growth per worker returns to $g$.

This explains why an investment expansion can have a lasting level effect
without a lasting growth-rate effect. It does not establish that any observed
investment campaign will be productive: the one-good benchmark abstracts from
project selection, idle capacity, financing, and heterogeneous returns.

A one-time loss of physical capital, with technology, labor, and saving behavior
unchanged, lowers $k$. The high marginal product at the lower stock supports
rebuilding toward the same path. Actual disasters can also destroy skills,
institutions, and technology, so this experiment isolates a capital-loss
mechanism rather than predicting every recovery.

The convergence result is conditional on the determinants of $k^*$ and the
technology path. A low-income economy with a low saving rate or low productivity
need not grow faster than a richer economy already close to a higher path.
Income alone does not tell us distance from the relevant steady state.

\subsubsection{Local convergence speed}

Let $x_t=k_t-k^*$. A first-order Taylor expansion at $k^*$ gives
\begin{align}
k_{t+1}&=\Phi(k^*)+\Phi'(k^*)x_t+O(x_t^2),\\
x_{t+1}&\approx\phi x_t,\qquad
\phi\equiv\frac{1-\delta+sf'(k^*)}{D}.
\end{align}
Because $sf'(k^*)<sf(k^*)/k^*=d_D$, we have $0<\phi<1$. Iterating the
linearized system yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:solow-discrete-speed}
x_t\approx\phi^t x_0,\qquad
\lambda_D=-\log\phi>0,\qquad
T_{1/2}\approx\frac{\log2}{\lambda_D}.
\end{equation}
$\phi$ is persistence; a larger $\phi$ means slower adjustment. $\lambda_D$ is
the corresponding decay rate per period, and $T_{1/2}$ is the local half-life
of a deviation. The recursion for the linearized system has an exact geometric solution;
the approximation is in using that system to describe the original model.
The nonlinear path need not have this half-life far from its steady state.

For Cobb--Douglas, $sf'(k^*)=\alpha d_D$, so
\begin{equation}
\phi=1-\frac{(1-\alpha)d_D}{D}.
\end{equation}
Adjustment is gradual because productive capacity is a stock. Investment can
change immediately, but only accumulated investment changes that stock.

\subsection{Solow model in continuous time}\label{solow-model-in-continuous-time}

\subsubsection{The same mechanism with continuous adjustment}

Now use continuous rates $\dot L/L=n$, $\dot A/A=g$, and depreciation rate
$\delta\geq0$, with $b\equiv n+g+\delta>0$. These are rates per unit of time;
they are not numerically identical to finite-period percentage changes. Write
\begin{equation}
\dot K=sF(K,AL)-\delta K,\qquad k=\frac{K}{AL},\qquad y=f(k).
\end{equation}
Log differentiation of the definition of $k$, followed by substitution, gives
\begin{align}
\frac{\dot k}{k}
&=\frac{\dot K}{K}-\frac{\dot A}{A}-\frac{\dot L}{L},\\
&=\frac{sY}{K}-\delta-g-n,\\
\dot k&=s\frac{Y}{AL}-(n+g+\delta)k
       =sf(k)-bk\equiv G(k).\label{eq:solow-continuous}
\end{align}
With $g=0$ and $A=1$, this becomes $\dot k=sf(k)-(n+\delta)k$.
That familiar constant-technology equation is an exact special case.

New investment increases capital intensity; depreciation, population growth,
and technical progress reduce it for a given aggregate stock. Technical
progress still raises productive possibilities: it reduces $k$ here because
each worker now represents more effective labor.

For a short time interval $\Delta t$, effective labor grows by approximately
$1+(n+g)\Delta t$ and depreciation is approximately $\delta\Delta t$. Applying
the discrete accounting over that interval gives
\begin{equation}
k(t+\Delta t)-k(t)
=\left[sf(k(t))-bk(t)\right]\Delta t+o(\Delta t).
\end{equation}
Divide by $\Delta t$ and take its limit to obtain
\eqref{eq:solow-continuous}. This explains why the continuous break-even rate
is $b$, whereas the exact discrete expression contains the product $ng$.

The positive steady state satisfies $sf(k^*)=bk^*$; $G(k)>0$ below it and
$G(k)<0$ above it. On the balanced growth path, $Y/L$ and $K/L$ grow at $g$,
$Y$ and $K$ grow at $n+g$, and $K/Y=s/b$. Under competitive pricing the net
instantaneous real return is $r=f'(k)-\delta$.

\subsubsection{Local approximation and an exact Cobb--Douglas solution}

Linearizing the law of motion around its steady state gives
\begin{align}
\dot k&\approx G'(k^*)(k-k^*),\\
G'(k^*)&=sf'(k^*)-b<0,\\
k(t)-k^*&\approx[k(0)-k^*]e^{-\lambda_Ct},
\qquad \lambda_C\equiv b-sf'(k^*)>0.\label{eq:solow-continuous-speed}
\end{align}
The exponential expression solves the \emph{linearized} system. It is generally
only a local approximation to the original nonlinear equation. For
Cobb--Douglas, $\lambda_C=(1-\alpha)b$ and the local half-life is
$\log2/[(1-\alpha)b]$.

Cobb--Douglas also permits an exact solution using a different variable. Set
$z(t)=k(t)^{1-\alpha}$ and differentiate:
\begin{align}
\dot z
&=(1-\alpha)k^{-\alpha}\dot k,\\
&=(1-\alpha)k^{-\alpha}(sk^\alpha-bk)
 =(1-\alpha)(s-bz).
\end{align}
This is a linear equation in $z$, with steady state $z^*=s/b$. Its solution
and the implied capital path are
\begin{align}
z(t)&=\frac{s}{b}+\left[z(0)-\frac{s}{b}\right]
 e^{-(1-\alpha)bt},\\
k(t)&=\left\{\frac{s}{b}+\left[k(0)^{1-\alpha}-\frac{s}{b}\right]
 e^{-(1-\alpha)bt}\right\}^{1/(1-\alpha)}.\label{eq:solow-exact-path}
\end{align}
Capital approaches its steady state without arriving there at a finite date
unless it starts there. ``Time to convergence'' must therefore specify a
tolerance or a half-life, rather than an exact date of arrival.

\subsection{The Golden Rule and the cost of saving}\label{golden-rate}

More saving raises steady-state output, but output is not consumption. To
maintain a larger capital stock, the economy must devote more resources to
investment. The Golden Rule asks which balanced growth path provides the
largest consumption per effective worker, holding the technology and labor
paths fixed.

In continuous time, a stationary $k$ requires investment $bk$, so feasible
steady-state consumption is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:solow-gr-consumption}
c^*=f(k)-bk.
\end{equation}
Maximize this expression over $k$:
\begin{align}
\frac{dc^*}{dk}&=f'(k)-b,\\
f'(k_{\mathrm{GR}})&=b=n+g+\delta,\qquad
\frac{d^2c^*}{dk^2}=f''(k)<0.\label{eq:solow-gr}
\end{align}
One extra unit of capital adds $f'(k)$ to output but requires $b$ units of
investment per unit of time to maintain its intensity. At the Golden Rule,
these marginal amounts are equal. Equivalently, the net marginal return
$f'(k_{\mathrm{GR}})-\delta$ equals $n+g$.

The saving rate that supports this stock satisfies
\begin{equation}
s_{\mathrm{GR}}=\frac{bk_{\mathrm{GR}}}{f(k_{\mathrm{GR}})}.
\end{equation}
For Cobb--Douglas, substitute
$\alpha k_{\mathrm{GR}}^{\alpha-1}=b$:
\begin{equation}
k_{\mathrm{GR}}=\left(\frac{\alpha}{b}\right)^{1/(1-\alpha)},
\qquad s_{\mathrm{GR}}=\alpha.
\end{equation}
In discrete time the argument is the same with $d_D$ replacing $b$:
$c^*=f(k)-d_Dk$ and $f'(k_{\mathrm{GR}})=d_D$. The Cobb--Douglas saving share
is again $\alpha$ under this timing convention.

\begin{blueblock}
\textbf{A welfare boundary.}
The Golden Rule maximizes consumption \emph{across balanced growth paths}.
With constant technology and strictly increasing utility $u(c)$, maximizing
stationary consumption also maximizes utility evaluated at that stationary
consumption. Solow has not specified discounting or how to value consumption
losses during a transition. It therefore does not establish that
moving toward the Golden Rule maximizes intertemporal welfare.
\end{blueblock}

If the initial steady state lies below $k_{\mathrm{GR}}$, raising saving toward
$s_{\mathrm{GR}}$ increases eventual consumption but requires sacrificing
consumption while capital is built. If it lies above $k_{\mathrm{GR}}$, reducing
saving toward the Golden Rule can raise both current and eventual consumption.
Investment is then maintaining more capital than is useful for maximizing
steady-state consumption. A high investment share alone is therefore not a
welfare criterion. Ramsey will specify preferences and make the transition
trade-off part of the saving decision; OLG will examine dynamic efficiency in
an economy with different generations.

\subsection{Extensions: which assumption needs to change?}\label{sec:solow-extensions}

An extension should answer a question left open by the benchmark. Adding an
input, changing the investment technology, and explaining productivity are
different changes; they need not have the same implications for long-run growth.
The examples below use continuous time.

\subsubsection{Investment spending and productive capital}

In the baseline, one unit of investment creates one unit of productive capital.
To separate spending from productive capacity, suppose only a fraction
$\eta\in(0,1]$ becomes usable capital:
\begin{equation}
I=sY,\qquad C=(1-s)Y,\qquad \dot K=\eta I-\delta K.
\end{equation}
The unconverted part is lost in installation or implementation; it is not
additional consumption. With the same labor and technology paths,
\begin{equation}
\dot k=\eta sf(k)-bk,\qquad
k^*=\left(\frac{\eta s}{b}\right)^{1/(1-\alpha)}
\quad\text{for }f(k)=k^\alpha.
\end{equation}
Higher investment spending and more effective conversion into capital both
raise the steady-state stock, but only the former directly reduces the
consumption share. Poor project selection or implementation can therefore
weaken the link between a reported investment share and productive capacity.
This scalar $\eta$ is an illustrative reduced form: it does not yet explain
which projects are chosen or how finance allocates resources.

At a stationary $k$, maintaining it requires $I/(AL)=bk/\eta$, so
$c=f(k)-bk/\eta$. The Golden Rule becomes $f'(k)=b/\eta$. Welfare comparisons
must account for the resources spent creating capital, not only the stock
that eventually appears in production.

\subsubsection{Accumulating human capital}

Machines need skills to be useful. The augmented Solow model of Mankiw,
Romer, and Weil adds a reproducible human-capital stock to the production
function.\footnote{N. Gregory Mankiw, David Romer, and David N. Weil (1992),
``A Contribution to the Empirics of Economic Growth,'' \textit{Quarterly Journal
of Economics}, 107(2), 407--437, Section II.
\url{https://doi.org/10.2307/2118477}.}
Write
\begin{equation}
Y=K^\alpha H^\beta(AL)^{1-\alpha-\beta},\qquad
\alpha,\beta>0,\quad \alpha+\beta<1.
\end{equation}
Here $H$ denotes a stock of human capital, rather than the quality-adjusted
hours denoted by $H$ in Chapter~\ref{sec:growth-facts}. To keep the distinction
visible, use $u\equiv H/(AL)$ for human capital per effective worker. Then
\begin{equation}
y=k^\alpha u^\beta.
\end{equation}
Suppose output can be transformed into either stock, with fixed investment
shares $s_K,s_H>0$, $s_K+s_H<1$, and the same depreciation rate for both:
\begin{align}
\dot K&=s_KY-\delta K,\qquad
\dot H=s_HY-\delta H,\\
\dot k&=s_Ky-bk,\qquad
\dot u=s_Hy-bu.\label{eq:solow-human-dynamics}
\end{align}
There are now two state variables. Consumption is
$c=(1-s_K-s_H)y$; investment in skills uses resources rather than appearing
costlessly in production.

At a positive steady state, the two accumulation equations imply
\begin{equation}
k^*=\frac{s_K}{b}y^*,\qquad u^*=\frac{s_H}{b}y^*.
\end{equation}
Substitute both into production and collect the powers of $y^*$:
\begin{align}
y^*&=\left(\frac{s_Ky^*}{b}\right)^\alpha
       \left(\frac{s_Hy^*}{b}\right)^\beta,\\
(y^*)^{1-\alpha-\beta}
&=\frac{s_K^\alpha s_H^\beta}{b^{\alpha+\beta}},\\
y^*&=\left(\frac{s_K^\alpha s_H^\beta}
 {b^{\alpha+\beta}}\right)^{1/(1-\alpha-\beta)}.
\end{align}
The condition $\alpha+\beta<1$ preserves diminishing returns to the two
accumulated inputs together. The extension can change income levels and
transition dynamics, while long-run growth per worker on its balanced growth
path still comes from $A$. A higher human-capital investment share is not, by
itself, a permanent growth-rate increase in this specification.

Years of schooling are a proxy for skills, not a direct observation of $H$ or
$s_H$. Pritchett (2001) highlights why schooling expansion need not translate
uniformly into aggregate growth: learning quality, demand for educated labor,
and the activities rewarded by institutions can differ.\footnote{Lant
Pritchett (2001), ``Where Has All the Education Gone?,'' \textit{World Bank
Economic Review}, 15(3), 367--391, especially Section II.
\url{https://doi.org/10.1093/wber/15.3.367}.}
\begin{greenblock}
\textbf{What can weak schooling--growth estimates tell us?}
Weak estimates in a particular specification do not establish that skills have
no productive value. They call for checking the proposed accumulation
mechanism, measurement, and identification.
\end{greenblock}

\subsubsection{Technology adoption and catch-up}

Giving every economy the same $A$ path assumes away differences in access to
and use of technology. To illustrate an adoption mechanism, let frontier
efficiency $A_F$ grow at $g_F$ and posit, for a follower with $A\leq A_F$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dot A}{A}=g_F+\mu\log\frac{A_F}{A},\qquad \mu>0.
\end{equation}
This is an illustrative adoption rule, not a consequence of the original
saving equation. Define the log technology gap $v=\log(A_F/A)$. Then
\begin{align}
\dot v&=g_F-\frac{\dot A}{A}=-\mu v,\\
v(t)&=v(0)e^{-\mu t}.
\end{align}
A larger gap creates scope for faster adoption; $\mu$ governs how quickly that
scope is used. Technology catch-up differs from rebuilding a low capital stock.
It can change output directly and also induce capital accumulation.

During catch-up, $\dot A/A$ varies with the gap. The capital equation is now
\begin{equation}
\dot k=sf(k)-\left[n+\delta+g_F+\mu v(t)\right]k.
\end{equation}
We cannot use the earlier constant-$g$ transition unchanged. As the technology
gap closes, this equation approaches the benchmark with $g=g_F$.
Training, organizational changes, and technology access could help determine
$\mu$, but this rule does not model their costs or incentives. Explaining those
choices is a further step toward endogenous productivity.

\subsubsection{Removing diminishing returns: the AK benchmark}

What if returns to the reproducible stock do not diminish? Let
\begin{equation}
Y=\mathcal A K,\qquad \mathcal A>0\text{ constant},\qquad
\dot K=(s\mathcal A-\delta)K.
\end{equation}
With labor growth $n$, output per worker satisfies
\begin{equation}
g_{Y/L}=s\mathcal A-\delta-n.
\end{equation}
A permanent increase in saving now raises the growth rate permanently. The
reason is the changed production assumption: each additional unit of capital
has the same product. The Solow Inada and strict-concavity conditions no longer
hold, and there is no positive stationary $K/L$ when this growth rate is positive.

$K$ can be interpreted as a broad reproducible composite only if we explain
how its components can be accumulated without diminishing aggregate returns.
The AK equation shows the role of that restriction; it does not by itself
supply a theory of innovation, externalities, or optimal saving. Those
mechanisms belong in the endogenous growth chapter.

\subsubsection{Fixed land and the transition to modern growth}

The baseline omits a fixed input that becomes scarce. Add land $X$:
\begin{equation}
Y=BK^aL^\ell X^{1-a-\ell},\qquad
a,\ell>0,\quad a+\ell<1.
\end{equation}
$B$ is Hicks-neutral productivity, as in Chapter~\ref{sec:growth-facts}.
Dividing by labor gives
\begin{equation}
\frac{Y}{L}=B\left(\frac{K}{L}\right)^a
                 \left(\frac{X}{L}\right)^{1-a-\ell}.
\end{equation}
At given $B$ and $K/L$, population growth lowers land per worker and hence
output per worker. If population growth also responds to income, productivity
gains may support more people rather than persistent gains in living standards.
The land equation alone does not establish this outcome; it needs a demographic
mechanism.

Hansen and Prescott (2002) study a transition in which a less land-intensive
production process becomes profitable as technology advances. Both production
processes are available throughout; which one is used changes in equilibrium.
Their analysis retains exogenous technical progress, so it explains a transition
in production rather than the ultimate source of innovation.\footnote{Gary D.
Hansen and Edward C. Prescott (2002), ``Malthus to Solow,'' \textit{American
Economic Review}, 92(4), 1205--1217.
\url{https://doi.org/10.1257/00028280260344731}.}

\textbf{Where the benchmark ends.}
Lower labor-force growth reduces dilution in Solow, holding everything else
fixed. This is not a complete assessment of ageing: participation, pensions,
and age-specific saving are absent. Likewise, the model contains no money,
banks, or foreign assets. Monetary, financial, or trade extensions need those
objects and their decision rules; their effects cannot be inferred merely
from their absence here.

The main unresolved choice is already inside the model: why should households
save a fixed share when their income and returns change? Ramsey replaces that
rule with an intertemporal problem, making consumption, patience, and the return
to saving jointly determine the accumulation path.
