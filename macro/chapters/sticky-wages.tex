\subsection{What if the wage contract survives the change in demand?}

Imagine a supplier whose orders fall while its annual pay agreements remain
in place. The supplier can reduce shifts before it can renegotiate the wage
schedule. Alternatively, product prices may rise before the next wage review,
reducing the purchasing power of an unchanged nominal wage. Both situations
ask how employment, labor cost, and household welfare adjust when the wage
quote was chosen earlier. Neither situation is described by saying simply
that the real wage is fixed.

The previous chapter let product prices adjust slowly but treated labor as
homogeneous and competitively traded. The household's marginal willingness to
work therefore equaled the common real wage at each date. To make wage setting
a meaningful choice, first give labor suppliers distinct services. A higher
wage then loses some employment, rather than either losing every customer or
leaving employment unaffected. A wage setter balances income against the
burden of supplying the hours employers demand at its quoted wage.

Next let that quote survive beyond the current date. A pay agreement that
looks attractive today may bring too many hours in a future boom or too little
income in a downturn. Wage setting must therefore consider future demand,
prices, and consumption valuation. Finally, allow product prices and wages to
be sticky together. The real wage becomes the link between households' labor
trade-off and firms' marginal costs, with neither side always able to move
its nominal quote immediately.

This is a teaching reconstruction. The model follows the staggered-wage and
price framework of Erceg, Henderson, and Levin, with the notation and local
pricing conventions of our NK chapter.\footnote{Erceg, Henderson, and Levin
(2000), \textit{Optimal Monetary Policy with Staggered Wage and Price Contracts},
Journal of Monetary Economics 46(2), 281--313; the
\href{https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/ifdp/optimal-monetary-policy-with-staggered-wage-and-price-contracts.htm}{authors' Federal Reserve working-paper version}
is dated 1999. See also Gal\'{\i}'s
\href{https://crei.cat/people/gali/}{official Chapter 6 teaching materials}.
The derivations and numerical examples below state their own assumptions.}

\begin{greenblock}
\textbf{Two contracts, two adjustment margins.}
A seller chooses what customers pay; a wage setter chooses what employers
pay for its labor service. Delayed product-price adjustment affects the
allocation of spending across goods. Delayed wage adjustment affects the
allocation of work across labor types. The two restrictions interact through
the real wage and the cost of producing goods.
\end{greenblock}

\subsection{Economic environment and timing}

\Needspace{12\baselineskip}
\begin{modelsetup}[1]
\begin{modelcondition}{01}{Products, labor types, and the household family}
Time is $t=0,1,\ldots$. There is a unit continuum of product varieties
$i\in[0,1]$ and labor services $j\in[0,1]$. A large representative family pools
its members' wage income and owns the firms. Consumption insurance within the
family equalizes consumption valuation across labor types; there is no
uninsured idiosyncratic consumption risk in this model. Each labor type has an
atomistic wage setter acting for the family and facing employers' demand for
that type. Product firms and wage setters take aggregate prices and quantities
as given. The economy is closed, with no capital accumulation, investment,
government purchases, banks, or job-search market.
\end{modelcondition}
\end{modelsetup}

\begin{modelsetup}[1]
\begin{modelcondition}{02}{Two CES composites and two price indices}
For $\epsilon_p,\epsilon_w>1$, product output and the labor input of firm $i$ are
\[
 Y_t=\left[\int Y_{it}^{(\epsilon_p-1)/\epsilon_p}di\right]^{\epsilon_p/(\epsilon_p-1)},
 \qquad N_{it}=\left[\int N_{ijt}^{(\epsilon_w-1)/\epsilon_w}dj\right]^{\epsilon_w/(\epsilon_w-1)}.
\]
Their dual indices are
\[
 P_t=\left[\int P_{it}^{1-\epsilon_p}di\right]^{1/(1-\epsilon_p)},
 \qquad W_t=\left[\int W_{jt}^{1-\epsilon_w}dj\right]^{1/(1-\epsilon_w)}.
\]
$N_{ijt}$ is type-$j$ work used at firm $i$; $N_{jt}=\int N_{ijt}di$ is demand
for that type across all firms. Write $N_t=\int N_{it}di$ for the aggregate
quantity of composite labor used by firms. It also equals the CES composite
of the $N_{jt}$ because every firm uses the same cost-minimizing type shares.
Physical hours $H_t=\int N_{jt}dj$ need not equal $N_t$ away from symmetry.
\end{modelcondition}
\end{modelsetup}

\begin{modelsetup}[1]
\begin{modelcondition}{03}{Technology, firms, and ownership income}
Firm $i$ produces $Y_{it}=A_tN_{it}^{1-\alpha}$ with $A_t>0$ and
$0\leq\alpha<1$. If $\alpha>0$, its fixed factor is normalized to one and is
owned through the family; it is not accumulated or traded. A firm minimizes
its labor bill for a required output, then chooses its product price when
allowed. Both product and labor markets have finite substitution elasticities;
constant desired gross markups are $\mu_p=\epsilon_p/(\epsilon_p-1)$ and
$\mu_w=\epsilon_w/(\epsilon_w-1)$. Distributed nominal profits $D_t$ include
fixed-factor income. The exact aggregate wage bill is $W_tN_t$, even when
individual wage quotes differ.
\end{modelcondition}
\end{modelsetup}

\begin{modelsetup}[1]
\begin{modelcondition}{04}{Preferences and the cost of different hours}
The family maximizes
\[
 E_0\sum_{t\geq0}\beta^t\xi_t
 \left[v(C_t)-\frac\chi{1+\varphi}\int_0^1N_{jt}^{1+\varphi}dj\right],
\]
where $0<\beta<1$, $\chi>0$, $\sigma>0$, $\varphi\geq0$, and $\xi_t>0$.
$v(C)=(C^{1-\sigma}-1)/(1-\sigma)$ if $\sigma\ne1$, and $v(C)=\log C$ if
$\sigma=1$, so $v'(C)=C^{-\sigma}$. The course normalization is $\chi=1$.
Labor disutility is the integral of each type's disutility; it is not utility
evaluated at the arithmetic mean of hours. The marginal consumption compensation
for type $j$ is $MRS_{jt}=\chi C_t^\sigma N_{jt}^{\varphi}$. Hours are interior
and have no explicit finite endowment bound here. A wage quote constrains the
hours demanded by employers; this does not change labor disutility into utility
from employing workers.
\end{modelcondition}
\end{modelsetup}

\begin{modelsetup}[1]
\begin{modelcondition}{05}{Budget, nominal bonds, and feasibility}
A bond costs $Q_t$ at $t$ and pays one currency unit at $t+1$. $B_t$ is its
face value, $R_t^n=Q_t^{-1}=1+i_t$, and the family budget is
\[
 P_tC_t+Q_tB_t=B_{t-1}+\int W_{jt}N_{jt}dj+D_t.
\]
The initial maturing asset position is given; bonds have zero aggregate net
supply. There are no baseline taxes or transfers. Lump-sum transfers, if
introduced, must have an explicit counterpart; they are not production income.
Each good clears at $Y_{it}=C_{it}$, giving $C_t=Y_t$ in composite units.
The common nominal multiplier is $\lambda_t=\xi_tC_t^{-\sigma}/P_t$.
Asset plans obey no-Ponzi restrictions and a vanishing terminal value,
$\lim_{T\to\infty}E_0\beta^T\lambda_TQ_TB_T=0$.
\end{modelcondition}
\end{modelsetup}

\begin{modelsetup}[1]
\begin{modelcondition}{06}{Who may reset, and which quote survives?}
A product firm retains its nominal price with probability $\theta_p$ and may
reset with probability $1-\theta_p$. A labor type retains its nominal wage
with probability $\theta_w$ and may reset with probability $1-\theta_w$.
Selections are independent across units, dates, ages, and aggregate states;
there is no baseline indexation. The main derivation uses
$0<\theta_p,\theta_w<1$. A price or wage chosen now survives $k$ further
periods with probability $\theta_p^k$ or $\theta_w^k$. Identical resetters choose
common quotes $P_t^{\#}$ and $W_t^{\#}$; nonresetters retain a distribution of
older quotes. Immediate adjustment, $\theta_p=0$ or $\theta_w=0$, will be treated
by its static first-order condition, not by division by zero in a Phillips slope.
\end{modelcondition}
\end{modelsetup}

\begin{modelsetup}[1]
\begin{modelcondition}{07}{Information, choices, and aggregate demand}
At date $t$, current shocks and inherited quotes are observed; eligible firms
and wage setters choose their quotes. Employers choose labor inputs to meet
goods demand, households allocate spending and assets, and markets clear
jointly. $E_t$ is conditional on current information; future innovations are
unknown. Employers' demand determines $N_{jt}$ at a type's posted wage.
Wage setters choose the quote subject to that demand; they do not freely choose
both a wage and an unrelated amount of work. Pooling income makes the same
consumption multiplier relevant to all type-specific wage decisions.
\end{modelcondition}
\end{modelsetup}

\begin{modelsetup}[1]
\begin{modelcondition}{08}{Reference, dispersion, and inherited states}
The deterministic reference has constant productivity, $\bar\xi=1$, symmetric
prices and wages, and zero price and wage inflation. Hats denote log deviations
from that reference; $\bar R^n=1/\beta$. Price dispersion affects aggregate
production, and wage dispersion affects physical hours and labor disutility.
They are retained exactly before linearizing; their first-order deviations
vanish when inherited dispersion is initially zero. The inherited real wage,
however, remains a first-order state when both contracts are sticky. A shock
experiment starts from the stated symmetric reference, including its real wage;
it cannot freely impose an unrelated current wage.
\end{modelcondition}
\end{modelsetup}

\begin{modelsetup}[1]
\begin{modelcondition}{09}{Shocks, policy, and the solution class}
For general definitions, $a_t=\log(A_t/\bar A)$ and
$q_t=\log(\xi_t/\bar\xi)$ can follow stationary AR(1) processes with mean-zero
innovations. The solved experiment sets $a_t=q_t=0$ and uses
$\nu_t=\rho_\nu\nu_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t^\nu$, $0\leq\rho_\nu<1$.
Policy sets the log gross-rate deviation
$j_t=\log(R_t^n/\bar R^n)=\phi_\pi\pi_t^p+\phi_xx_t+\nu_t$, with
$\phi_\pi,\phi_x\geq0$. There is no binding interest-rate lower bound, and money
is omitted. We seek a local bounded equilibrium with convergent wage/pricing
sums and the stated terminal conditions. Root counts are checked for this
larger system; the earlier two-jump-variable proof is not assumed to carry over.
\end{modelcondition}
\end{modelsetup}

\begin{modelsetup}[1]
\begin{modelcondition}{10}{Objectives under surviving nominal contracts}
For $s=t+k$, product firms value nominal profits using the owners' kernel
$\mathcal Q_{t,s}=\beta^k\lambda_s/\lambda_t$ and choose
\[
 \max_{P_t^{\#}}E_t\sum_{k\geq0}\theta_p^k\mathcal Q_{t,s}
            [P_t^{\#}Y_{s|t}-TC_s^n(Y_{s|t})],
 \qquad Y_{s|t}=(P_t^{\#}/P_s)^{-\epsilon_p}Y_s.
\]
Here $TC_s^n(Y)=W_s(Y/A_s)^{1/(1-\alpha)}$. A type's wage setter chooses
its nominal quote using the same family's marginal valuation of income:
\[
 \max_{W_t^{\#}}E_t\sum_{k\geq0}(\beta\theta_w)^k
 [\lambda_sW_t^{\#}n_{s|t}-\xi_s\chi n_{s|t}^{1+\varphi}/(1+\varphi)],
\]
where $n_{s|t}=(W_t^{\#}/W_s)^{-\epsilon_w}N_s$. It takes aggregate family
consumption valuation and employers' type-demand schedule as given. Labor
income gains and work costs are both valued; the objective is the family's
marginal welfare gain from this atomistic labor type. The budget-substitution
and Lagrangian routes below give the same condition.
\end{modelcondition}
\end{modelsetup}

\subsection{Labor demand and the wage index}

\subsubsection{Which workers does the firm hire?}

Fix a date and firm. To acquire composite labor $N_i$, choose its type mix:
\begin{align}
 \min_{\{N_{ij}\}}\int_0^1W_jN_{ij}dj
 &\quad\text{subject to }\mathcal N_i\geq N_i,\\
 \mathcal N_i&=\left[\int N_{ij}^{r_w}dj\right]^{1/r_w},
 \qquad r_w=(\epsilon_w-1)/\epsilon_w,\\
 \mathcal L_i&=\int W_jN_{ij}dj+\Lambda_i(N_i-\mathcal N_i).
\end{align}
The chain rule gives the marginal composite contribution of one type:
\begin{align}
 \frac{\partial\mathcal N_i}{\partial N_{ij}}
 &=\frac1{r_w}\left[\int N_{ih}^{r_w}dh\right]^{1/r_w-1}
                                  r_wN_{ij}^{r_w-1}\\
 &=\mathcal N_i^{1/\epsilon_w}N_{ij}^{-1/\epsilon_w},\\
 0&=W_j-\Lambda_i(N_i/N_{ij})^{1/\epsilon_w},\\
 N_{ij}&=(W_j/\Lambda_i)^{-\epsilon_w}N_i.
\end{align}
Substitute into the binding labor requirement:
\begin{align}
 N_i^{r_w}&=\int[(W_j/\Lambda_i)^{-\epsilon_w}N_i]^{r_w}dj,\\
 1&=\Lambda_i^{\epsilon_w-1}\int W_j^{1-\epsilon_w}dj,\\
 \Lambda_i&=\left[\int W_j^{1-\epsilon_w}dj\right]^{1/(1-\epsilon_w)}=W,\\
 N_{ij}&=(W_j/W)^{-\epsilon_w}N_i,\qquad
 \int W_jN_{ij}dj=N_iW^{\epsilon_w}\int W_j^{1-\epsilon_w}dj=WN_i.
 \label{eq:sw-type-demand}
\end{align}
$W$ is the shadow price of an extra unit of composite labor. If the firm
instead minimizes its bill subject to
$A\mathcal N_i^{1-\alpha}\geq Y_i$, call its output multiplier $MC_i^n$.
Its type-$j$ derivative is
\begin{align}
 0&=W_j-MC_i^n(1-\alpha)A\mathcal N_i^{-\alpha}
                         (\mathcal N_i/N_{ij})^{1/\epsilon_w},\\
 \Lambda_i&=MC_i^n(1-\alpha)AN_i^{-\alpha}=W,\\
 MC_i^n&=\frac{W}{(1-\alpha)AN_i^{-\alpha}}.
\end{align}
Thus the original output-constrained cost problem gives the same type demand,
but its multiplier is output marginal cost, not automatically the wage index.
The two multipliers differ by the marginal product of composite labor.

Sum type demand across firms before interpreting the aggregate:
\begin{align}
 N_{jt}&=\int N_{ijt}di
       =(W_{jt}/W_t)^{-\epsilon_w}\int N_{it}di\\
       &=(W_{jt}/W_t)^{-\epsilon_w}N_t,\\
 \left[\int N_{jt}^{r_w}dj\right]^{1/r_w}
 &=N_t\left[\int(W_{jt}/W_t)^{1-\epsilon_w}dj\right]^{1/r_w}=N_t,\\
 \int W_{jt}N_{jt}dj&=W_tN_t.
\end{align}
A type that raises its relative wage loses employers' demand with elasticity
$\epsilon_w$. This is a labor \emph{demand} curve faced by a wage setter,
not a household labor-supply condition equating its wage to its MRS.

\begin{blueblock}
\textbf{One wage index does not mean one wage quote.}
$W_t$ measures the minimum bill for a unit of composite labor. Individual
$W_{jt}$ can differ because their last reset dates differ. Only under symmetry
do all quotes equal the index. An arithmetic average of nominal wages is not
the CES wage index away from that reference.
\end{blueblock}

\subsubsection{Composite labor, physical hours, and work disutility}

Three different aggregates must be kept apart:
\begin{align}
 H_t&=\int N_{jt}dj
    =N_t\underbrace{\int(W_{jt}/W_t)^{-\epsilon_w}dj}_{\Delta_t^{w,H}},\\
 \int N_{jt}^{1+\varphi}dj
 &=N_t^{1+\varphi}
       \underbrace{\int(W_{jt}/W_t)^{-\epsilon_w(1+\varphi)}dj}_{\Delta_t^{w,U}},\\
 N_t&=(Y_t/A_t)^{1/(1-\alpha)}
       \underbrace{\int(P_{it}/P_t)^{-\epsilon_p/(1-\alpha)}di}_{\Delta_t^p}.
 \label{eq:sw-dispersion}
\end{align}
The family suffers the integral of convex work costs. Concentrating hours
in low-wage types can increase this burden even if the composite labor input
is unchanged. Product-price dispersion separately makes more composite labor
necessary for a given consumption basket. Neither effect is an unemployed-worker
count; there is no participation or matching margin here.

For any wage-dispersion exponent $h>0$, write
$\Delta_t^{w,h}=\int(W_{jt}/W_t)^{-h}dj$. Calvo aggregation implies
\begin{align}
 \Delta_t^{w,h}
 &=(1-\theta_w)(W_t^{\#}/W_t)^{-h}
                  +\theta_w(\Pi_t^w)^h\Delta_{t-1}^{w,h},
 \qquad\Pi_t^w=W_t/W_{t-1}.
\end{align}
The wage-index expansion derived below gives
$\hat w_t^{\#}-\hat w_t=\theta_w\pi_t^w/(1-\theta_w)$. Therefore,
\begin{align}
 \hat\Delta_t^{w,h}
 &=-(1-\theta_w)h(\hat w_t^{\#}-\hat w_t)
             +\theta_wh\pi_t^w+\theta_w\hat\Delta_{t-1}^{w,h}\\
 &=-\theta_wh\pi_t^w+\theta_wh\pi_t^w+\theta_w\hat\Delta_{t-1}^{w,h}
 =\theta_w\hat\Delta_{t-1}^{w,h}.
\end{align}
With initially symmetric quotes, these first-order dispersion terms vanish.
Locally, $\hat h_t=\hat n_t$ and
$\hat y_t=a_t+(1-\alpha)\hat n_t$. At second order, especially for welfare,
the different dispersion moments cannot be dropped merely because this
first-order cancellation occurred.

\subsection{Household spending and wage-setting incentives}

\subsubsection{The common consumption valuation}

Use resources minus expenditures in the family Lagrangian:
\begin{align}
 \mathcal L=E_0\sum_{t\geq0}\beta^t\Big\{&\xi_t
  [v(C_t)-\chi\textstyle\int N_{jt}^{1+\varphi}dj/(1+\varphi)]\notag\\
 &+\lambda_t[B_{t-1}+\textstyle\int W_{jt}N_{jt}dj+D_t
                                      -P_tC_t-Q_tB_t]\Big\}.
\end{align}
The consumption and asset derivatives give
\begin{align}
 0&=\xi_tC_t^{-\sigma}-\lambda_tP_t,
 &\lambda_t&=\xi_tC_t^{-\sigma}/P_t,\\
 0&=-\beta^t\lambda_tQ_t+\beta^{t+1}E_t\lambda_{t+1},\\
 Q_t&=\beta E_t\left[\frac{\xi_{t+1}}{\xi_t}
             (C_{t+1}/C_t)^{-\sigma}\frac{P_t}{P_{t+1}}\right].
 \label{eq:sw-euler}
\end{align}
Income pooling makes $\lambda_t$ common across wage setters even though their
hours differ. Their compensation for an extra hour is type-specific:
$MRS_{jt}=\chi C_t^\sigma N_{jt}^\varphi$. This is why replacing every type's
hours by the family average before differentiating would change the problem.

Profits and wages together finance consumption:
\begin{align}
 D_t&=\int P_{it}Y_{it}di-W_tN_t=P_tY_t-W_tN_t,\\
 P_tC_t&=W_tN_t+D_t=P_tY_t
                  \quad\text{in aggregate, with net bonds zero}.
\end{align}
The budget must include profits; monopolistic competition does not make them
vanish.

\subsubsection{A flexible wage already carries a markup}

Consider an eligible wage setter at a date when it can choose a new quote.
First ignore future survival. Conditional demand and its derivative are
\begin{align}
 N_j&=(W_j/W)^{-\epsilon_w}N,
 &\frac{dN_j}{dW_j}&=-\epsilon_wN_j/W_j,\\
 \frac{d(W_jN_j)}{dW_j}
 &=N_j+W_j(-\epsilon_wN_j/W_j)=(1-\epsilon_w)N_j.
\end{align}
A higher quote raises income per hour but loses enough hours to reduce the
wage bill along this elastic demand curve. The associated utility derivative
per unit mass of type-$j$ members is
\begin{align}
 0&=\lambda(1-\epsilon_w)N_j
           -\xi\chi N_j^\varphi(-\epsilon_wN_j/W_j),\\
 (\epsilon_w-1)\lambda W_jN_j
 &=\epsilon_w\xi\chi N_j^{1+\varphi},\\
 W_j&=\frac{\epsilon_w}{\epsilon_w-1}
                        \frac{\xi\chi N_j^\varphi}{\lambda},\\
 \frac{W_j}{P}&=\mu_w\chi C^\sigma N_j^\varphi=\mu_w MRS_j.
 \label{eq:sw-flex-wage}
\end{align}
The gross wage markup $\mu_w>1$ is distinct from delayed adjustment. Flexible
wages in this differentiated-labor economy do not give the competitive
condition $W/P=MRS$ unless the wage markup is removed by an additional change.

\subsubsection{Budget substitution and the surviving wage quote}

Now a reset wage $W_t^{\#}$ survives through $s=t+k$ with probability
$\theta_w^k$. Conditional hours are
\begin{align}
 n_{s|t}&=(W_t^{\#}/W_s)^{-\epsilon_w}N_s,\qquad
 \frac{\partial n_{s|t}}{\partial W_t^{\#}}
                  =-\epsilon_wn_{s|t}/W_t^{\#}.
\end{align}
The course substitutes the budget before differentiating. Keep that useful
route, with the family interpretation explicit. For a small group of types
of mass $dj$, isolate the wage-dependent part of its family's budget:
\begin{align}
 C_s(W_t^{\#})
 &=\frac{W_t^{\#}n_{s|t}\,dj+\mathcal R_s}{P_s},\\
 \mathcal R_s&=B_{s-1}+D_s+\text{other types' wage income}-Q_sB_s,\\
 \left.\frac{\partial C_s}{\partial W_t^{\#}}\right|_{\mathcal R_s}
 &=\frac{n_{s|t}+W_t^{\#}(-\epsilon_wn_{s|t}/W_t^{\#})}{P_s}\,dj\\
 &=\frac{(1-\epsilon_w)n_{s|t}}{P_s}\,dj.
\end{align}
The group's contribution to labor disutility is
$\chi n_{s|t}^{1+\varphi}dj/(1+\varphi)$. Differentiate consumption utility
and work disutility separately, then divide by $dj$:
\begin{align}
 \frac1{dj}\frac{\partial U_s}{\partial W_t^{\#}}
 &=\xi_s C_s^{-\sigma}\frac{(1-\epsilon_w)n_{s|t}}{P_s}
       -\xi_s\chi n_{s|t}^\varphi(-\epsilon_wn_{s|t}/W_t^{\#})\\
 &=n_{s|t}\left[(1-\epsilon_w)\lambda_s
           +\frac{\epsilon_w\xi_s\chi n_{s|t}^{\varphi}}{W_t^{\#}}\right].
\end{align}
This is a marginal change in insured family consumption, not a claim that
an individual's own wage bill must equal its own consumption at every date.
Optimal asset choices do not add a missing income derivative: their two
adjacent budget contributions have coefficient
$-\lambda_sQ_s+\beta E_s\lambda_{s+1}=0$ by \eqref{eq:sw-euler}; admissible
terminal asset terms vanish. Equivalently, wage setting can be written directly
in consumption-value units, taking the common multiplier as given:
\begin{align}
 \max_{W_t^{\#}}E_t\sum_{k\geq0}(\beta\theta_w)^k
 \left[\lambda_sW_t^{\#}n_{s|t}
                   -\frac{\xi_s\chi}{1+\varphi}n_{s|t}^{1+\varphi}\right].
 \label{eq:sw-wage-objective}
\end{align}
The survival factor weights the wage-dependent period gain alongside time discounting.
Future innovations remain inside the conditional expectation. After a reset,
future wage decisions have the same continuation problem and the family's
valuation of wealth is already represented by $\lambda_s$.

Set the discounted derivative to zero and rearrange each factor:
\begin{align}
 0&=E_t\sum_{k\geq0}(\beta\theta_w)^k n_{s|t}
       [(1-\epsilon_w)\lambda_s
             +\epsilon_w\xi_s\chi n_{s|t}^{\varphi}/W_t^{\#}],\\
 (\epsilon_w-1)W_t^{\#}E_t\sum_k(\beta\theta_w)^k\lambda_sn_{s|t}
 &=\epsilon_w E_t\sum_k(\beta\theta_w)^k\xi_s\chi n_{s|t}^{1+\varphi}.
\end{align}
\begin{align}
 W_t^{\#}
 &=\mu_w\frac{E_t\sum_k(\beta\theta_w)^k\xi_s\chi n_{s|t}^{1+\varphi}}
                 {E_t\sum_k(\beta\theta_w)^k\lambda_sn_{s|t}}\\
 &=\mu_w\frac{E_t\sum_k(\beta\theta_w)^k\lambda_sn_{s|t}P_sMRS_{s|t}}
                 {E_t\sum_k(\beta\theta_w)^k\lambda_sn_{s|t}},
 \label{eq:sw-reset-ratio}\\
 MRS_{s|t}&=\chi C_s^\sigma n_{s|t}^{\varphi}.
\end{align}
The MRS replaces the ratio of marginal disutility to consumption marginal
utility. It does not multiply marginal disutility a second time. At time zero,
this same equation reads
\begin{align}
 W_0^{\#}
 &=\mu_w\frac{E_0\sum_{s\geq0}(\beta\theta_w)^s
                     \lambda_sn_{s|0}P_sMRS_{s|0}}
                 {E_0\sum_{s\geq0}(\beta\theta_w)^s\lambda_sn_{s|0}}.
\end{align}
For $\theta_w=0$ only the current term remains, yielding
\eqref{eq:sw-flex-wage}. For positive duration, the chosen quote reflects
future work burdens and income valuation, not just today's real compensation.

\begin{redblock}
\textbf{Which variable is actually chosen?}
A wage setter may choose its wage and let the labor demand curve determine
hours. It could equivalently choose a point on the inverse labor demand curve
and quote the corresponding wage. It cannot vary wages and hours independently.
When differentiating, include both the lost hours in wage income and the
reduction in labor disutility.
\end{redblock}

\subsubsection{The nonlinear reset equation and own-hours feedback}

Insert the demand powers before canceling common reset-wage factors:
\begin{align}
 \lambda_sn_{s|t}
 &=(W_t^{\#})^{-\epsilon_w}\lambda_sW_s^{\epsilon_w}N_s,\\
 \xi_s\chi n_{s|t}^{1+\varphi}
 &=\xi_s\chi(W_t^{\#})^{-\epsilon_w(1+\varphi)}
                         W_s^{\epsilon_w(1+\varphi)}N_s^{1+\varphi},\\
 (W_t^{\#})^{1+\epsilon_w\varphi}
 &=\mu_w\chi\frac{E_t\sum_k(\beta\theta_w)^k
                \xi_sW_s^{\epsilon_w(1+\varphi)}N_s^{1+\varphi}}
              {E_t\sum_k(\beta\theta_w)^k\lambda_sW_s^{\epsilon_w}N_s}.
 \label{eq:sw-reset-nonlinear}
\end{align}
Here an individual wage really does alter that type's own work and MRS. The
factor $1+\epsilon_w\varphi$ therefore has a microeconomic source in this
labor-market specification. It is not obtained by treating an individual
product firm's employment as aggregate household hours in the previous
competitive-labor model.

\subsection{From wage resetting to wage inflation}

\subsubsection{Expand both sides before forming a wage Phillips curve}

Use $\hat w_t=\log(W_t/\bar W)$ for nominal wage-index deviations and
$\hat w_t^{\#}=\log(W_t^{\#}/\bar W)$ for reset-wage deviations. At the
symmetric reference,
\begin{align}
 \bar W&=\mu_w\bar P\chi\bar C^\sigma\bar N^\varphi,\qquad
 \bar\lambda=\bar C^{-\sigma}/\bar P,\\
 S_w&=\sum_{k\geq0}(\beta\theta_w)^k=1/(1-\beta\theta_w).
\end{align}
Write the reset FOC as a product equality using \eqref{eq:sw-reset-ratio}:
\begin{align}
 W_t^{\#}E_t\sum_k(\beta\theta_w)^k\lambda_sn_{s|t}
 &=\mu_w E_t\sum_k(\beta\theta_w)^k
                         \lambda_sn_{s|t}P_sMRS_{s|t}.
\end{align}
The relevant individual first-order deviations are
\begin{align}
 \hat\lambda_s&=q_s-\sigma\hat c_s-\hat p_s,\\
 \hat n_{s|t}&=\hat n_s-\epsilon_w(\hat w_t^{\#}-\hat w_s),\\
 \hat{MRS}_{s|t}&=\sigma\hat c_s+\varphi\hat n_{s|t}.
\end{align}
Here $\hat{MRS}$ means the log deviation of the type-specific MRS. Dividing
both sides by $\bar W\bar\lambda\bar N$, their Taylor expansions are
\begin{align}
 L&\simeq S_w+S_w\hat w_t^{\#}
       +E_t\sum_k(\beta\theta_w)^k(\hat\lambda_s+\hat n_{s|t}),\\
 R&\simeq S_w+E_t\sum_k(\beta\theta_w)^k
       (\hat\lambda_s+\hat n_{s|t}+\hat p_s+\hat{MRS}_{s|t}).
\end{align}
The steady terms match because $\bar W=\mu_w\bar P\overline{MRS}$.
Cancel the common variations explicitly:
\begin{align}
 S_w\hat w_t^{\#}
 &=E_t\sum_k(\beta\theta_w)^k(\hat p_s+\hat{MRS}_{s|t}),\\
 \hat w_t^{\#}
 &=(1-\beta\theta_w)E_t\sum_k(\beta\theta_w)^k
       [\hat p_s+\sigma\hat c_s+\varphi\hat n_s
                        -\epsilon_w\varphi(\hat w_t^{\#}-\hat w_s)],\\
 (1+\epsilon_w\varphi)\hat w_t^{\#}
 &=(1-\beta\theta_w)E_t\sum_k(\beta\theta_w)^k
       [\hat p_s+\sigma\hat c_s+\varphi\hat n_s+\epsilon_w\varphi\hat w_s].
\end{align}
Define the wage-markup deviation and its own-hours correction:
\begin{align}
 \hat\mu_t^w&=(\hat w_t-\hat p_t)-\sigma\hat c_t-\varphi\hat n_t,
 \qquad\Omega_w=\frac1{1+\epsilon_w\varphi},\\
 \hat p_s+\sigma\hat c_s+\varphi\hat n_s+\epsilon_w\varphi\hat w_s
 &=(1+\epsilon_w\varphi)\hat w_s-\hat\mu_s^w,\\
 \hat w_t^{\#}&=(1-\beta\theta_w)E_t\sum_{k\geq0}(\beta\theta_w)^k
                                  [\hat w_{t+k}-\Omega_w\hat\mu_{t+k}^w].
 \label{eq:sw-reset-linear}
\end{align}
The wage-markup deviation is relative to the desired markup $\mu_w$; it is not
the gross wage/MRS ratio itself. A positive deviation means compensation is
high relative to the family's current work trade-off, so a resetter wants a
lower quote, other conditions equal.

Separate the first date and reindex the remaining expected sum:
\begin{align}
 \hat w_t^{\#}
 &=(1-\beta\theta_w)(\hat w_t-\Omega_w\hat\mu_t^w)\notag\\
 &\quad+\beta\theta_w E_t\left[(1-\beta\theta_w)E_{t+1}
        \sum_{h\geq0}(\beta\theta_w)^h
                  (\hat w_{t+1+h}-\Omega_w\hat\mu_{t+1+h}^w)\right]\\
 &=(1-\beta\theta_w)(\hat w_t-\Omega_w\hat\mu_t^w)
                          +\beta\theta_wE_t\hat w_{t+1}^{\#}.
 \label{eq:sw-reset-recursion}
\end{align}
Iterated expectations justify the inner-to-outer forecast step. Future random
workloads do not become known merely because a wage quote spans many dates.

\subsubsection{The wage index combines new and inherited contracts}

Calvo selection preserves the previous wage distribution among nonresetters:
\begin{align}
 W_t^{1-\epsilon_w}
 &=(1-\theta_w)(W_t^{\#})^{1-\epsilon_w}
                              +\theta_wW_{t-1}^{1-\epsilon_w}.
\end{align}
Divide by the symmetric reference and expand each power at one:
\begin{align}
 1+(1-\epsilon_w)\hat w_t
 &=(1-\theta_w)[1+(1-\epsilon_w)\hat w_t^{\#}]
               +\theta_w[1+(1-\epsilon_w)\hat w_{t-1}],\\
 \hat w_t&=(1-\theta_w)\hat w_t^{\#}+\theta_w\hat w_{t-1},\\
 \pi_t^w&=\hat w_t-\hat w_{t-1},\\
 r_t^{w,\#}\equiv\hat w_t^{\#}-\hat w_t
 &=\frac{\hat w_t-\theta_w\hat w_{t-1}}{1-\theta_w}-\hat w_t
 =\frac{\theta_w}{1-\theta_w}\pi_t^w.
\end{align}
Subtract current $\hat w_t$ from \eqref{eq:sw-reset-recursion}:
\begin{align}
 r_t^{w,\#}
 &=-(1-\beta\theta_w)\Omega_w\hat\mu_t^w
        +\beta\theta_w E_t(\hat w_{t+1}^{\#}-\hat w_t)\\
 &=-(1-\beta\theta_w)\Omega_w\hat\mu_t^w
        +\beta\theta_w E_t(r_{t+1}^{w,\#}+\pi_{t+1}^w),\\
 \frac{\theta_w}{1-\theta_w}\pi_t^w
 &=-(1-\beta\theta_w)\Omega_w\hat\mu_t^w
            +\frac{\beta\theta_w}{1-\theta_w}E_t\pi_{t+1}^w,\\
 \pi_t^w&=\beta E_t\pi_{t+1}^w-\lambda_w\hat\mu_t^w,\\
 \lambda_w&=\frac{(1-\theta_w)(1-\beta\theta_w)}
                   {\theta_w(1+\epsilon_w\varphi)}>0.
 \label{eq:sw-wage-pc}
\end{align}
An above-target wage markup encourages lower new wages. Wage inflation falls
as those quotes enter the index. Expectations of future conditions also matter
because a reset wage may still apply then.

\begin{blueblock}
\textbf{Nominal wage rigidity does not fix purchasing power.}
Let $\omega_t=\hat w_t-\hat p_t$ denote the real wage's log deviation. Then
\[
 \omega_t-\omega_{t-1}=\pi_t^w-\pi_t^p.
\]
Even if a particular nominal wage is unchanged, its real value falls when
product prices rise. In the aggregate, both inflation rates and their difference
must be tracked. Price inflation alone does not describe wage adjustment.
\end{blueblock}

\subsection{Natural output, real wages, and two markup gaps}

\subsubsection{The reference allocation when both quotes are flexible}

Under symmetric flexible prices and wages, $C=Y$, $N=(Y/A)^{1/(1-\alpha)}$,
and the two markup conditions are
\begin{align}
 \frac WP&=\mu_w\chi Y^\sigma N^\varphi,\\
 \frac WP&=\frac{1-\alpha}{\mu_p}AN^{-\alpha}.
\end{align}
Equate the two expressions, then substitute the labor requirement:
\begin{align}
 \mu_p\mu_w\chi Y^\sigma N^{\varphi+\alpha}&=(1-\alpha)A,\\
 (Y_t^n)^\gamma
 &=\frac{1-\alpha}{\mu_p\mu_w\chi}
             A_t^{(1+\varphi)/(1-\alpha)},\\
 \gamma&=\sigma+\frac{\varphi+\alpha}{1-\alpha},\\
 \gamma\log Y_t^n
 &=\log(1-\alpha)-\log\chi-\log\mu_p-\log\mu_w
                        +\frac{1+\varphi}{1-\alpha}\log A_t,\\
 \hat y_t^n&=\psi_a a_t,\qquad
 \psi_a=\frac{1+\varphi}{\sigma(1-\alpha)+\varphi+\alpha},\\
 \hat n_t^n&=\frac{\hat y_t^n-a_t}{1-\alpha}.
\end{align}
The constant wage markup shifts the \emph{level} of natural output even when
its response coefficient to technology matches the previous chapter. Flexible
wages here still have market power; the previous competitive-wage baseline
has no $\mu_w$ wedge. Comparisons of local responses below use deviations
from each specification's own natural reference.

The natural real wage follows either flexible condition:
\begin{align}
 \omega_t^n&=a_t-\alpha\hat n_t^n
            =\frac{1-\alpha\psi_a}{1-\alpha}a_t\\
            &=\sigma\hat y_t^n+\varphi\hat n_t^n.
 \label{eq:sw-natural-real-wage}
\end{align}
Its desired change need not be zero after a productivity disturbance. Monetary
policy then faces a moving wage as well as a moving output benchmark.

For the efficient goods-and-work allocation, a planner values an extra unit of
composite labor by its output contribution. At symmetry, solve
\begin{align}
 \max_{N_t>0}\ \xi_t[v(A_tN_t^{1-\alpha})-\chi N_t^{1+\varphi}/(1+\varphi)].
\end{align}
Differentiating the consumption term by the chain rule gives
\begin{align}
 0&=\xi_t[v'(Y_t^e)(1-\alpha)A_t(N_t^e)^{-\alpha}-\chi(N_t^e)^\varphi],\\
 (Y_t^e)^{-\sigma}(1-\alpha)A_t(N_t^e)^{-\alpha}
 &=\chi(N_t^e)^\varphi.
\end{align}
Compared with the natural allocation, both market-power wedges disappear:
\begin{align}
 \gamma(\log Y_t^e-\log Y_t^n)&=\log\mu_p+\log\mu_w,\\
 \log Y_t^e-\log Y_t^n&=\frac{\log\mu_p+\log\mu_w}{\gamma}.
\end{align}
This compares the goods-and-work allocations, not an optimum under unspecified
subsidies. Constant markup distortions and costly fluctuations are separate
sources of welfare loss.

\subsubsection{How the real wage connects demand, costs, and work incentives}

Define the natural output gap and the real-wage gap:
\begin{align}
 x_t&=\hat y_t-\hat y_t^n,\qquad
 g_t=\omega_t-\omega_t^n,\\
 \hat n_t-\hat n_t^n&=x_t/(1-\alpha).
\end{align}
Average real marginal-cost deviation satisfies
\begin{align}
 \hat{mc}_t&=\omega_t-a_t+\alpha\hat n_t,\\
 0&=\omega_t^n-a_t+\alpha\hat n_t^n,\\
 \hat{mc}_t&=g_t+\frac\alpha{1-\alpha}x_t
            =g_t+\gamma_p x_t,\qquad\gamma_p=\frac\alpha{1-\alpha}.
\end{align}
The wage-markup deviation follows from the family's work trade-off:
\begin{align}
 \hat\mu_t^w&=\omega_t-\sigma\hat y_t-\varphi\hat n_t,\\
 0&=\omega_t^n-\sigma\hat y_t^n-\varphi\hat n_t^n,\\
 \hat\mu_t^w&=g_t-\left[\sigma+\frac\varphi{1-\alpha}\right]x_t
             =g_t-\gamma_wx_t,\\
 \gamma_w&=\sigma+\frac\varphi{1-\alpha},\qquad
 \gamma_p+\gamma_w=\gamma.
 \label{eq:sw-markup-gaps}
\end{align}
At a given output gap, a larger real wage raises firms' costs but also raises
the wage relative to households' MRS. Price resetters then want higher prices,
while wage resetters want lower wages relative to their inherited quotes.
This is an adjustment mechanism, not an automatic spiral in which every wage
increase causes an indefinitely repeated price increase.

\subsection{The joint price-and-wage equilibrium}

\subsubsection{Four relations beyond the policy rule}

The product-pricing derivation retains the own-output cost correction from
the previous chapter. Define
\begin{align}
 a_p&=\frac{(1-\theta_p)(1-\beta\theta_p)}{\theta_p}
                  \frac{1-\alpha}{1-\alpha+\epsilon_p\alpha},\qquad
 a_w=\lambda_w.
\end{align}
Then the local model is
\begin{align}
 \pi_t^p&=\beta E_t\pi_{t+1}^p+a_p(\gamma_p x_t+g_t),
 \label{eq:sw-price-system}\\
 \pi_t^w&=\beta E_t\pi_{t+1}^w+a_w(\gamma_w x_t-g_t),
 \label{eq:sw-wage-system}\\
 x_t&=E_tx_{t+1}-\frac1\sigma(j_t-E_t\pi_{t+1}^p-r_t^n),
 \label{eq:sw-is-system}\\
 g_t&=g_{t-1}+\pi_t^w-\pi_t^p-(\omega_t^n-\omega_{t-1}^n),
 \label{eq:sw-gap-law}\\
 j_t&=\phi_\pi\pi_t^p+\phi_xx_t+\nu_t.
 \label{eq:sw-policy-system}
\end{align}
The real-wage law follows by subtracting changes in the natural wage from
$\omega_t-\omega_{t-1}=\pi_t^w-\pi_t^p$. The natural real-rate deviation is
\begin{align}
 r_t^n&=\sigma(E_t\hat y_{t+1}^n-\hat y_t^n)+q_t-E_tq_{t+1}.
\end{align}
The state $g_{t-1}$ is inherited. The current gap, inflation rates, and real
wage are jointly determined; their expectations cannot be chosen separately.
Here $a_p,a_w$ are positive slope coefficients, not productivity shocks $a_t$.

\begin{greenblock}
\textbf{A reason to track wages in a demand contraction.}
When purchases fall, the desired work compensation falls with consumption and
hours. An inherited wage may then be high relative to that MRS, even when its
real value has fallen relative to its own pre-shock level. Firms respond to
real labor cost; wage setters respond to the wage markup. Those comparisons
can move differently and should not be merged into one ``rigid wage'' label.
\end{greenblock}

\subsubsection{What the flexible limits actually impose}

If wages can reset every date, impose the static wage condition
$\hat\mu_t^w=0$. Then
\begin{align}
 g_t&=\gamma_wx_t,\\
 \pi_t^p&=\beta E_t\pi_{t+1}^p
              +a_p(\gamma_p+\gamma_w)x_t
        =\beta E_t\pi_{t+1}^p+a_p\gamma x_t.
\end{align}
This is the price-only NK gap equation. The constant wage-market markup still
shifts its reference level, as explained above. If product prices are instead
flexible, impose $\hat{mc}_t=0$, giving
\begin{align}
 g_t&=-\gamma_p x_t,\\
 \pi_t^w&=\beta E_t\pi_{t+1}^w
                  +a_w(\gamma_w+\gamma_p)x_t.
\end{align}
The real-wage law still links wage and price inflation; simply relabeling this
wage equation a price Phillips curve would omit that link. If both quotes
are flexible, the two conditions jointly imply
\begin{align}
 \gamma_wx_t&=-\gamma_px_t,\qquad
 \gamma x_t=0,\qquad x_t=g_t=0.
\end{align}
With no additional real distortion shocks, monetary disturbances then do not
move output away from its natural allocation. Nominal variables can still
adjust. The limit is taken through static optimality, not through an infinite
coefficient inserted into a finite-slope Phillips curve.

\subsubsection{Why stabilizing both inflation rates is demanding}

Suppose policy tried to keep both $\pi_t^p=0$ and $\pi_t^w=0$ at all dates
with the same zero future forecasts. The two pricing relations require
\begin{align}
 0&=\gamma_px_t+g_t,\qquad0=\gamma_wx_t-g_t,\\
 0&=(\gamma_p+\gamma_w)x_t,\qquad x_t=g_t=0.
\end{align}
But the real-wage law would then require
\begin{align}
 0&=0+0-0-(\omega_t^n-\omega_{t-1}^n),\\
 \omega_t^n-\omega_{t-1}^n&=0.
\end{align}
If the natural real wage needs to change, keeping both nominal inflation rates
at zero prevents that change. At least one target must give way. This is a
conditional incompatibility result, not a complete optimal-policy calculation.
To evaluate welfare, one must include the output wedge and both types of
relative-contract dispersion, with explicit subsidies and welfare weights.
The policy part develops that comparison.

\subsection{Solving a policy disturbance with an inherited real wage}

\subsubsection{The state and the three current choices}

Set technology and the preference shifter at their reference so
$r_t^n=\omega_t^n=0$. Let
$\nu_t=\rho_\nu\nu_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t^\nu$.
We solve for three current choices and the current state transition:
\begin{align}
 x_t&=f_xg_{t-1}+h_x\nu_t,\\
 \pi_t^p&=f_pg_{t-1}+h_p\nu_t,\\
 \pi_t^w&=f_wg_{t-1}+h_w\nu_t,\\
 g_t&=Gg_{t-1}+J\nu_t.
 \label{eq:sw-linear-policies}
\end{align}
The real-wage identity immediately imposes
\begin{align}
 G&=1+f_w-f_p,\qquad J=h_w-h_p.
\end{align}
Forecasting next period uses current $g_t$, not last period's state again:
\begin{align}
 E_tx_{t+1}&=f_xg_t+\rho_\nu h_x\nu_t,\\
 E_t\pi_{t+1}^p&=f_pg_t+\rho_\nu h_p\nu_t,\\
 E_t\pi_{t+1}^w&=f_wg_t+\rho_\nu h_w\nu_t.
\end{align}
This extra inherited wage position is why the earlier solution proportional
only to the current monetary shock is no longer sufficient.

\subsubsection{Find the endogenous state root}

First match the coefficients on inherited $g_{t-1}$ in dynamic IS, price
setting, and wage setting. Substitution gives
\begin{align}
 (\sigma+\phi_x)f_x+\phi_\pi f_p&=(\sigma f_x+f_p)G,\\
 (1-\beta G)f_p&=a_p\gamma_pf_x+a_pG,\\
 (1-\beta G)f_w&=a_w\gamma_wf_x-a_wG.
\end{align}
Use the second equation in the first, multiplying by $1-\beta G$:
\begin{align}
 [(\sigma+\phi_x)-\sigma G]f_x+(\phi_\pi-G)f_p&=0,\\
 \mathcal D(G)f_x&=-a_pG(\phi_\pi-G),\\
 \mathcal D(G)
 &=[\sigma+\phi_x-\sigma G](1-\beta G)
                         +a_p\gamma_p(\phi_\pi-G),\\
 f_x&=-\frac{a_pG(\phi_\pi-G)}{\mathcal D(G)}.
\end{align}
For a stable candidate $|G|<1$, $1-\beta G\ne0$; any other division also
requires its displayed denominator to be nonzero. Next subtract the price
coefficient from the wage coefficient and use $f_w-f_p=G-1$:
\begin{align}
 (1-\beta G)(G-1)
 &=(a_w\gamma_w-a_p\gamma_p)f_x-(a_w+a_p)G,\\
 \mathcal L(G)&=(G-1)(1-\beta G)+(a_w+a_p)G,\\
 E&=a_w\gamma_w-a_p\gamma_p,\qquad
 \mathcal L(G)=E f_x,\\
 \mathcal L(G)\mathcal D(G)+Ea_pG(\phi_\pi-G)&=0.
 \label{eq:sw-state-polynomial}
\end{align}
This is a fourth-degree equation. Its coefficients can be computed without
hiding the polynomial expansion. Write
\begin{align}
 \mathcal D(G)&=d_2G^2+d_1G+d_0,\\
 d_2&=\sigma\beta,\\
 d_1&=-\beta(\sigma+\phi_x)-\sigma-a_p\gamma_p,\\
 d_0&=\sigma+\phi_x+a_p\gamma_p\phi_\pi,\\
 \mathcal L(G)&=-\beta G^2+\ell_1G-1,\qquad
 \ell_1=1+\beta+a_p+a_w.
\end{align}
Multiply the two quadratics and the remaining term to get
\begin{align}
 0&=c_4G^4+c_3G^3+c_2G^2+c_1G+c_0,\\
 c_4&=-\beta d_2,\\
 c_3&=-\beta d_1+\ell_1d_2,\\
 c_2&=-\beta d_0+\ell_1d_1-d_2-Ea_p,\\
 c_1&=\ell_1d_0-d_1+Ea_p\phi_\pi,\\
 c_0&=-d_0.
\end{align}
Select the admissible stable root and recover
\begin{align}
 f_p&=\frac{a_p(\gamma_pf_x+G)}{1-\beta G},\qquad
 f_w=\frac{a_w(\gamma_wf_x-G)}{1-\beta G}.
\end{align}
There is one inherited endogenous state and three current jump variables.
A unique local bounded solution requires one stable and three unstable roots
of the forward system, with the state projection well defined. The parameter
example below passes that count; it is not asserted for arbitrary rules.
A candidate generated by denominator multiplication must also pass the
unreduced coefficient equations above.

\subsubsection{Solve the shock coefficients}

Match the coefficients on $\nu_t$ using the same forecasts:
\begin{align}
 [\sigma(1-\rho_\nu)+\phi_x]h_x
      +(\phi_\pi-\rho_\nu)h_p-(\sigma f_x+f_p)J&=-1,\\
 (1-\beta\rho_\nu)h_p&=a_p\gamma_ph_x+(\beta f_p+a_p)J,\\
 (1-\beta\rho_\nu)h_w&=a_w\gamma_wh_x+(\beta f_w-a_w)J,\\
 h_w-h_p&=J.
\end{align}
Subtract the second equation from the third, then substitute the last:
\begin{align}
 (1-\beta\rho_\nu)J
 &=E h_x+[\beta(f_w-f_p)-(a_w+a_p)]J,\\
 K_\nu J&=E h_x,\\
 K_\nu&=1-\beta\rho_\nu-\beta(G-1)+a_w+a_p,\\
 L_\nu&=E/K_\nu,\qquad J=L_\nu h_x.
\end{align}
Use this result to obtain the two inflation coefficients:
\begin{align}
 P_\nu&=\frac{a_p\gamma_p+(\beta f_p+a_p)L_\nu}{1-\beta\rho_\nu},\\
 h_p&=P_\nu h_x,\qquad h_w=(P_\nu+L_\nu)h_x.
\end{align}
Finally, the IS coefficient equation becomes a scalar equation:
\begin{align}
 \big[\sigma(1-\rho_\nu)+\phi_x
       +(\phi_\pi-\rho_\nu)P_\nu
       -(\sigma f_x+f_p)L_\nu\big]h_x&=-1,\\
 h_x&=-\frac1{\sigma(1-\rho_\nu)+\phi_x
       +(\phi_\pi-\rho_\nu)P_\nu-(\sigma f_x+f_p)L_\nu}.
 \label{eq:sw-shock-coefficients}
\end{align}
All coefficients are now determined. If a displayed denominator vanishes,
this derivation must be reconsidered rather than assigning a numerical value
to an undefined expression.

\subsubsection{A joint response and a comparison of contracts}

Use illustrative quarterly parameters
\begin{align}
 \beta&=.99,\quad\sigma=\varphi=1,\quad\alpha=1/3,
 \quad\epsilon_p=\epsilon_w=6,\\
 \theta_p&=\theta_w=.75,\qquad\phi_\pi=1.5,\quad\phi_x=.125,
 \quad\rho_\nu=.5.
\end{align}
Starting from $g_{-1}=\nu_{-1}=0$, apply one innovation
$\varepsilon_0^\nu=.001$ and set later innovations to zero. Each date is
computed in the following order:
\begin{align}
 \nu_t&=\rho_\nu\nu_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t^\nu,\\
 (x_t,\pi_t^p,\pi_t^w)&=(f_x,f_p,f_w)g_{t-1}+(h_x,h_p,h_w)\nu_t,\\
 g_t&=g_{t-1}+\pi_t^w-\pi_t^p,\\
 j_t&=\phi_\pi\pi_t^p+\phi_xx_t+\nu_t,\\
 r_t&=j_t-[f_pg_t+\rho_\nu h_p\nu_t],\\
 \hat\mu_t^w&=g_t-\gamma_wx_t.
\end{align}
The real-rate response subtracts the actual conditional inflation forecast;
it does not presume price inflation stays unchanged. The response values and

The large initial wage change in the price-only case is possible because that
contract is flexible; its wage markup remains at target. With sticky wages,
the wage markup absorbs more of the adjustment. Later movements can partly
reverse the initial change. In particular, persistent inherited real-wage
positions need not produce a one-signed output response at every horizon.
Even with $\rho_\nu=0$, the both-contract model has a nonzero next-period
response through $g_0$; the contract state and the shock's own persistence are
separate sources of memory.


A drop in the real wage is compatible with an above-target wage markup when
the MRS falls further. Likewise, adding a second sticky contract does not
mechanically add a fixed percentage to the output response. It changes the
joint solution for forecasts, costs, and the inherited real-wage position.
The comparison re-solves that equilibrium for every variant; it does not hold
behavioral coefficients fixed while changing contract duration.

\subsection{What wage rigidity explains, and what it does not}

Consider three hypothetical observations. A supplier cuts shifts while a
nominal pay schedule remains unchanged: an inherited contract can constrain
cost adjustment. Consumer prices rise before the next wage review: an unchanged
nominal wage can imply a lower real wage. Employment recovers slowly after
orders improve: wage setting may contribute, but search, hiring costs, skills,
and financing may also matter. The first two observations map directly to
this chapter's quantities; the third cannot be assigned to the wage contract
without further evidence.

The model has no direct count of unemployed people, vacancies, quits, or labor
force participation. Its labor types supply the hours employers demand under
the specified wage schedule; it has not modeled the process of finding a job.
Nor does symmetric Calvo adjustment capture a special prohibition on wage
cuts, institutional bargaining details, or every kind of annual agreement.
Those are distinct extensions. Lower wage flexibility also need not improve
or worsen every policy outcome independently of price flexibility and the
policy rule.

\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\begin{blueblock}
\textbf{A real wage, a wage markup, and unemployment are different objects.}
The real wage compares nominal compensation with goods prices. The wage markup
compares that compensation with the marginal work trade-off. Unemployment
compares workers seeking jobs with jobs actually obtained under a specified
labor-market mechanism. One cannot infer the last object merely by plotting
one of the first two.
\end{blueblock}

We now have a common framework containing real disturbances, staggered product
prices, and staggered wages. The labor-market chapter marks the next mechanism
needed for employment and unemployment. The synthesis at the end of this part
compares what the completed models predict before moving on to international
and financial transmission.
