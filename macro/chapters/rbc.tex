\subsection{From a growth path to changing opportunities}

\subsubsection{What would make a Ramsey household change its working week?}

Imagine a group of factories. Their machines have not changed overnight, but
an interruption in power supply now makes each working shift less productive.
Orders, hours, investment, and consumption may all change. Start with questions
rather than labeling the event a ``technology shock.'' Can the factories produce
less with the same inputs? Are households poorer? Should they postpone work or
investment until conditions improve? Would the answer differ if the interruption
lasted one week rather than several years?

Ramsey already gives a way to compare consumption today and tomorrow. But with
fixed labor and a known production path, it leaves two margins closed. Households
cannot move working time toward more rewarding dates, and they never have to
choose before learning tomorrow's production conditions. Let labor be chosen;
let future productivity be uncertain. Keep preferences, ownership, production,
and market clearing explicit. This produces a tractable real business cycle
(RBC) benchmark.

This is a teaching reconstruction of the modeling questions, not a record of
any author's private thought process. Kydland and Prescott (1982) and Long and
Plosser (1983) are foundational contributions. Their actual environments differ;
the one-sector model below is a simpler teaching economy, without the former's
multi-period construction technology or the latter's sectoral structure.
\footnote{Finn E. Kydland and Edward C. Prescott (1982), ``Time to Build and
Aggregate Fluctuations,'' \textit{Econometrica}, 50(6), 1345--1370,
\url{https://www.minneapolisfed.org/~/media/files/research/prescott/papers/timetobuild.pdf};
John B. Long, Jr., and Charles I. Plosser (1983), ``Real Business Cycles,''
\textit{Journal of Political Economy}, 91(1), 39--69,
\url{https://doi.org/10.1086/261128}.}

\subsubsection{Choose the disturbance, then explain its transmission}

A scalar $A_t$ summarizes how much output given capital and hours can produce.
An unexpected change in it is an innovation. The persistence of $A_t$ describes
how long production opportunities change. Household choices and accumulated
capital determine how that change travels through the economy. These are three
different objects: innovation, persistence, and propagation.

A fall in $A_t$ need not mean that engineers forgot a blueprint. A power or
logistics disruption can reduce effective production opportunities. But treating
an event as a scalar productivity change is a modeling decision: energy inputs,
relative prices, network bottlenecks, and distributional losses have been
suppressed. A credit refusal or an absence of orders cannot automatically be
reclassified as technology. Their mechanisms need their own constraints.

The growth models explain accumulation and, in some cases, the source of trend
growth. Here we hold trend productivity constant to isolate fluctuations around
a stationary reference. This does not assert that actual output has no trend.
Adding a deterministic trend requires a consistent normalization of production,
preferences, and Euler conditions; it cannot be hidden inside the shock term.

\Needspace{11\baselineskip}
\begin{blueblock}
\textbf{A real benchmark is a conditional experiment.}
The original course principles become two questions. How much joint movement
can real disturbances produce when agents respond optimally? Which observed
movements require additional mechanisms? Money and nominal frictions are
excluded from the real allocation block below. Their exclusion does not
establish their empirical irrelevance. Government purchases, relative import
prices, and preference disturbances can be studied in extensions, but are not
silently included in this one-shock economy.
\end{blueblock}

\subsection{Complete conditions and the order of decisions}\label{sec:rbc-conditions}

The main model has endogenous capital. The course's fixed-capital benchmark
will be recovered as a separate restriction in Section~\ref{sec:rbc-fixed}.
Population is one and does not grow; hours are allowed to vary at every date.
In particular, zero population growth is not constant labor input.

\Needspace{18\baselineskip}
\begin{modelsetup}[1]
\begin{modelcondition}{01}{Dates, people, and the time endowment}
Time is discrete, $t=0,1,\ldots$. A closed economy contains many identical,
infinite-lived, price-taking households, aggregated to one representative
household of constant mass one. Each household has one unit of time per date:
$N_t\in[0,1]$ is work and $L_t=1-N_t$ is leisure. Work denotes an intensive
hours margin; there is no separate unemployment, participation, matching, or
involuntary rationing margin. Consumption satisfies $C_t>0$.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{02}{Goods, states, ownership, and timing}
One homogeneous good can be consumed or installed as capital at unit resource
cost. Households own the capital and firm shares. The given initial stock is
$K_0>0$; $K_t$ was chosen at $t-1$ and is usable at $t$. The current productivity
$A_t$ is observed before choosing $C_t,N_t,I_t,K_{t+1}$ and financial positions.
Next period's innovation is not yet observed. Capital is the only accumulated
productive stock; there are no inventories, skills, adjustment costs,
installation delays, or variable utilization in this benchmark.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{03}{Production and competitive firms}
Firms rent capital and hire labor in competitive spot markets. Their technology
is $Y_t=A_tK_t^\alpha N_t^{1-\alpha}$, with $A_t>0$ and $0<\alpha<1$.
It has constant returns in $(K_t,N_t)$, positive marginal products, and diminishing
marginal products holding the other input fixed. Firms take prices and $A_t$
as given; there is no markup, externality, or nominal price or wage rigidity.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{04}{The stochastic process and information}
Write $a_t=\log(A_t/\bar A)$, where $\bar A>0$, and let
\[
a_t=\rho_a a_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t^a,\qquad |\rho_a|<1,\qquad
E_{t-1}\varepsilon_t^a=0,\quad E[(\varepsilon_t^a)^2]=s_\varepsilon^2<\infty.
\]
Innovations are independent and identically distributed, with a distribution
known to households; Gaussian innovations are an optional specification.
$E_t$ conditions on the full observed history through $t$ and the equilibrium
rules, including current $K_t,A_t$. Rational expectations means using this
conditional law, not knowing the future realization. $a_t=0$ is the deterministic
reference, not a claim that $E A_t=\bar A$ under uncertainty.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{05}{Preferences and the household objective}
The household maximizes $E_0\sum_{t=0}^\infty\beta^t u(C_t,N_t)$, with
$0<\beta<1$, $\sigma>0$, $\chi>0$, and $\varphi>0$, where
\[
u(C,N)=
\begin{cases}
(C^{1-\sigma}-1)/(1-\sigma)-\chi N^{1+\varphi}/(1+\varphi),&\sigma\ne1,\\
\log C-\chi N^{1+\varphi}/(1+\varphi),&\sigma=1.
\end{cases}
\]
The original course normalization is $\chi=1$. $\sigma$ governs consumption
curvature; $\varphi$ governs the curvature of work disutility. We derive local
interior conditions with $0<N_t<1$. Parameter and shock choices must support
finite expected welfare and feasible paths; large shocks may activate corners
that a local solution does not describe.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{06}{Accumulation, prices, and household resources}
Capital depreciates at $0<\delta<1$ and satisfies
$K_{t+1}=(1-\delta)K_t+I_t\geq0$. The baseline permits reversible investment;
there is no additional $I_t\geq0$ constraint. Let $w_t$ be the real wage,
$q_t$ the real capital rental payment before depreciation, and $d_t$ real
profits distributed to households. With no financial trade the household budget is
\[
C_t+K_{t+1}=w_tN_t+(q_t+1-\delta)K_t+d_t.
\]
Households face no tax, transfer, borrowing wedge, or idiosyncratic uninsured
risk. Prices and returns are taken as given in individual optimization;
market clearing is imposed afterward.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{07}{Financial claims and nominal units}
For the course's nominal bond calculation, $P_t>0$ is the goods price,
$W_t=P_tw_t$, and $D_t=P_td_t$. $B_t$ pays one currency unit per unit at $t+1$
and costs $Q_t>0$ at $t$, so $R_t^n=1/Q_t=1+i_t$ is the gross nominal return.
The full budget is
\[
P_t[C_t+K_{t+1}-(1-\delta)K_t]+Q_tB_t
 =W_tN_t+P_tq_tK_t+B_{t-1}+D_t.
\]
Bonds are in zero aggregate net supply and $B_{-1}=0$. A household's small
financial trade is allowed when deriving its Euler condition; identical-agent
market clearing then sets aggregate $B_t=0$. A risk-free \emph{real} claim is
a separate hypothetical payoff, not the nominal bond deflated by a known
future price. There is no monetary asset in the core household budget.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{08}{Market clearing, solvency, and terminal behavior}
Household labor and capital supplies equal firms' demands. Goods clear as
$Y_t=C_t+I_t$, and constant returns with competitive pricing imply $d_t=0$.
Admissible household plans rule out debt rollovers that finance consumption
without resources. For the capital-only aggregate optimum the transversality
restriction is
\[
\lim_{T\to\infty}E_0[\beta^T C_T^{-\sigma}K_{T+1}]=0.
\]
Solvency restricts admissible borrowing; transversality selects optimal terminal
behavior. Neither follows merely from writing the interior Euler equations.
No-Ponzi restrictions also apply to any individual financial trades.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{09}{Equilibrium, reference state, and scope}
An equilibrium is a collection of history-contingent allocations and prices
satisfying household optimality and admissibility, firm optimality, the
productivity law, and all market-clearing conditions for the given initial
state. We select the locally stable interior solution near its deterministic
steady state. Money, government, foreign trade, monopoly, financial frictions,
and household heterogeneity are absent. A nominal policy or quantity closure
is an extra specification; the real equations alone do not select $P_t$.
\end{modelcondition}
\end{modelsetup}

The within-date sequence is
\[
\underbrace{K_t,\ A_t\text{ observed}}_{\text{inherited stock and current information}}
\ \longrightarrow\
\underbrace{N_t,\ Y_t,\ C_t,\ I_t}_{\text{work, production, and allocation}}
\ \longrightarrow\
\underbrace{K_{t+1}}_{\text{productive next date}}.
\]
An announcement about tomorrow can change today's choices; tomorrow's machine
stock cannot jump retroactively. In the experiment below only unanticipated
productivity innovations occur.

\subsection{Household choice: working now and transferring consumption}

\subsubsection{Retain the course's nominal bond route}

First suppress capital trade to isolate the original asset calculation. Write
nominal nonlabor income as $\mathcal D_t$; this includes the fixed factor's
income in the course benchmark. The budget is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rbc-course-budget}
P_tC_t+Q_tB_t=B_{t-1}+W_tN_t+\mathcal D_t.
\end{equation}
Use resources minus expenditures in the multiplier term:
\begin{equation}
\mathscr L=E_0\sum_{t=0}^\infty\beta^t
\{u(C_t,N_t)+\lambda_t[B_{t-1}+W_tN_t+\mathcal D_t-P_tC_t-Q_tB_t]\}.
\end{equation}
For an interior choice at a history observed at $t$, the first two derivatives are
\begin{align}
u_{C,t}-\lambda_tP_t&=0,
 &\lambda_t&=u_{C,t}/P_t,\\
u_{N,t}+\lambda_tW_t&=0,
 &-u_{N,t}&=\lambda_tW_t.
\end{align}
The common factor $\beta^t$ and the probability of reaching this history cancel
from the two conditions. Divide the second condition by the first:
\begin{align}
\frac{-u_{N,t}}{u_{C,t}}&=\frac{\lambda_tW_t}{\lambda_tP_t},\\
-\frac{u_{N,t}}{u_{C,t}}&=\frac{W_t}{P_t}=w_t.\label{eq:rbc-labor-general}
\end{align}
The marginal disutility of another working hour equals its consumption-valued
wage. This is an intratemporal condition: all its objects are known at $t$.

One unit of $B_t$ costs $Q_t$ now and pays one currency unit in every next-date
state. Its two multiplier contributions give
\begin{align}
0&=-\beta^t\lambda_tQ_t+\beta^{t+1}E_t\lambda_{t+1},\\
\lambda_tQ_t&=\beta E_t\lambda_{t+1},\\
Q_t\frac{u_{C,t}}{P_t}&=\beta E_t\!\left[\frac{u_{C,t+1}}{P_{t+1}}\right],\\
Q_t&=\beta E_t\!\left[
\frac{u_{C,t+1}}{u_{C,t}}\frac{P_t}{P_{t+1}}\right].\label{eq:rbc-nominal-euler}
\end{align}
Current $P_t$ and $u_{C,t}$ can leave the expectation; future marginal utility
and the future price generally cannot. The last line retains the course's
nominal Euler equation with a consistent sign and information convention.

Differentiate the stated utility explicitly:
\begin{align}
\frac{d}{dC}\frac{C^{1-\sigma}-1}{1-\sigma}
 &=\frac{(1-\sigma)C^{-\sigma}}{1-\sigma}=C^{-\sigma},\\
\frac{d}{dC}\log C&=1/C=C^{-1},\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial N}
 &=-\frac{\chi(1+\varphi)N^\varphi}{1+\varphi}=-\chi N^\varphi.
\end{align}
Substitution into the two optimality conditions yields
\begin{align}
-\frac{-\chi N_t^\varphi}{C_t^{-\sigma}}
 &=\chi C_t^\sigma N_t^\varphi=w_t,\label{eq:rbc-labor}\\
\frac{u_{C,t+1}}{u_{C,t}}
 &=\frac{C_{t+1}^{-\sigma}}{C_t^{-\sigma}}
   =\left(\frac{C_{t+1}}{C_t}\right)^{-\sigma},\\
Q_t&=\beta E_t\!\left[
\left(\frac{C_{t+1}}{C_t}\right)^{-\sigma}
\frac{P_t}{P_{t+1}}\right].\label{eq:rbc-bond-crra}
\end{align}
When $C_t$ and thus the marginal value of resources are held fixed,
\begin{align}
\log N_t&=\frac{\log w_t-\log\chi-\sigma\log C_t}{\varphi},\\
\left.\frac{\partial\log N_t}{\partial\log w_t}\right|_{u_{C,t}}
 &=1/\varphi.
\end{align}
This is the Frisch hours elasticity. Along an equilibrium, consumption also
changes: a higher wage creates both a reward to working and additional wealth.
The Frisch calculation alone does not determine the total hours response.

\Needspace{12\baselineskip}
\begin{blueblock}
\textbf{Keep random prices inside the expectation.}
In general $E_t(X/Z)\ne E_tX/E_tZ$, and $E_t(XZ)\ne E_tX\,E_tZ$.
For two equally likely future states with $(X,Z)=(1,1),(3,2)$,
\[
E(X/Z)=\tfrac12(1+3/2)=5/4,\qquad EX/EZ=2/(3/2)=4/3.
\]
The nominal bond pays a certain currency amount, but its purchasing power
$1/P_{t+1}$ is uncertain. A risk-free real bond instead has a certain goods
payoff. Confusing the two loses the connection between prices and consumption
risk.
\end{blueblock}

\subsubsection{Capital brings back the Ramsey margin, with uncertain returns}

For the full budget in card 07, consumption and hours have the same derivatives.
The cost of one extra unit of $K_{t+1}$ is $P_t$ at $t$. Next date it delivers
rental income $P_{t+1}q_{t+1}$ plus surviving capital $P_{t+1}(1-\delta)$.
Taking prices as given, the two-date derivative is
\begin{align}
0&=-\beta^t\lambda_tP_t
 +\beta^{t+1}E_t[\lambda_{t+1}P_{t+1}(q_{t+1}+1-\delta)],\\
\lambda_tP_t
 &=\beta E_t[\lambda_{t+1}P_{t+1}(q_{t+1}+1-\delta)],\\
C_t^{-\sigma}
 &=\beta E_t[C_{t+1}^{-\sigma}(q_{t+1}+1-\delta)].\label{eq:rbc-capital-euler}
\end{align}
Let $R^K_{t+1}=q_{t+1}+1-\delta$ and
$M_{t,t+1}=\beta(C_{t+1}/C_t)^{-\sigma}$. Division by current marginal utility gives
\begin{align}
1&=E_t[M_{t,t+1}R^K_{t+1}],\\
Q_t&=E_t[M_{t,t+1}P_t/P_{t+1}],\\
R_t^f&=1/E_tM_{t,t+1}.\label{eq:rbc-pricing}
\end{align}
The last line prices a hypothetical risk-free real payoff. The bond can help
price intertemporal transfers even though identical households hold zero bonds
in aggregate. It cannot make aggregate consumption exceed aggregate resources.

Expected capital returns need not equal the risk-free return. Expanding the
conditional covariance gives
\begin{align}
1&=(E_tM_{t,t+1})(E_tR^K_{t+1})
       +\operatorname{Cov}_t(M_{t,t+1},R^K_{t+1}),\\
E_tR^K_{t+1}
 &=R_t^f-\frac{\operatorname{Cov}_t(M_{t,t+1},R^K_{t+1})}{E_tM_{t,t+1}}.
\end{align}
A payoff arriving when consumption is scarce is especially valuable. This
risk distinction disappears to first order around the deterministic reference;
it is not absent from the exact model.

\subsection{Firms, resource clearing, and the real equilibrium}

\subsubsection{Differentiate before normalizing the capital stock}

In real goods the firm solves
\[
\max_{K_t^d,N_t^d}\;
 A_t(K_t^d)^\alpha(N_t^d)^{1-\alpha}-w_tN_t^d-q_tK_t^d.
\]
The household owns the stock; the firm rents its current services. Thus the
firm's capital demand is a within-date choice, even though aggregate capital
supply $K_t$ is predetermined. The input derivatives at an interior optimum are
\begin{align}
0&=(1-\alpha)A_t(K_t^d)^\alpha(N_t^d)^{-\alpha}-w_t,\\
0&=\alpha A_t(K_t^d)^{\alpha-1}(N_t^d)^{1-\alpha}-q_t.
\end{align}
Impose $K_t^d=K_t$ and $N_t^d=N_t$ afterward:
\begin{align}
w_t&=(1-\alpha)A_tK_t^\alpha N_t^{-\alpha}
       =(1-\alpha)Y_t/N_t,\label{eq:rbc-wage}\\
q_t&=\alpha A_tK_t^{\alpha-1}N_t^{1-\alpha}
       =\alpha Y_t/K_t.\label{eq:rbc-rent}
\end{align}
For a prescribed output $Y_t$, cost minimization over labor alone, with fixed
$K_t$, delivers the same conditional labor requirement. It is not an
unconstrained minimization of wage payments with no output requirement.
The stock cannot be replaced by the wage in the production function.

Factor income exhausts output:
\begin{align}
w_tN_t&=(1-\alpha)Y_t,\qquad q_tK_t=\alpha Y_t,\\
d_t&=Y_t-w_tN_t-q_tK_t
 =Y_t-(1-\alpha)Y_t-\alpha Y_t=0.
\end{align}
Zero profit does not remove households' capital income. Use the household
budget, zero bond supply, and these payments to obtain
\begin{align}
C_t+K_{t+1}&=w_tN_t+q_tK_t+(1-\delta)K_t+d_t,\\
 &=Y_t+(1-\delta)K_t,\\
Y_t-C_t&=K_{t+1}-(1-\delta)K_t=I_t,\\
Y_t&=C_t+I_t.\label{eq:rbc-resources}
\end{align}
Saving allocates current goods to capital; bond trade only rearranges claims
between households. The labor condition, resource constraint, and capital
Euler equation play different roles and must all hold.

\subsubsection{The planner route checks the same allocation}

With the stated competition, ownership, and absence of wedges, a planner can
choose the aggregate allocation under the same technology and information:
\begin{align}
\mathscr L^P=E_0\sum_{t=0}^\infty\beta^t\{u(C_t,N_t)
 +\mu_t[A_tK_t^\alpha N_t^{1-\alpha}+(1-\delta)K_t-C_t-K_{t+1}]\}.
\end{align}
Its derivatives are
\begin{align}
u_{C,t}-\mu_t&=0,\\
u_{N,t}+\mu_t(1-\alpha)A_tK_t^\alpha N_t^{-\alpha}&=0,\\
-\mu_t+\beta E_t[\mu_{t+1}
 (\alpha A_{t+1}K_{t+1}^{\alpha-1}N_{t+1}^{1-\alpha}+1-\delta)]&=0.
\end{align}
Substituting $\mu_t=C_t^{-\sigma}$ recovers
\eqref{eq:rbc-labor} and \eqref{eq:rbc-capital-euler}, with the competitive
factor prices. Concavity, feasibility, and the terminal restriction accompany
these interior conditions. The equivalence must be reconsidered when a wedge,
externality, markup, or market incompleteness changes the environment.

\Needspace{12\baselineskip}
\begin{greenblock}
\textbf{The equilibrium is a loop, not two independent curves.}
Observed productivity and inherited capital determine productive opportunities.
Households choose hours and consumption jointly, using both current wages and
expected future returns. Hours change output and factor prices; the consumption
choice leaves investment; investment changes tomorrow's capital. Prices must
support all of these choices at once. An AS/AD grouping can organize the
original equations, but cannot close the economy without its resource,
accumulation, expectations, and terminal conditions.
\end{greenblock}

A compact list of the \emph{exact} real conditions is therefore
\begin{align}
Y_t&=A_tK_t^\alpha N_t^{1-\alpha},\\
\chi C_t^\sigma N_t^\varphi&=(1-\alpha)Y_t/N_t,\\
C_t^{-\sigma}&=\beta E_t[C_{t+1}^{-\sigma}
 (\alpha Y_{t+1}/K_{t+1}+1-\delta)],\\
Y_t&=C_t+I_t,\\
K_{t+1}&=(1-\delta)K_t+I_t,\\
a_t&=\rho_a a_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t^a.
\end{align}
The initial state and transversality condition remain part of the model even
when they are not repeated in this list. Wages, rentals, and bond prices are
then recovered from their pricing equations. No nominal variable is required
to solve this real block.

\subsection{Recover the course's fixed-capital benchmark}\label{sec:rbc-fixed}

Freeze productive capital at $\bar K=1$, remove its investment choice, and
assume no depreciation, government purchases, or foreign trade. There is no
replacement expenditure, so $I_t=0$ and $C_t=Y_t$. This is a separate classroom
restriction, not the full model with its capital Euler equation still imposed.
Owners receive fixed-factor income $\mathcal D_t/P_t=\alpha Y_t$; the labor-only
firm's residual pays that factor. The course's $\chi=1$ may also be imposed.
The limit $\alpha=0$ recovers labor-only production, as allowed in the course;
the main capital model uses $0<\alpha<1$.

The real equations become
\begin{align}
Y_t&=A_tN_t^{1-\alpha},\qquad C_t=Y_t,\\
w_t&=(1-\alpha)A_tN_t^{-\alpha},\\
w_t&=\chi C_t^\sigma N_t^\varphi.
\end{align}
Solve this restricted allocation without skipping the wealth effect. Equate
wages and replace consumption by production:
\begin{align}
\chi C_t^\sigma N_t^\varphi
 &=(1-\alpha)A_tN_t^{-\alpha},\\
\chi(A_tN_t^{1-\alpha})^\sigma N_t^\varphi
 &=(1-\alpha)A_tN_t^{-\alpha},\\
N_t^{\sigma(1-\alpha)+\varphi+\alpha}
 &=\frac{1-\alpha}{\chi}A_t^{1-\sigma}.
\end{align}
Let $d_N=\sigma(1-\alpha)+\varphi+\alpha>0$. At an interior solution,
\begin{align}
N_t&=\left[\frac{1-\alpha}{\chi}A_t^{1-\sigma}\right]^{1/d_N},\\
\log N_t&=\frac{\log(1-\alpha)-\log\chi}{d_N}
              +\frac{1-\sigma}{d_N}\log A_t,\\
\frac{d\log N_t}{d\log A_t}&=\frac{1-\sigma}{d_N},\\
\frac{d\log Y_t}{d\log A_t}
 &=1+(1-\alpha)\frac{1-\sigma}{d_N}
 =\frac{1+\varphi}{d_N}.
\end{align}
The last equality follows by collecting
$d_N+(1-\alpha)(1-\sigma)=\varphi+\alpha+1-\alpha=1+\varphi$.
The formulas apply while $0<N_t<1$; they do not bypass the time endowment.

\Needspace{11\baselineskip}
\begin{redblock}
\textbf{Question: does better productivity necessarily raise work?}
In this fixed-factor economy, $\sigma=1$ gives
$N_t=[(1-\alpha)/\chi]^{1/(1+\varphi)}$, independent of $A_t$.
Higher productivity raises the wage and income, but the work-reward and wealth
margins exactly offset. For $\sigma<1$ hours rise; for $\sigma>1$ they fall,
provided the solution is interior. Endogenous labor is not by itself a
prediction that hours always move with output. The investment margin in the
full model changes this experiment.
\end{redblock}

There is no endogenous capital memory here. With serially independent $A_t$,
the real allocation depends only on the current shock. A persistent $A_t$
produces persistence mechanically; zero-net-supply bond prices do not create
another aggregate resource state. This is precisely why the planned capital
section matters.

\subsection{Capital accumulation and the deterministic reference}\label{sec:rbc-steady}

\subsubsection{Find the steady state in the order the conditions determine it}

Switch innovations off and set $A_t=\bar A$. A deterministic steady state has
constant $K^*,C^*,N^*,Y^*,I^*$. It is a reference for local expansion, not the
unconditional mean of the nonlinear stochastic economy. The Euler equation gives
\begin{align}
(C^*)^{-\sigma}&=\beta(C^*)^{-\sigma}(q^*+1-\delta),\\
1&=\beta(q^*+1-\delta),\\
q^*&=\beta^{-1}-1+\delta.
\end{align}
Let $v=Y^*/K^*=q^*/\alpha>0$. Capital stationarity and goods clearing imply
\begin{align}
K^*&=(1-\delta)K^*+I^*,&I^*&=\delta K^*,\\
C^*&=Y^*-\delta K^*,&s_I\equiv I^*/Y^*&=\delta/v,\\
s_C\equiv C^*/Y^*&=1-\delta/v,&s_C+s_I&=1.
\end{align}
Here $v>\delta$ since $q^*>\delta$ and $\alpha<1$, so $s_C>0$.
The steady capital--hours ratio $x=K^*/N^*$ follows from the rental equation:
\begin{align}
q^*&=\alpha\bar A(K^*)^{\alpha-1}(N^*)^{1-\alpha}
 =\alpha\bar A x^{\alpha-1},\\
x^{1-\alpha}&=\alpha\bar A/q^*,\\
x&=(\alpha\bar A/q^*)^{1/(1-\alpha)}.
\end{align}
Define $z=Y^*/N^*=\bar A x^\alpha$. Then $C^*=s_CzN^*$ and
$w^*=(1-\alpha)z$. The labor condition pins down the scale:
\begin{align}
\chi(s_CzN^*)^\sigma(N^*)^\varphi&=(1-\alpha)z,\\
(N^*)^{\sigma+\varphi}
 &=\frac{(1-\alpha)z}{\chi(s_Cz)^\sigma},\\
N^*&=\left[\frac{(1-\alpha)z}{\chi(s_Cz)^\sigma}\right]^{1/(\sigma+\varphi)},\\
K^*&=xN^*,\quad Y^*=zN^*,\quad C^*=s_CzN^*,\quad I^*=\delta xN^*.
\end{align}
If this $N^*$ is outside $(0,1)$, the interior formula is inapplicable. One can
instead choose an intended interior $N^*$ and solve the same equation for
$\chi$; that is a stated calibration, not an extra equilibrium condition.

\subsubsection{Why current investment and current capital must not be confused}

A factory can change operating hours after learning $A_t$, but the stock
$K_t$ is already installed. Investment uses goods today and changes
$K_{t+1}$. Even after $A_t$ returns to its reference, the economy can inherit
a different capital stock. A single innovation can therefore have effects
lasting beyond the disturbance itself. The equations must determine whether
the stock builds up or runs down; the accounting identity alone does not
choose investment.

\subsection{Log-linearization: show the approximation in each equation}\label{sec:rbc-loglinear}

\subsubsection{The course's two routes describe the same local deviation}

For any positive $X_t$ with positive deterministic reference $X^*$, define
$\hat x_t=\log X_t-\log X^*$. Lowercase hats always denote log deviations;
$K_t$ is a level, $\hat k_t$ its deviation. Let $d_t=(X_t-X^*)/X^*$.
The logarithm route gives
\begin{align}
\hat x_t&=\log(X_t/X^*)=\log(1+d_t),\\
\log(1+d_t)&=\log1+\left.\frac{1}{1+d}\right|_{d=0}d_t+O(d_t^2),\\
\hat x_t&=d_t+O(d_t^2)\approx (X_t-X^*)/X^*.
\end{align}
The exponential route starts from the exact inverse:
\begin{align}
\log X_t&=\log X^*+\hat x_t,\\
X_t&=e^{\log X^*+\hat x_t}=X^*e^{\hat x_t},\\
e^{\hat x_t}&=1+\hat x_t+O(\hat x_t^2),\\
X_t&=X^*(1+\hat x_t)+O(\hat x_t^2),\\
(X_t-X^*)/X^*&=\hat x_t+O(\hat x_t^2).
\end{align}
The two derivations retain the original teaching routes. Positivity makes an
absolute-value sign unnecessary. An equality between a log deviation and a
percentage deviation is only first order. In the figures, $100\hat x_t$ is an
approximate percentage response, not an exact level change.

\subsubsection{Products become exact log differences}

Take logs of production and of its steady-state counterpart, then subtract:
\begin{align}
\log Y_t&=\log A_t+\alpha\log K_t+(1-\alpha)\log N_t,\\
\log Y^*&=\log\bar A+\alpha\log K^*+(1-\alpha)\log N^*,\\
\hat y_t&=a_t+\alpha\hat k_t+(1-\alpha)\hat n_t.\label{eq:rbc-linear-y}
\end{align}
This log identity is exact for Cobb--Douglas, before any solution approximation.
Do the same for the real wage and rental price:
\begin{align}
\log w_t&=\log(1-\alpha)+\log Y_t-\log N_t,\\
\hat w_t&=\hat y_t-\hat n_t
 =a_t+\alpha\hat k_t-\alpha\hat n_t,\label{eq:rbc-linear-w}\\
\log q_t&=\log\alpha+\log Y_t-\log K_t,\\
\hat q_t&=\hat y_t-\hat k_t
 =a_t+(\alpha-1)\hat k_t+(1-\alpha)\hat n_t.\label{eq:rbc-linear-q}
\end{align}
The labor condition gives
\begin{align}
\log w_t&=\log\chi+\sigma\log C_t+\varphi\log N_t,\\
\hat w_t&=\sigma\hat c_t+\varphi\hat n_t,\\
a_t+\alpha\hat k_t-\alpha\hat n_t
 &=\sigma\hat c_t+\varphi\hat n_t,\\
(\varphi+\alpha)\hat n_t
 &=a_t+\alpha\hat k_t-\sigma\hat c_t.\label{eq:rbc-linear-n}
\end{align}
If writing nominal wages instead, $w_t=W_t/P_t$ implies
$\hat w_t=\hat W_t-\hat p_t$. Keeping this distinction avoids mixing a real
wage with a nominal wage divided by the price level again.

\subsubsection{Sums retain their steady-state weights}

Goods clearing is a sum, so its logarithm does not distribute across terms.
Instead insert the exponential representations:
\begin{align}
Y^*e^{\hat y_t}&=C^*e^{\hat c_t}+I^*e^{\hat\iota_t},\\
Y^*(1+\hat y_t)&\approx C^*(1+\hat c_t)+I^*(1+\hat\iota_t),\\
Y^*+Y^*\hat y_t&\approx C^*+I^*+C^*\hat c_t+I^*\hat\iota_t,\\
Y^*\hat y_t&\approx C^*\hat c_t+I^*\hat\iota_t,\\
\hat y_t&\approx s_C\hat c_t+s_I\hat\iota_t.\label{eq:rbc-linear-resources}
\end{align}
Here $\hat\iota_t=\log(I_t/I^*)$ denotes \emph{investment}, not an interest
rate. The local neighborhood has $I_t>0$ since $I^*>0$, even though the general
budget permits reversible investment. In the zero-investment course benchmark,
this log is undefined; $Y=C$ instead gives $\hat y=\hat c$ exactly.

Apply the same steps to accumulation:
\begin{align}
K^*e^{\hat k_{t+1}}&=(1-\delta)K^*e^{\hat k_t}+I^*e^{\hat\iota_t},\\
K^*(1+\hat k_{t+1})&\approx(1-\delta)K^*(1+\hat k_t)
 +I^*(1+\hat\iota_t),\\
K^*\hat k_{t+1}&\approx(1-\delta)K^*\hat k_t+I^*\hat\iota_t,\\
\hat k_{t+1}&\approx(1-\delta)\hat k_t+\delta\hat\iota_t.\label{eq:rbc-linear-k}
\end{align}
The weights sum to one because $I^*/K^*=\delta$. Substituting the resource
condition is often more useful for solving:
\begin{align}
\hat\iota_t&\approx(\hat y_t-s_C\hat c_t)/s_I,\\
\hat k_{t+1}&\approx(1-\delta)\hat k_t
 +\frac{\delta}{s_I}(\hat y_t-s_C\hat c_t),\\
&=(1-\delta)\hat k_t+v(\hat y_t-s_C\hat c_t).
\end{align}
The final coefficient uses $s_I=\delta/v$. It is not permissible to replace
investment by consumption when capital is being chosen.

\subsubsection{Expand inside the conditional expectation}

The capital Euler condition contains both a random marginal utility and a
random gross return. Since $R^{K*}=q^*+1-\delta=1/\beta$,
\begin{align}
R^K_{t+1}&=q^*e^{\hat q_{t+1}}+1-\delta,\\
&\approx R^{K*}+q^*\hat q_{t+1}
 =R^{K*}(1+\omega\hat q_{t+1}),\\
\omega&\equiv q^*/R^{K*}=\beta q^*.
\end{align}
Divide the Euler equation by $(C^*)^{-\sigma}$ and expand:
\begin{align}
e^{-\sigma\hat c_t}
 &=\beta E_t[e^{-\sigma\hat c_{t+1}}R^K_{t+1}],\\
1-\sigma\hat c_t
 &\approx\beta R^{K*}E_t[(1-\sigma\hat c_{t+1})
                          (1+\omega\hat q_{t+1})],\\
&=E_t[1-\sigma\hat c_{t+1}+\omega\hat q_{t+1}
             -\sigma\omega\hat c_{t+1}\hat q_{t+1}],\\
-\sigma\hat c_t&\approx-\sigma E_t\hat c_{t+1}
                         +\omega E_t\hat q_{t+1},\\
\sigma(E_t\hat c_{t+1}-\hat c_t)
 &\approx\omega E_t\hat q_{t+1}.\label{eq:rbc-linear-euler}
\end{align}
The product of deviations is second order and has been discarded, not replaced
by a product of expectations. The equality signs below refer to the resulting
linear approximation. Expected future rental income raises the reward to
saving; $K_{t+1}$ is chosen with date-$t$ information, but $A_{t+1}$ and hours
next date remain uncertain.

\Needspace{11\baselineskip}
\begin{blueblock}
\textbf{Expectations can be linearized; logarithms cannot simply pass through them.}
For an integrable local expansion $e^z=1+z+O(z^2)$, take $E_t$ after expanding.
In general $\log E_te^z\ne E_tz$. If $z=+h$ or $-h$ with equal probability,
\[
E z=0,\qquad \log E e^z=\log[(e^h+e^{-h})/2]>0\quad(h\ne0).
\]
The difference is of second order for small $h$. A first-order solution near a
deterministic state omits risk adjustments of that order; it does not justify
removing expectations from the exact Euler equation. This refines the original
course warning rather than dropping the expectation calculation.
\end{blueblock}

Finally, the shock law is already linear in logs:
\begin{align}
a_{t+1}&=\rho_a a_t+\varepsilon_{t+1}^a,\\
E_ta_{t+1}&=\rho_a a_t+E_t\varepsilon_{t+1}^a=\rho_a a_t,\\
E_ta_{t+2}&=\rho_a E_ta_{t+1}=\rho_a^2a_t,\\
E_ta_{t+h}&=\rho_a^h a_t.
\end{align}
A higher $\rho_a$ changes what households expect before they choose, not just
the shape of productivity after they have chosen.

\subsection{Solve the model before drawing its responses}\label{sec:rbc-solve}

\subsubsection{An exact special case checks the consumption--investment logic}

As a separate special case, set $\delta=1$ and $\sigma=1$, while retaining
$0<\alpha<1$ and the hours disutility. The partial-depreciation baseline will
be restored afterward. Guess $K_{t+1}=sY_t$ and $C_t=(1-s)Y_t$, where $0<s<1$.
The capital Euler equation becomes
\begin{align}
\frac{1}{C_t}&=\alpha\beta E_t\!\left[
 \frac{1}{C_{t+1}}\frac{Y_{t+1}}{K_{t+1}}\right],\\
\frac{1}{(1-s)Y_t}
 &=\alpha\beta E_t\!\left[
 \frac{Y_{t+1}}{(1-s)Y_{t+1}K_{t+1}}\right],\\
&=\frac{\alpha\beta}{(1-s)K_{t+1}}
 =\frac{\alpha\beta}{(1-s)sY_t},\\
1&=\alpha\beta/s,\qquad s=\alpha\beta.
\end{align}
Tomorrow's output cancels \emph{state by state}, before taking expectations.
It has not been replaced by its mean. The labor condition then gives
\begin{align}
\chi C_tN_t^\varphi&=(1-\alpha)Y_t/N_t,\\
\chi(1-\alpha\beta)Y_tN_t^{1+\varphi}&=(1-\alpha)Y_t,\\
N_t&=\left[\frac{1-\alpha}{\chi(1-\alpha\beta)}
     \right]^{1/(1+\varphi)}\equiv N^e.
\end{align}
Choose parameters with $0<N^e<1$. Feasibility and the terminal restriction are
also explicit:
\begin{align}
C_t+K_{t+1}&=(1-\alpha\beta)Y_t+\alpha\beta Y_t=Y_t,\\
E_0[\beta^T C_T^{-1}K_{T+1}]
 &=\beta^T\frac{\alpha\beta}{1-\alpha\beta}\ \longrightarrow\ 0.
\end{align}
Under admissible shock moments these policies satisfy the concave planner's
conditions. The constant share resembles the exact Ramsey log example, now
with uncertainty and hours. It does not extend to arbitrary depreciation or
curvature. Indeed, it makes consumption and investment proportional to output,
so it cannot explain every relative-volatility fact merely by being an RBC model.

\subsubsection{Reduce the partial-depreciation model without hiding its coefficients}

Return to $0<\delta<1$ and the general curvature parameters. Solve the linear
labor condition first, then insert it into production:
\begin{align}
\hat n_t&=\frac{\alpha}{\varphi+\alpha}\hat k_t
 +\frac{1}{\varphi+\alpha}a_t
 -\frac{\sigma}{\varphi+\alpha}\hat c_t,\\
\hat y_t&=a_t+\alpha\hat k_t+(1-\alpha)
 \left[\frac{\alpha\hat k_t+a_t-\sigma\hat c_t}{\varphi+\alpha}\right],\\
&=b_k\hat k_t+b_a a_t+b_c\hat c_t,
\end{align}
where collection of each coefficient gives
\begin{align}
b_k&=\alpha+\frac{(1-\alpha)\alpha}{\varphi+\alpha}
 =\frac{\alpha(1+\varphi)}{\varphi+\alpha},\\
b_a&=1+\frac{1-\alpha}{\varphi+\alpha}
 =\frac{1+\varphi}{\varphi+\alpha},\\
b_c&=-\frac{(1-\alpha)\sigma}{\varphi+\alpha}.
\end{align}
Substitute into capital accumulation:
\begin{align}
\hat k_{t+1}&=(1-\delta)\hat k_t
 +v[b_k\hat k_t+b_a a_t+b_c\hat c_t-s_C\hat c_t],\\
&=\gamma_k\hat k_t+\gamma_a a_t-\gamma_c\hat c_t,\label{eq:rbc-reduced-k}\\
\gamma_k&=1-\delta+vb_k,\qquad
\gamma_a=vb_a,\qquad \gamma_c=v(s_C-b_c)>0.
\end{align}
The Euler equation uses $\hat q_{t+1}=\hat y_{t+1}-\hat k_{t+1}$.
Since $K_{t+1}$ is known when chosen,
\begin{align}
E_t\hat q_{t+1}
 &=(b_k-1)\hat k_{t+1}+b_a\rho_a a_t+b_cE_t\hat c_{t+1},\\
\sigma E_t\hat c_{t+1}-\sigma\hat c_t
 &=\omega[(b_k-1)\hat k_{t+1}+b_a\rho_a a_t+b_cE_t\hat c_{t+1}],\\
(\sigma-\omega b_c)E_t\hat c_{t+1}
 &=\sigma\hat c_t+\omega(b_k-1)\hat k_{t+1}+\omega b_a\rho_a a_t.
\end{align}
Define $\tau=\sigma-\omega b_c$, $\upsilon=\omega(b_k-1)$, and
$\zeta=\omega b_a\rho_a$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rbc-reduced-euler}
\tau E_t\hat c_{t+1}=\sigma\hat c_t+\upsilon\hat k_{t+1}+\zeta a_t.
\end{equation}
These coefficients come from stated structural equations; none is an
independently fitted persistence parameter.

\subsubsection{Match coefficients and select the stable rule}

Conjecture a linear consumption rule
$\hat c_t=p_k\hat k_t+p_a a_t$. Equations
\eqref{eq:rbc-reduced-k} and the productivity forecast imply
\begin{align}
\hat k_{t+1}&=(\gamma_k-\gamma_cp_k)\hat k_t
                   +(\gamma_a-\gamma_cp_a)a_t,\\
&=H_k\hat k_t+H_a a_t,\\
E_t\hat c_{t+1}&=p_k\hat k_{t+1}+p_a\rho_a a_t.
\end{align}
Insert the last line into \eqref{eq:rbc-reduced-euler}, then collect:
\begin{align}
\tau(p_k\hat k_{t+1}+p_a\rho_a a_t)
 &=\sigma(p_k\hat k_t+p_a a_t)+\upsilon\hat k_{t+1}+\zeta a_t,\\
(\tau p_k-\upsilon)\hat k_{t+1}
 &=\sigma p_k\hat k_t+(\sigma p_a+\zeta-\tau\rho_a p_a)a_t.
\end{align}
Equating the coefficient of the predetermined stock yields
\begin{align}
(\tau p_k-\upsilon)(\gamma_k-\gamma_cp_k)&=\sigma p_k,\\
\tau\gamma_kp_k-\tau\gamma_cp_k^2-\upsilon\gamma_k
 +\upsilon\gamma_cp_k-\sigma p_k&=0,\\
\tau\gamma_cp_k^2-(\tau\gamma_k+\upsilon\gamma_c-\sigma)p_k
 +\upsilon\gamma_k&=0.
\end{align}
For $d_0=\tau\gamma_k+\upsilon\gamma_c-\sigma$, the candidate slopes are
\begin{equation}
p_k^{\pm}=\frac{d_0\pm\sqrt{d_0^2-4\tau\gamma_c\upsilon\gamma_k}}
                    {2\tau\gamma_c}.
\end{equation}
For each candidate compute $H_k=\gamma_k-\gamma_cp_k$. Keep the locally stable
root with $|H_k|<1$; the numerical example below has exactly one. As in Ramsey,
an arbitrary consumption slope also admits the unstable mode and does not
satisfy the desired terminal behavior near the reference.

For the productivity coefficient, let $J=\tau p_k-\upsilon$. Matching $a_t$ gives
\begin{align}
J(\gamma_a-\gamma_cp_a)&=\sigma p_a+\zeta-\tau\rho_a p_a,\\
J\gamma_a-\zeta&=(\sigma+J\gamma_c-\tau\rho_a)p_a,\\
p_a&=\frac{J\gamma_a-\zeta}{\sigma+J\gamma_c-\tau\rho_a},\qquad
H_a=\gamma_a-\gamma_cp_a.\label{eq:rbc-policy-a}
\end{align}
The denominator must be nonzero. Changing $\rho_a$ changes $p_a$ and $H_a$,
not just the future path of $a_t$. Resolve the rule when changing beliefs
about persistence.

The solved local state system is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rbc-state-rule}
\begin{pmatrix}\hat k_{t+1}\\a_{t+1}\end{pmatrix}
 =\begin{pmatrix}H_k&H_a\\0&\rho_a\end{pmatrix}
  \begin{pmatrix}\hat k_t\\a_t\end{pmatrix}
 +\begin{pmatrix}0\\\varepsilon_{t+1}^a\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Its two persistence roots are $H_k$ and $\rho_a$. Given the states,
$\hat c_t$, $\hat n_t$, $\hat y_t$, and $\hat\iota_t$ follow from the rules
already derived. Only the first-order approximation has been solved here;
large shocks, corners, and higher-order risk effects require other methods.

\subsection{A worked shock experiment and its economic meaning}\label{sec:rbc-experiment}

\subsubsection{State the calibration and reproduce the initial response}

Use quarters as dates and the illustrative parameters
\[
\alpha=0.33,\quad\beta=0.99,\quad\delta=0.025,\quad
\sigma=1,\quad\varphi=1,\quad\bar A=1.
\]
Set $N^*=1/3$ and choose the preference weight from the steady-state labor
condition. These are teaching choices, not estimates or a claimed fit to any
country. The steady calculations give
\begin{align}
q^*&=1/0.99-1+0.025\simeq0.035101,\\
v&=q^*/0.33\simeq0.106367,\\
s_I&=0.025/v\simeq0.235036,\qquad s_C\simeq0.764964,\\
K^*&\simeq9.449473,\quad Y^*\simeq1.005109,\quad
C^*\simeq0.768872,\quad I^*\simeq0.236237,\\
\chi&=\frac{(1-\alpha)Y^*/N^*}{(C^*)^\sigma(N^*)^\varphi}
 \simeq7.882724.
\end{align}
For $\rho_a=0.95$, coefficient matching selects
\[
p_k\simeq0.553075,\quad p_a\simeq0.386565,\quad
H_k\simeq0.953146,\quad H_a\simeq0.107783.
\]
The other consumption-stock slope is about $-0.236912$, implying
$H_k\simeq1.059755>1$, and is rejected. Start at the reference stock
$\hat k_0=0$, apply a one-time innovation $\varepsilon_0^a=0.01$, and set
all later innovations to zero. On impact,
\begin{align}
a_0&=0.01,\qquad \hat c_0=p_a a_0\simeq0.003866,\\
\hat n_0&=\frac{0.01-\hat c_0}{1.33}\simeq0.004612,\\
\hat y_0&=0.01+0.67\hat n_0\simeq0.013090,\\
\hat\iota_0&=\frac{\hat y_0-s_C\hat c_0}{s_I}\simeq0.043113,\\
\hat k_1&=(1-\delta)\hat k_0+\delta\hat\iota_0
          \simeq0.001078.
\end{align}
For the same $1\%$ log innovation with $\rho_a=0$, solve again:
$p_a\simeq0.086073$, $H_a\simeq0.148334$, while $p_k$ and $H_k$ are unchanged
in this model. The immediate responses are
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\hline
\tableheading{Variable} & \tableheading{$\rho_a=0.95$} & \tableheading{$\rho_a=0$}\\
\hline
Productivity & 1.000 & 1.000\\
Output & 1.309 & 1.460\\
Consumption & 0.387 & 0.086\\
Investment & 4.311 & 5.933\\
Hours & 0.461 & 0.687\\
Capital available at date 0 & 0.000 & 0.000\\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Entries are $100$ times log deviations, approximately percentage changes.
With $\rho_a=0$ the opportunity is brief: the household increases current work
more and consumes less of the additional output. A persistent improvement
raises the value of lifetime resources more, supporting a larger initial
consumption increase. This comparison includes both the changed opportunity
path and the reoptimized household response.

\subsubsection{Follow the goods and the inherited stock}

\Needspace{32\baselineskip}
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.94\linewidth]{rbc_impulse_responses.pdf}
\end{center}
\textit{Model-generated, first-order responses to one positive productivity
innovation.} Both paths start with the same capital and $a_0=0.01$. All later
innovations are zero; the consumption rule is solved separately for each
persistence parameter. The plot uses only the equations and hypothetical
parameters above, not estimated responses or observed data.

Output rises immediately because the existing machines and chosen work are
more productive. Consumption rises less in this example: households spread
resources across dates. Investment absorbs more of the extra output and changes
next quarter's machines. Its percentage response is especially large partly
because investment is a smaller steady-state share of output. From resources,
\[
\hat\iota_t=(\hat y_t-s_C\hat c_t)/s_I;
\]
a modest goods reallocation can be a large percentage of $I^*$.
This arithmetic helps interpret relative volatility; an impact ratio is not
itself a simulated standard-deviation ratio.

In the dashed path, productivity is back at its reference at date 1, but capital
is then above its reference. Output and consumption can remain above it. Hours
can be below it: the inherited stock makes households wealthier and changes
the wage they need to work. A real shock does not require every variable to
move in the same direction at every horizon. In the solid path, both
productivity persistence and capital memory operate. Stable roots imply
convergence back to this reference after the temporary innovation, rather
than a permanent change in trend growth.

The factory interpretation is concrete. More productive shifts free goods for
both consumption and replacement or expansion of equipment. Once equipment is
installed, its services remain after the unusually productive shift ends.
An automation investment, however, can also change tasks and workers' skills;
a credit shock can prevent a profitable installation. Those are additional
mechanisms, not effects already represented by this single $A_t$.

\Needspace{11\baselineskip}
\begin{greenblock}
\textbf{Capital propagates; expectations shape the initial allocation.}
A given current productivity surprise does not determine how much is consumed,
worked, or invested. The expected duration and future returns help determine
those choices. Capital then carries part of today's reallocation into later
production. Compare $\rho_a=0$ with $\rho_a>0$ only after resolving the policy;
holding the old consumption rule fixed would mix two different experiments.
\end{greenblock}

\subsection{Retain nominal pricing without confusing it with real closure}\label{sec:rbc-nominal}

\subsubsection{Derive the nominal Euler approximation and the Fisher comparison}

For this calculation take the deterministic nominal reference to have a
constant price level, hence zero inflation and $R^{n*}=1/\beta$. Define
\[
\hat j_t=\log(R_t^n/R^{n*}),\qquad
\pi_{t+1}=\log(P_{t+1}/P_t).
\]
$\hat j_t$ is the log deviation of the \emph{gross nominal interest factor};
it is not the investment deviation $\hat\iota_t$, and not generally the
percentage deviation of the net rate $i_t$. Invert $Q_t$ in
\eqref{eq:rbc-bond-crra} and divide by its reference:
\begin{align}
1&=\beta R_t^n E_t\!\left[
 (C_{t+1}/C_t)^{-\sigma}P_t/P_{t+1}\right],\\
&=e^{\hat j_t}E_t\exp\{-\sigma(\hat c_{t+1}-\hat c_t)-\pi_{t+1}\},\\
1&\approx(1+\hat j_t)
 [1-\sigma(E_t\hat c_{t+1}-\hat c_t)-E_t\pi_{t+1}],\\
0&\approx\hat j_t-\sigma(E_t\hat c_{t+1}-\hat c_t)-E_t\pi_{t+1},\\
\hat c_t&\approx E_t\hat c_{t+1}
 -\frac{1}{\sigma}(\hat j_t-E_t\pi_{t+1}).\label{eq:rbc-nominal-linear}
\end{align}
In the multiplication, products of deviations are discarded. The form resembles
the intertemporal demand relation used in the next chapter; here flexible-price
production and labor choices determine the supporting allocation jointly.

A real risk-free payoff instead satisfies
$1=\beta R_t^fE_t(C_{t+1}/C_t)^{-\sigma}$. Let
$\hat r_t^f=\log(R_t^f/(1/\beta))$. Expanding the same way gives
\begin{align}
0&\approx\hat r_t^f-\sigma(E_t\hat c_{t+1}-\hat c_t),\\
\hat r_t^f&\approx\hat j_t-E_t\pi_{t+1}.
\end{align}
This is the local Fisher comparison. Under uncertain inflation it is not an
exact statement equating the nominal bond's random realized real return to
a risk-free real return. Exact pricing retains the covariance terms and the
random purchasing power in \eqref{eq:rbc-pricing}.

\subsubsection{What does the original money-demand equation add?}

One can append the course's nominal relation in the clearly stated form
\begin{equation}
\frac{M_t}{P_t}=\kappa Y_t(R_t^n)^{-\eta},\qquad\kappa>0,\quad\eta>0.
\end{equation}
It is a reduced-form attachment, not a household FOC derived from the
money-free preferences and budget above. With $R_t^n=1/Q_t$, the same expression
is $M_t/P_t=\kappa Y_tQ_t^\eta$. Its log transformation is
\begin{align}
\log M_t-\log P_t&=\log\kappa+\log Y_t-\eta\log R_t^n,\\
\hat m_t-\hat p_t&=\hat y_t-\eta\hat j_t.
\end{align}
A genuine monetary model would specify why households hold money, its budget
and supply arrangements, and a compatible policy rule and boundary conditions.
Neither the real block nor this attachment alone selects a unique nominal
price path. The original warning about money neutrality is thus conditional.

For example, start with a nominal price path and claims that support the real
allocation and satisfy the attachment. Rescale $M_t,P_t,W_t,B_t,\mathcal D_t$
by the same positive constant at all dates, keeping $Q_t$ fixed. Real wages,
real balances, real payoffs, and every real allocation are unchanged. No real
resource or relative reward has changed. This unit-rescaling result does not
establish that an unanticipated monetary intervention has no real effects in
an economy with sticky contracts, redistribution, or financial frictions.

\Needspace{10\baselineskip}
\begin{blueblock}
\textbf{Money neutrality is a property of a specified environment.}
The real core excludes monetary wedges; its equations determine allocation
without a price level. A nominal attachment must still be closed consistently.
A particular level rescaling, a change in money growth, and a change in the
policy rule are different experiments. The core's separation of real and
nominal variables is useful for comparison with the next chapter, not evidence
that money never matters in actual fluctuations.
\end{blueblock}

\subsection{What should an economist learn from the benchmark?}

\subsubsection{Use joint paths to test mechanisms}

A plausible output response is only a starting point. Compare consumption,
investment, hours, real wages, and capital jointly, under a common measurement
and detrending convention. At a minimum, ask whether the same parameter values
produce credible relative volatilities, correlations, persistence, and response
timing. A model calibrated to selected moments has not independently explained
all those moments. A convenient choice of shock persistence cannot substitute
for evidence about its source.

For the production function used here, the measured residual would be
\begin{align}
\log A_t&=\log Y_t-\alpha\log K_t-(1-\alpha)\log N_t,\\
\Delta\log A_t&=\Delta\log Y_t-\alpha\Delta\log K_t
 -(1-\alpha)\Delta\log N_t.
\end{align}
This is accounting under the assumed technology. An omitted utilization rate,
input quality, changing markup, or measurement error can enter that residual.
Calling it an exogenous technology innovation requires an identification
argument beyond subtracting measured inputs.

Estimated technology responses can challenge the labor mechanism. Gal\'i
(1999), using his long-run identification strategy, reports a decline in hours
after a positive technology innovation.\footnote{Jordi Gal\'i (1999),
``Technology, Employment, and the Business Cycle: Do Technology Shocks Explain
Aggregate Fluctuations?'' \textit{American Economic Review}, 89(1), 249--271,
\url{https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.89.1.249}. The result is conditional on that
paper's identification and measurement, not a definition of a technology shock.}
The response of hours is an empirical question; the fixed-capital calculation
already showed why it is not a mathematical consequence of the word
``productivity.'' Different trend, identification, and propagation assumptions
must be examined rather than hidden behind an unconditional correlation.

Rebelo's review places the basic framework alongside subsequent extensions
and unresolved questions.\footnote{Sergio Rebelo (2005), ``Real Business Cycle
Models: Past, Present, and Future,'' \textit{Scandinavian Journal of Economics},
107(2), 217--238, \url{https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9442.2005.00405.x}.}
The teaching exercise here demonstrates an internally consistent mechanism;
it does not assign a numerical share of actual recessions to technology.

\subsubsection{Efficient adjustment can still be painful}

In the undistorted representative-household economy, the competitive allocation
matches the planner allocation under the same changing resources and information.
A negative productivity shock can reduce attainable consumption even when the
response is efficient. Restoring yesterday's quantities is not automatically a
feasible improvement. Efficiency of a response does not make the disturbance
beneficial.

In actual communities a closure can leave particular workers without income,
credit, matching opportunities, or insurance. This model has an intensive hours
choice and common ownership, so it cannot turn such losses into a theory of
involuntary unemployment or distribution. Removing hours from a work schedule
and failing to find a job are economically different events.

The first-order paths also do not suffice for a quantitatively valid welfare
comparison under uncertainty. Utility curvature, risk effects, and mean shifts
can contribute at second order. Plugging a first-order allocation into nonlinear
utility does not automatically supply all terms of a second-order welfare
calculation. Welfare claims require an approximation and a feasible policy
comparison suited to the question.

\subsubsection{Ask which missing constraint would change the diagnosis}

If a solvent factory has a profitable order but cannot finance working capital,
add the financing constraint. If workers want jobs at the current wage but
cannot find matches, add a labor-market mechanism. If firms cut production
because sales decline while prices adjust slowly, study demand and nominal
rigidity. If imported energy becomes scarce, distinguish its relative price
and resource constraint from a neutral productivity scalar. Government spending
similarly requires its purchases, taxes or debt, and household wealth effects
to be specified before a fiscal prediction can be derived.

The purpose of the benchmark is to make those additions meaningful. We now know
which outcomes can arise from uncertain real opportunities, work choice, and
accumulation alone, and which assumptions support the welfare interpretation.
The next chapter changes price setting. Its flexible-price comparison allocation
must not be confused with a fitted statistical trend, an economy with literally
no shocks, or a policy target that can be reached without cost.
