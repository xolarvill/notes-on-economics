\section{Growth Facts and Growth Accounting}\label{sec:growth-facts}

Why are some economies richer than others, and why do living standards grow?
Before choosing a model, we need to separate the outcomes to be explained.
This chapter distinguishes income levels from growth rates, then decomposes
output growth into measured inputs and productivity. The decomposition helps
locate a question; explaining the underlying decisions requires a growth model.

\subsection{Income levels, growth rates, and convergence}

\subsubsection{Which output, and per whom?}

Let $Y$ be real GDP, $N$ the population, $E$ the number of employed people,
and $L$ total hours worked. For positive quantities,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:facts-income-identity}
\frac{Y}{N}=\frac{Y}{L}\frac{L}{E}\frac{E}{N}.
\end{equation}
Output per person therefore depends on output per hour, hours per employed
person, and the employment-to-population ratio. Higher GDP growth need not
mean faster growth in GDP per person. Likewise, a rise in GDP per person can
come from more work per person without any rise in output per hour.

Use real output to study changes over time. For cross-country level comparisons,
purchasing power parities (PPPs) adjust for differences in price levels;
conversion at market exchange rates can mix output differences with price and
exchange-rate movements.\footnote{World Bank, ``ICP 2021: Concepts and
Definitions,'' explains PPP-based comparisons of expenditure volumes.
\url{https://www.worldbank.org/en/brief/2021/04/30/icp2021-concepts-definitions}.}
Use a common measure and specify the comparison year. GDP per person measures
average production, not its distribution or welfare: leisure, health, and
consumption opportunities also matter.

\subsubsection{A level difference is not a growth-rate difference}

For a positive continuous-time variable $X(t)$, define its instantaneous growth
rate as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:facts-growth-rate}
g_X(t)\equiv\frac{\dot X(t)}{X(t)}=\frac{d\log X(t)}{dt}.
\end{equation}
Logarithms turn products into sums. Applying this definition to
\eqref{eq:facts-income-identity} gives the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:facts-income-growth}
g_{Y/N}=g_{Y/L}+g_{L/E}+g_{E/N}.
\end{equation}
The ratios must be defined consistently; employment growth and population
growth do not enter in the same way.

For observations at dates $t$ and $t+T$, the average annual log growth rate is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:facts-average-growth}
\bar g_X=\frac{\log X_{t+T}-\log X_t}{T},
\qquad
\frac{X_{t+T}}{X_t}=e^{T\bar g_X}.
\end{equation}
The corresponding compound annual percentage change is $e^{\bar g_X}-1$,
approximately $\bar g_X$ for small rates. Unless stated otherwise, finite-period
accounting below uses log changes, $\Delta\log X_t=\log X_t-\log X_{t-1}$.

At a constant growth rate $g>0$, doubling takes $T=\log 2/g$. A 2\% annual
log growth rate doubles output in about 35 years. A one-off 10\% increase in
output raises its level; it leaves subsequent growth unchanged if the economy
then returns to its previous growth rate. A lasting increase in the growth
rate keeps widening the gap relative to the previous path.

\textbf{Facts that guide the models.}
Jones (2016) documents three useful patterns.\footnote{Charles I. Jones (2016),
``The Facts of Economic Growth,'' \textit{Handbook of Macroeconomics}, 2A,
3--69, especially Sections 1, 2, and 4. The empirical statements here describe
that historical evidence, not a current data release.
\url{https://doi.org/10.1016/bs.hesmac.2016.03.002}.}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item Sustained growth is a feature of modern economic history. US GDP per
person grew at about 2\% per year over 1870--2007, with substantial variation
across shorter periods.
\item Income levels differ widely across countries. Some economies catch up;
others remain far behind or lose ground over particular periods.
\item A roughly stable capital-output ratio is a useful long-run benchmark.
Factor shares and growth rates can change, so balanced growth is an
approximation to be assessed, rather than a universal empirical law.
\end{itemize}
A theory must distinguish growth at the frontier from movement toward it.
Matching a long-run average also does not require every shock to be temporary.

\subsubsection{What does convergence mean?}

Consider a poorer economy $i$ and a richer economy $j$, with per-person outputs
$y_i$ and $y_j$ measured in comparable units. Their log income gap evolves as
\begin{align}
D(t)&\equiv\log y_j(t)-\log y_i(t),\\
\dot D(t)&=g_{y_j}(t)-g_{y_i}(t),\\
D(T)-D(0)&=\int_0^T\left[g_{y_j}(t)-g_{y_i}(t)\right]dt.
\end{align}
The relative gap narrows when the poorer economy grows faster over the interval.
Positive growth in both economies is insufficient. Nor does a narrowing
relative gap necessarily reduce the absolute difference $y_j-y_i$.

\textit{Unconditional convergence} means poorer economies tend to grow faster
without conditioning on other characteristics. \textit{Conditional convergence}
allows economies to approach different long-run paths: an economy further
below its own path tends to grow faster, holding the determinants of that path
fixed. Saving, population growth, and productivity may differ across economies.
Diminishing returns to capital alone therefore do not imply that every poor
country must catch up with every rich country.\footnote{Robert J. Barro,
N. Gregory Mankiw, and Xavier Sala-i-Martin (1992), ``Capital Mobility in
Neoclassical Models of Growth,'' NBER Working Paper 4206, discusses convergence
relative to each economy's steady state. \url{https://doi.org/10.3386/w4206}.}

A tendency for initially poorer economies to grow faster is called
$\beta$-convergence. A decline in the cross-country dispersion of log income is
called $\sigma$-convergence. These are different statements: new shocks can
increase dispersion even when growth tends to be faster at lower initial
income. The sample and observation period are part of any convergence claim.

\subsection{Capital, labor, and productivity}

\subsubsection{From a production function to growth accounting}

Start with a one-good aggregate production function in continuous time:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:facts-production}
Y(t)=B(t)F\bigl(K(t),H(t)\bigr),\qquad H(t)=h(t)L(t).
\end{equation}
$K$ is productive physical capital, $L$ hours worked, and $h$ average labor
quality per hour. Thus $H$ is quality-adjusted labor input. $B$ is
Hicks-neutral total factor productivity (TFP). It multiplies the whole
production function; its growth need not equal the growth of labor-augmenting
technology used in the Solow chapter.

Assume positive inputs, differentiable $F$, constant returns to scale, and no
additional inputs. The total derivative is
\begin{equation}
\dot Y=\dot B F+B F_K\dot K+B F_H\dot H.
\end{equation}
Divide by $Y=BF$ and multiply and divide each input term by its input level:
\begin{align}
\frac{\dot Y}{Y}
&=\frac{\dot B}{B}
 +\frac{BF_KK}{Y}\frac{\dot K}{K}
 +\frac{BF_HH}{Y}\frac{\dot H}{H},\\
g_Y&=g_B+\varepsilon_K g_K+\varepsilon_H g_H,
\quad
\varepsilon_K\equiv\frac{BF_KK}{Y},\quad
\varepsilon_H\equiv\frac{BF_HH}{Y}.\label{eq:facts-elasticities}
\end{align}
The weights are output elasticities. Constant returns implies
$F_KK+F_HH=F$, so $\varepsilon_K+\varepsilon_H=1$.

To replace elasticities by observed income shares, we need further assumptions.
With competitive factor markets and no wedges, let $q$ be the real rental price
of capital services and $w_H$ the price of one unit of quality-adjusted labor.
Profit maximization gives
\begin{equation}
q=BF_K,\qquad w_H=BF_H.
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
s_K\equiv\frac{qK}{Y}=\varepsilon_K,\qquad
s_H\equiv\frac{w_HH}{Y}=\varepsilon_H=1-s_K.
\end{equation}
Here $q$ is a gross rental price before depreciation, not the net interest rate.
Labor compensation is $w_HH$; with common quality $h$, the wage per hour is
$w_Hh$. In this benchmark all output is paid to these two inputs. Pure profits,
markups, taxes, or omitted factors can break the equality between measured
income shares and output elasticities.

Substitute the shares and use $g_H=g_h+g_L$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:facts-accounting}
\boxed{g_Y=g_B+s_K g_K+(1-s_K)(g_h+g_L).}
\end{equation}
This is an exact instantaneous decomposition under the stated production and
pricing assumptions. It does not yet determine any input's growth rate.

\subsubsection{Labor productivity and finite-period contributions}

Subtract $g_L$ from both sides of \eqref{eq:facts-accounting}:
\begin{align}
g_{Y/L}
&=g_B+s_K g_K+(1-s_K)g_h-s_Kg_L,\\
&=g_B+s_Kg_{K/L}+(1-s_K)g_h.\label{eq:facts-labor-productivity}
\end{align}
Output per hour can rise through capital deepening, better labor quality, or
higher TFP. Labor productivity $Y/L$ and TFP $B$ are therefore different objects.
Combining this result with \eqref{eq:facts-income-growth} also separates
productivity growth from changes in work per person.

For annual data, a common implementation uses average adjacent-year income
shares, $\bar s_{K,t}=(s_{K,t}+s_{K,t-1})/2$:
\begin{align}
\Delta\log Y_t
&\approx \bar s_{K,t}\Delta\log K_t
 +(1-\bar s_{K,t})\Delta\log H_t
 +\Delta\log B_t,\label{eq:facts-discrete-accounting}\\
\Delta\log B_t^{\mathrm{res}}
&\equiv\Delta\log Y_t
 -\bar s_{K,t}\Delta\log K_t
 -(1-\bar s_{K,t})\Delta\log H_t.\label{eq:facts-residual}
\end{align}
The first line approximates the continuous-time decomposition over an interval.
The second defines the measured residual exactly for the chosen series and
weights. It need not equal a pure technology change. For Cobb--Douglas
production with constant exponents, the corresponding log decomposition is
exact over a finite interval.

\begin{blueblock}
\textbf{An accounting example.}
Suppose annual log changes are 0.04 for output, 0.06 for capital, and 0.01 for
quality-adjusted labor, with capital share $1/3$. Then
\[
\Delta\log B^{\mathrm{res}}
=0.04-\frac13(0.06)-\frac23(0.01)
=0.013\overline{3}.
\]
Capital contributes 2 percentage points, labor about 0.67, and the residual
about 1.33, when log changes are multiplied by 100. These sum to 4. This is an
illustration, not an estimate for a country. The calculation does not imply
that raising investment would cause exactly 2 percentage points of growth.
\end{blueblock}

\subsubsection{What is inside the residual?}

The measured residual depends on what the inputs capture. Capital services can
change differently from the value of the capital stock. Hours need not track
employment, and labor quality need not be constant. Unmeasured utilization,
input quality, reallocation, and measurement error can all enter the
residual.\footnote{OECD (2001), \textit{Measuring Productivity: OECD Manual},
especially Chapters 2, 4, and 5, on interpretation, labor input, and capital
services. \url{https://doi.org/10.1787/9789264194519-en}.}
For example, if true labor input is $hL$ but the calculation uses only $L$,
then \eqref{eq:facts-accounting} implies
\begin{equation}
g_B^{\mathrm{res}}=g_Y-s_Kg_K-(1-s_K)g_L
=g_B+(1-s_K)g_h.
\end{equation}
Omitted improvements in labor quality appear as TFP growth in that calculation.
A change in the residual therefore calls for a mechanism and measurement checks
before it is interpreted as innovation or technological regress.

\subsection{From accounting to a growth model}

\subsubsection{Level accounting and joint determination}

For a transparent benchmark, assume
\begin{equation}\label{eq:facts-cobb-douglas}
Y=BK^\alpha(hL)^{1-\alpha},\qquad 0<\alpha<1.
\end{equation}
Divide by $L$ and define $y\equiv Y/L$ and $k\equiv K/L$:
\begin{equation}
y=Bk^\alpha h^{1-\alpha}.
\end{equation}
For two economies using the same $\alpha$ and comparable measurements,
\begin{align}
\frac{y_i}{y_j}
&=\frac{B_i}{B_j}\left(\frac{k_i}{k_j}\right)^\alpha
  \left(\frac{h_i}{h_j}\right)^{1-\alpha},\\
\log\frac{y_i}{y_j}
&=\log\frac{B_i}{B_j}+\alpha\log\frac{k_i}{k_j}
 +(1-\alpha)\log\frac{h_i}{h_j}.
\end{align}
This decomposes a level gap; it is distinct from accounting for growth over
time. $B_i/B_j$ is inferred after choosing the production function and measuring
the inputs. The decomposition does not establish three independent causes.

Higher productivity can raise the return to investment and induce more capital
accumulation. Its effects may then appear in both the productivity and capital
terms. Similarly, education, saving, and technology adoption may respond to
common policies or institutions. An accounting contribution records where
output differences appear; a causal explanation specifies what changes first
and how the other variables respond.

\subsubsection{Connecting TFP to labor-augmenting technology}

Chapter~\ref{sec:solow} writes technology as $Y=F(K,AL)$. To connect the two conventions,
set $h=1$ in \eqref{eq:facts-cobb-douglas} and use Cobb--Douglas production:
\begin{align}
Y&=BK^\alpha L^{1-\alpha},\\
Y&=K^\alpha(AL)^{1-\alpha}
  =A^{1-\alpha}K^\alpha L^{1-\alpha}.
\end{align}
The two representations describe the same technology when
\begin{equation}\label{eq:facts-technology-convention}
B=A^{1-\alpha},\qquad g_B=(1-\alpha)g_A.
\end{equation}
This equivalence uses the Cobb--Douglas form and constant $\alpha$; it is not a
general conversion for every production function.

Suppose a balanced growth path has constant $K/Y$, constant $h=1$, and labor
growth $n$. A constant capital-output ratio implies $g_K=g_Y$. Substitution into
\eqref{eq:facts-accounting}, where $s_K=\alpha$, gives
\begin{align}
g_Y&=g_B+\alpha g_Y+(1-\alpha)n,\\
(1-\alpha)(g_Y-n)&=g_B,\\
g_{Y/L}&=\frac{g_B}{1-\alpha}=g_A.
\end{align}
Capital deepening accompanies growth on this path. It does not follow that
capital accumulation supplies an independent permanent growth engine. We have
only derived what the growth rates must satisfy \emph{if} this path exists.

\begin{greenblock}
\textbf{What a growth model must add.}
Growth accounting leaves saving, capital accumulation, labor supply, and
productivity unexplained. Harrod and Domar ask how investment plans and demand
can remain consistent with expanding capacity. Solow begins with a given
saving rate and technology path. Ramsey makes saving an intertemporal choice; OLG changes who saves and
when; endogenous growth models study the forces sustaining productivity or
other reproducible inputs.
\end{greenblock}

For Solow's closed one-good economy without government, feasibility and a
constant saving rate $s$ imply
\begin{equation}
Y=C+I,\qquad I=sY,\qquad \dot K=sY-\delta K.
\end{equation}
Here $I$ is gross investment and $\delta$ is the continuous-time depreciation
rate. Together with production, population growth, and a technology path,
these assumptions determine capital dynamics from an initial stock.
Chapter~\ref{sec:solow} uses that system to study convergence, income levels,
and sustained growth per person. First, Chapter~\ref{sec:harrod-domar} asks how
investment creates demand and capacity, and what is required for a growth path
to remain consistent with firms' plans and full employment.
