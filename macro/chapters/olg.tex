\section{OLG Model}\label{sec:olg}

Ramsey assigns intertemporal choices to a dynasty that continues indefinitely.
Now separate a person's lifetime from the economy's lifetime. People work,
retire, and leave; new people arrive. Saving remains an optimal choice, but
the ownership of assets and the identity of the people paying for transfers
must be tracked by generation.

\subsection{From retirement needs to overlapping generations}

\subsubsection{An economy continues after its current savers have died}

Imagine looking at households' balance sheets at one date. Some people are
building retirement assets from wages; others are spending assets accumulated
earlier. A single consumption total conceals these opposite positions. A policy
that taxes workers and pays retirees changes the resources of different people,
even when it leaves aggregate resources unchanged at the moment of payment.

One response would be to add age labels to the Ramsey household. But if an
altruistic dynasty still pools all resources and values every descendant,
those labels need not change the underlying allocation problem. To isolate
generational turnover, instead let each person value only consumption within
his or her own finite life. Give the young labor income and the old a need to
finance consumption without working. Then ask what assets can carry resources
between these stages, and who supplies the counterpart to those claims.

Two periods are the smallest useful life cycle: one for work and saving, one
for retirement and spending. New workers arrive while earlier workers retire.
The approximation does not describe every age or predict a literal retirement
age. It makes the relation between life-cycle saving, asset ownership, and
population replacement visible with only two age groups.

\subsubsection{Choose a way to finance retirement, then close the economy}

Start with physical capital as the only asset. A young person acquires capital
claims; next period those claims pay rental income and the value of surviving
capital. Retirees consume the proceeds. A wage and a return determine an
individual saving choice, but they are not arbitrary economy-wide inputs:
workers' saving determines the next capital stock, which determines the return
those same workers receive when old. This closes the market model.

An alternative is a transfer from next period's workers. That alternative
cannot simply be assumed to be a private enforceable contract with people who
have not yet been born. A pension system or a government issuing debt adds an
institution capable of continuing across generations. We first solve the
capital-only economy, then introduce those institutions explicitly.

The production economy follows Diamond's OLG framework; the sequence of
questions above is a teaching reconstruction, not a claim about the author's
private thoughts.\footnote{Peter A. Diamond (1965), ``National Debt in a
Neoclassical Growth Model,'' \textit{American Economic Review}, 55(5),
1126--1150. Diamond's original model uses no depreciation; we keep general
$\delta$ and identify the full-depreciation course example separately.
\url{https://www.aeaweb.org/aer/top20/55.5.1126-1150.pdf}.}

\Needspace{12\baselineskip}
\begin{blueblock}
\textbf{Meeting only once does not rule out intertemporal trade.}
A young person can save in capital and consume its payoff when old. That is
already an intertemporal allocation. The important limitation is that currently
living people cannot make ordinary private contracts with unborn generations.
Without bequests or altruistic links, no household represents the interests
of the infinite sequence of future entrants. Finite individual lives and an
infinite economic horizon can therefore have different welfare implications
from a single infinite-lived dynasty.
\end{blueblock}

\subsection{Assumptions and the cohort timeline}\label{sec:olg-assumptions}

The cards state the full environment before solving it. Within a cohort people
are identical; across ages, their income, remaining lifetime, and asset position
differ. There is no single representative person for both age groups.

\begin{modelsetup}[1]
\begin{modelcondition}{01}{Dates, cohorts, and population}
Time is discrete, $t=0,1,\ldots$. A cohort of $L_t$ people is born at each date,
lives for two periods with certainty, and dies after its second period.
At date $t$, $L_t$ young coexist with $L_{t-1}$ old:
\[
L_t=NL_{t-1},\qquad N\equiv1+n>0,\qquad L_t=N^tL_0.
\]
The baseline takes $n\geq0$; the population comparison later also permits
$-1<n<0$ when the other conditions remain feasible. Each young person supplies
one unit of labor inelastically; the old supply none. Labor is $L_t$, whereas
total population is $L_t+L_{t-1}$. A model period is a life stage, not
necessarily a calendar year.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{02}{Goods, capital, and technology growth}
A closed economy produces one good, usable for consumption and capital.
There is no government, foreign trade, money, or public debt in the baseline.
Technology is fixed and normalized to one: $Y_t=F(K_t,L_t)$.
This is an explicit zero-technology-growth benchmark, so $k_t=K_t/L_t$ is
capital per worker, not per effective worker. Capital depreciates at
$0\leq\delta\leq1$ and surviving capital can be sold or consumed:
\[
Y_t+(1-\delta)K_t=C_t+K_{t+1}.
\]
General formulas retain $\delta$. The numerical and explicit full-depreciation
examples set $\delta=1$; that restriction is not needed for the general model.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{03}{Production restrictions}
Firms share a freely available technology. $F$ is continuous, twice
differentiable for positive inputs, positive there, with positive diminishing
marginal products and constant returns:
\[
F(\lambda K,\lambda L)=\lambda F(K,L),\qquad
f(k)\equiv F(k,1),\qquad Y_t/L_t=f(k_t).
\]
For $k>0$, assume
\[
\begin{gathered}
f(0)=0,\qquad f'(k)>0,\qquad f''(k)<0,\\
\lim_{k\downarrow0}f'(k)=\infty,\qquad
\lim_{k\to\infty}f'(k)=0.
\end{gathered}
\]
Cobb--Douglas, $f(k)=k^\alpha$ with $0<\alpha<1$, is a specified example,
not the only technology consistent with these conditions.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{04}{Households, ownership, and initial conditions}
There are many identical, price-taking people in each cohort. They receive
wages when young and asset income when old; firms belong to households and
any profits would accrue to their owners. No one values descendants' utility,
leaves a bequest, or receives an inheritance. The initially old are the
exception to the no-inherited-wealth statement: they enter date zero already
owning the given $K_0>0$, equally within their cohort. The young start with
labor income and no assets. Initially old consumption must be counted even
though their earlier saving decision lies outside the model's initial date.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{05}{Preferences, information, and choices}
A person born at $t$ chooses saving $a_t$ and consumption at ages one and two:
\[
U_t=u(c_{1,t})+\beta u(c_{2,t+1}),\qquad 0<\beta<1,
\]
\[
u(c)=\begin{cases}(c^{1-\theta}-1)/(1-\theta),&\theta>0,\ \theta\ne1,\\
\log c,&\theta=1.
\end{cases}
\]
There is no aggregate or individual uncertainty; the next return is correctly
anticipated. People take wages and returns as given, despite their joint
effect on equilibrium prices. There is no labor choice or liquidity shock.
Consumption is positive. The baseline interior optimum has $0<a_t<w_t$;
no borrowing or short sales of physical capital are allowed. Later pension
formulas must be checked against this asset constraint.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{06}{Firms, factor prices, and returns}
Competitive firms rent capital and hire labor at each date:
\[
\max_{K,L}\;F(K,L)-q_tK-w_tL.
\]
Interior demands imply $q_t=f'(k_t)$ and $w_t=f(k_t)-k_tf'(k_t)$.
Economic profits are zero under constant returns. The owner of a unit of
capital receives the rental payment and its remaining capital value:
\[
R_t=1-\delta+q_t,\qquad r_t=R_t-1=q_t-\delta.
\]
$R_t$ is a gross payoff, $r_t$ a net return, and $q_t$ a rental price.
Only with $\delta=1$ does $R_t=f'(k_t)$.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{07}{Budgets and age-specific consumption}
A person born at $t$ obeys
\[
c_{1,t}+a_t=w_t,\qquad c_{2,t+1}=R_{t+1}a_t.
\]
The old consume all their wealth and leave no assets after death. At date $t$,
$c_{2,t}$ is the consumption of cohort $t-1$, not the future consumption of
cohort $t$. Aggregate consumption and young people's asset purchases are
\[
C_t=L_tc_{1,t}+L_{t-1}c_{2,t},\qquad
\mathcal A_t=L_ta_t.
\]
We use $a_t$ for saving per young person to distinguish it from Solow's saving
share $s$. $\mathcal A_t$ corresponds to the original course notation for
aggregate young saving; it is not automatically national saving.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{08}{Market clearing and the equilibrium path}
Capital and labor demands equal their supplied stocks, $K_t^d=K_t$ and
$L_t^d=L_t$. Capital is the only asset, so young people's wealth at the end of
$t$ must equal next period's capital:
\[
K_{t+1}=\mathcal A_t=L_ta_t,\qquad Nk_{t+1}=a_t.
\]
Goods clearing must also hold:
\[
c_{1,t}+\frac{c_{2,t}}{N}+Nk_{t+1}
=f(k_t)+(1-\delta)k_t.
\]
An equilibrium is a path of allocations and prices satisfying household and
firm optimality, correct return expectations, and these clearing conditions,
starting from the specified initial ownership. No infinite-lived household's
transversality condition is imposed on the economy as a whole.
\end{modelcondition}
\end{modelsetup}

\subsection{Household optimization and life-cycle saving}

\subsubsection{Budgets, multipliers, and the Euler condition}

Take $w_t>0$ and $R_{t+1}>0$ as given. Monotonic utility makes the two budgets
bind. With multipliers on resources remaining, write
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\mathscr L={}&u(c_{1,t})+\beta u(c_{2,t+1})\\
&+\lambda_{1,t}(w_t-c_{1,t}-a_t)
+\lambda_{2,t+1}(R_{t+1}a_t-c_{2,t+1}).
\end{split}
\end{equation}
At an interior optimum,
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial\mathscr L}{\partial c_{1,t}}
 &=u'(c_{1,t})-\lambda_{1,t}=0,\\
\frac{\partial\mathscr L}{\partial c_{2,t+1}}
 &=\beta u'(c_{2,t+1})-\lambda_{2,t+1}=0,\\
\frac{\partial\mathscr L}{\partial a_t}
 &=-\lambda_{1,t}+R_{t+1}\lambda_{2,t+1}=0,\\
u'(c_{1,t})&=\lambda_{1,t},\\
\beta u'(c_{2,t+1})&=\lambda_{2,t+1},\\
\lambda_{1,t}&=R_{t+1}\lambda_{2,t+1}.
\end{align}
Eliminating both multipliers gives
\[
u'(c_{1,t})=\lambda_{1,t}
 =R_{t+1}\lambda_{2,t+1}
 =\beta R_{t+1}u'(c_{2,t+1}).
\]
\begin{equation}\label{eq:olg-euler}
u'(c_{1,t})=\beta R_{t+1}u'(c_{2,t+1}).
\end{equation}
Giving up one consumption unit while young buys $R_{t+1}$ units when old.
The Euler condition prices that marginal transfer. The two budgets still
supply the levels. No infinite-horizon terminal condition is needed for this
person: after age two, there is neither consumption nor a bequest to choose.

\subsubsection{CRRA saving: an explicit solution and its derivatives}

Under CRRA, substitute the budgets into \eqref{eq:olg-euler}:
\begin{align}
(w_t-a_t)^{-\theta}
&=\beta R_{t+1}(R_{t+1}a_t)^{-\theta},\\
\left(\frac{w_t-a_t}{a_t}\right)^{-\theta}
&=\beta R_{t+1}^{1-\theta}.
\end{align}
Define $h(R)\equiv\beta^{-1/\theta}R^{(\theta-1)/\theta}$. Taking powers
and rearranging, step by step,
\begin{align}
\left(\frac{w_t-a_t}{a_t}\right)^{-\theta}
 &=\beta R_{t+1}^{1-\theta},\\
\frac{w_t-a_t}{a_t}
 &=(\beta R_{t+1}^{1-\theta})^{-1/\theta},\\
 &=\beta^{-1/\theta}R_{t+1}^{-(1-\theta)/\theta}
  =h(R_{t+1}),\\
\frac{w_t}{a_t}-1&=h(R_{t+1}),\\
w_t&=a_t[1+h(R_{t+1})].
\end{align}
\begin{equation}
\frac{w_t-a_t}{a_t}=h(R_{t+1}),\qquad
\frac{w_t}{a_t}=1+h(R_{t+1}),
\end{equation}
so the saving function is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:olg-saving}
a_t=a(w_t,R_{t+1})
=\frac{w_t}{1+\beta^{-1/\theta}R_{t+1}^{(\theta-1)/\theta}}.
\end{equation}
The utility constant $-1/(1-\theta)$ has no effect on choices. The log case
will be obtained directly rather than substituting $\theta=1$ into that
utility expression.

Holding the return fixed, differentiate only $w$. Holding the wage fixed,
differentiate the denominator through $h(R)$:
\begin{align}
a_w&=\frac{\partial}{\partial w}[w(1+h(R))^{-1}]
       =(1+h(R))^{-1}\in(0,1),\\
h'(R)&=\beta^{-1/\theta}\frac{\theta-1}{\theta}
                 R^{(\theta-1)/\theta-1},\\
 &=\frac{\theta-1}{\theta R}
       [\beta^{-1/\theta}R^{(\theta-1)/\theta}]
  =\frac{\theta-1}{\theta R}h(R),\\
a_R&=w[-(1+h)^{-2}]h'(R),\\
 &=-\frac{w}{(1+h)^2}\frac{\theta-1}{\theta R}h.
\end{align}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:olg-saving-derivatives}
a_R=-\frac{wh'(R)}{(1+h)^2}
=\frac{1-\theta}{\theta}\frac{wh}{R(1+h)^2}.
\end{equation}
Thus $a_R$ is positive for $\theta<1$, zero for $\theta=1$, and negative
for $\theta>1$. These are partial derivatives; an equilibrium price change
may also change wages and expected future returns together.

\begin{greenblock}
\textbf{A higher return need not raise retirement saving.}
A higher return makes postponing consumption more rewarding, but it also
allows a given retirement expenditure to be financed with fewer assets.
For $\theta<1$, willingness to substitute consumption across ages is strong
and saving rises. For $\theta>1$, the income effect dominates in this
retirement-only-income specification and saving falls. Under log utility,
these effects cancel in the saving share out of wages. The result depends
on the assumed income profile; it is not a universal empirical saving law.
\end{greenblock}

\subsubsection{The log case: a constant share of wages}

For $u(c)=\log c$, \eqref{eq:olg-euler} implies
\begin{equation}
\frac{c_{2,t+1}}{c_{1,t}}=\beta R_{t+1}.
\end{equation}
Substitute $c_{1,t}=w_t-a_t$ and $c_{2,t+1}=R_{t+1}a_t$:
\begin{align}
R_{t+1}a_t&=\beta R_{t+1}(w_t-a_t),\\
a_t&=\beta w_t-\beta a_t\qquad(R_{t+1}>0),\\
(1+\beta)a_t&=\beta w_t,\\
a_t&=\frac{\beta}{1+\beta}w_t.
\end{align}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:olg-log-saving}
R_{t+1}a_t=\beta R_{t+1}(w_t-a_t)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 a_t=\kappa w_t,\qquad \kappa\equiv\frac{\beta}{1+\beta}.
\end{equation}
Saving is endogenous even though this example gives a constant share of
\emph{wages}. Solow imposes a share of total output; those are different
income bases. Log utility does not make retirement consumption independent
of the return: $c_{2,t+1}=R_{t+1}\kappa w_t$ still changes with $R_{t+1}$.

\subsection{Firm optimization and the return to ownership}

At date $t$, firms choose $K,L$ in
\begin{equation}
\max_{K,L}\;F(K,L)-q_tK-w_tL.
\end{equation}
Differentiate with respect to actual factor inputs before using constant
returns to express the prices per worker:
\begin{align}
F(K,L)&=Lf(K/L)=Lf(k),\\
\pi_K&=Lf'(k)(1/L)-q_t=f'(k)-q_t=0,\\
\pi_L&=f(k)+Lf'(k)(-K/L^2)-w_t=0,\\
q_t&=f'(k_t),\qquad w_t=f(k_t)-k_tf'(k_t),\\
R_t&=(1-\delta)+q_t.
\end{align}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:olg-prices}
q_t=f'(k_t),\qquad
w_t=f(k_t)-k_tf'(k_t),\qquad
R_t=1-\delta+f'(k_t).
\end{equation}
Euler's theorem gives $Y_t=q_tK_t+w_tL_t$, so profits are zero after factor
payments. Capital owners still receive both rental income and the value of
surviving assets. That second item is an asset sale or liquidation, not
additional production in GDP.

For Cobb--Douglas,
\begin{equation}
q_t=\alpha k_t^{\alpha-1},\qquad
w_t=(1-\alpha)k_t^\alpha,\qquad
R_t=1-\delta+\alpha k_t^{\alpha-1}.
\end{equation}
A larger inherited capital intensity raises the current wage and lowers the
current return. A young saver cares about the \emph{next} return, determined
by $k_{t+1}$, because that is when the retirement payoff is received.

\Needspace{12\baselineskip}
\begin{blueblock}
\textbf{The course formula $R=f'(k)$ is a full-depreciation case.}
With $\delta=1$, no capital survives after production, so the gross payoff
is the rental product alone. With $\delta=0$, the owner also recovers the
unit of capital and $R=1+f'(k)$. Both conventions can be useful. The
household Euler equation uses the gross payoff; replacing it by a rental
price without stating depreciation changes the saving problem.
\end{blueblock}

\subsection{Capital accumulation and equilibrium dynamics}

\subsubsection{Young wealth versus aggregate saving}

At the start of date $t$, the old own $K_t$. Since
$K_t=L_{t-1}a_{t-1}$, their aggregate consumption is
$L_{t-1}c_{2,t}=R_tK_t$; the same relation holds for the initial owners at
date zero. The young consume $L_t(w_t-a_t)$. Add them:
\begin{align}
C_t&=L_tw_t-L_ta_t+R_tK_t,\\
&=Y_t-L_ta_t+(1-\delta)K_t.
\end{align}
In the second line, substitute $R_t=q_t+1-\delta$ and
$Y_t=w_tL_t+q_tK_t$. Rearranging gives
\begin{align}
Y_t-C_t&=L_ta_t-(1-\delta)K_t,\\
K_{t+1}&=(1-\delta)K_t+(Y_t-C_t),\\
 &=(1-\delta)K_t+L_ta_t-(1-\delta)K_t=L_ta_t,\\
\frac{K_{t+1}}{L_{t+1}}\frac{L_{t+1}}{L_t}
 &=\frac{L_ta_t}{L_t},\\
Nk_{t+1}&=a_t.
\end{align}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:olg-capital-market}
K_{t+1}=L_ta_t,\qquad Nk_{t+1}=a_t.
\end{equation}
Thus the asset-clearing equation also satisfies goods clearing.

\Needspace{19\baselineskip}
\begin{blueblock}
\textbf{Young saving is not national saving.}
The young acquire assets, while the old liquidate theirs. In this one-good
model, gross investment and net national saving are respectively
\[
I_t\equiv K_{t+1}-(1-\delta)K_t
=L_ta_t-(1-\delta)K_t,
\]
\[
S_t^{\mathrm{net}}\equiv Y_t-C_t-\delta K_t
=K_{t+1}-K_t=L_ta_t-K_t.
\]
Writing $K_{t+1}=(1-\delta)K_t+L_ta_t$ would count surviving capital twice:
young people's purchases already include acquiring that capital from the
old. With full depreciation, gross investment does equal young saving;
with general depreciation, the distinction must remain visible.
\end{blueblock}

\subsubsection{An implicit law of motion under general preferences}

Substitute the saving function and competitive prices into capital clearing:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:olg-implicit-map}
Nk_{t+1}
=a\!\left(f(k_t)-k_tf'(k_t),\ 1-\delta+f'(k_{t+1})\right).
\end{equation}
This is generally implicit in $k_{t+1}$: a larger capital stock changes the
return that induced people to accumulate it. Solving household saving at an
arbitrary fixed return is not yet a general-equilibrium solution.

At a positive stationary equilibrium,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:olg-general-steady}
Nk^*=a\!\left(f(k^*)-k^*f'(k^*),\ 1-\delta+f'(k^*)\right).
\end{equation}
Cobb--Douglas makes this condition
\begin{equation}
Nk^*=
\frac{(1-\alpha)(k^*)^\alpha}
{1+\beta^{-1/\theta}[1-\delta+\alpha(k^*)^{\alpha-1}]^{(\theta-1)/\theta}}.
\end{equation}
This scalar equation replaces the unfinished general steady-state expression
in the course notes. A numerical root may be needed; the explicit log result
below is a separate tractable case.

To see why stability needs conditions, differentiate
\eqref{eq:olg-implicit-map}. Since $w'(k)=-kf''(k)>0$,
\begin{align}
w'(k)&=f'(k)-[f'(k)+kf''(k)]=-kf''(k),\\
N\frac{dk_{t+1}}{dk_t}
 &=a_w w'(k_t)+a_R R'(k_{t+1})\frac{dk_{t+1}}{dk_t},\\
 &=a_w[-k_tf''(k_t)]
    +a_R f''(k_{t+1})\frac{dk_{t+1}}{dk_t},\\
\left[N-a_Rf''(k_{t+1})\right]\frac{dk_{t+1}}{dk_t}
 &=a_w[-k_tf''(k_t)].
\end{align}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:olg-map-slope}
\left[N-a_R f''(k_{t+1})\right]\frac{dk_{t+1}}{dk_t}
=a_w[-k_tf''(k_t)].
\end{equation}
If the bracket is nonzero, this gives the local slope. A locally defined map
is stable at its steady state when the absolute slope is below one.
For $\theta\leq1$, $a_R\geq0$ makes the bracket positive. For $\theta>1$,
the return response can overturn that sign or make the equation locally
singular. The log example's uniqueness and convergence cannot be asserted
for every OLG specification.

\subsubsection{Log utility and Cobb--Douglas: solve the whole path}

Using \eqref{eq:olg-log-saving}, capital clearing reduces to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:olg-log-map}
k_{t+1}=\frac{\kappa(1-\alpha)}{N}k_t^\alpha
\equiv M k_t^\alpha,
\qquad M\equiv\frac{\kappa(1-\alpha)}{N}>0.
\end{equation}
This formula holds for general $\delta$: log saving does not respond to the
return, and current wages do not depend on depreciation. Depreciation still
changes the old's consumption and welfare.

At a positive steady state, divide $k^*=M(k^*)^\alpha$ by $(k^*)^\alpha$:
\begin{align}
\frac{k^*}{(k^*)^\alpha}&=M,\\
(k^*)^{1-\alpha}&=M,\\
k^*&=M^{1/(1-\alpha)},\\
M&=\frac{\kappa(1-\alpha)}{N}
   =\frac{\beta(1-\alpha)}{(1+\beta)N}.
\end{align}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:olg-log-steady}
(k^*)^{1-\alpha}=M,\qquad
k^*=\left[\frac{\beta(1-\alpha)}{(1+\beta)N}\right]^{1/(1-\alpha)}.
\end{equation}
The map is increasing; $k_{t+1}/k_t=Mk_t^{\alpha-1}$ exceeds one below $k^*$
and is below one above it. It maps either side toward the steady state without
crossing it. Thus every $k_0>0$ converges monotonically; zero is an absorbing
boundary, not the initial condition of the positive-capital result.

There is also an exact solution in log deviations. Divide the map by its
steady-state version and take logarithms:
\begin{align}
\frac{k_{t+1}}{k^*}
 &=\frac{Mk_t^\alpha}{M(k^*)^\alpha}
  =\left(\frac{k_t}{k^*}\right)^\alpha,\\
\log(k_{t+1}/k^*)&=\alpha\log(k_t/k^*),\\
\log(k_t/k^*)&=\alpha^t\log(k_0/k^*),\\
k_t/k^*&=\exp[\alpha^t\log(k_0/k^*)]
        =(k_0/k^*)^{\alpha^t}.
\end{align}
\begin{equation}
\log(k_{t+1}/k^*)=\alpha\log(k_t/k^*),\qquad
k_t=k^*\left(\frac{k_0}{k^*}\right)^{\alpha^t}.
\end{equation}
The local slope is $\alpha\in(0,1)$. Capital-level deviations satisfy
$k_t-k^*\approx\alpha^t(k_0-k^*)$ only locally; log deviations decay exactly
at that factor. This separates convergence of a path from efficiency of the
allocation to which it converges.

\begin{greenblock}
\textbf{What the log example does and does not establish.}
Young people optimally save a fixed share of wages. Wages depend on the
inherited stock; their asset purchases become the next stock. This gives a
unique positive steady state and a stable path in this example. It does not
show that all OLG economies converge, that capital is socially optimal, or
that a household's constant saving share equals Solow's imposed output share.
\end{greenblock}

\Needspace{21\baselineskip}
\subsection{Comparing Solow, Ramsey, and OLG}

Use a common discrete-time benchmark here: fixed technology, population
factor $N$, and depreciation $\delta$. Let $\sigma$ denote Solow's imposed
saving share to keep it distinct from individual saving $a_t$.

The corresponding aggregate resource and accumulation conditions are
\begin{align}
\text{Solow:}\quad
Nk_{t+1}&=(1-\delta)k_t+\sigma f(k_t),\\
\text{Ramsey:}\quad
Nk_{t+1}&=f(k_t)+(1-\delta)k_t-c_t,\\
\text{OLG:}\quad
Nk_{t+1}&=a(w_t,R_{t+1})\\
&=f(k_t)+(1-\delta)k_t-c_{1,t}-c_{2,t}/N.
\end{align}
In the Ramsey row, $c_t$ is aggregate dynasty consumption per worker. Its
population-weighted objective in this discrete convention gives the
dynasty Euler equation $u'(c_t)=\beta R_{t+1}u'(c_{t+1})$, together with an
appropriate infinite-horizon terminal condition. The OLG Euler equation
instead links two ages of one person. The equations look similar but have
different coverage of future generations.

\Needspace{10\baselineskip}
\begin{blueblock}
\textbf{The common resource constraint does not make the models identical.}
In OLG the old spend asset income and the young buy assets, so clearing gives
$Nk_{t+1}=a_t$. In Ramsey the continuing household chooses its next wealth
while valuing its members' future consumption. Solow specifies saving without
that optimization. The distinction is who makes the choice and whose future
is included, not whether one model respects accounting and another does not.
\end{blueblock}

\subsection{Dynamic efficiency and intergenerational welfare}

\subsubsection{Count consumption at a date before comparing steady states}

Divide goods clearing by $L_t$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:olg-resources}
c_{1,t}+\frac{c_{2,t}}{N}+Nk_{t+1}
=f(k_t)+(1-\delta)k_t.
\end{equation}
The factor $1/N=L_{t-1}/L_t$ counts retirees relative to workers. Define
$\bar c_t\equiv C_t/L_t=c_{1,t}+c_{2,t}/N$. This is consumption per worker,
not consumption per resident; the latter is $\bar c_t/(1+1/N)$.
At a stationary capital intensity,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:olg-steady-consumption}
\bar c(k)=f(k)-(n+\delta)k.
\end{equation}
When $n+\delta>0$, steady consumption per worker is maximized at
\begin{equation}\label{eq:olg-golden-rule}
f'(k_{\mathrm{GR}})=n+\delta,
\qquad R_{\mathrm{GR}}=1-\delta+f'(k_{\mathrm{GR}})=N.
\end{equation}
For Cobb--Douglas,
$k_{\mathrm{GR}}=[\alpha/(n+\delta)]^{1/(1-\alpha)}$.
If $n+\delta\leq0$, there is no finite interior maximum under $f'(k)>0$;
the positive steady-state overaccumulation comparison below then does not apply.

The course condition $f'(k_{\mathrm{GR}})=1+n$ is exactly
\eqref{eq:olg-golden-rule} with $\delta=1$. The comparable net-return statement
is $r_{\mathrm{GR}}=n$, not $R_{\mathrm{GR}}=n$.

\subsubsection{Why overaccumulation can make every generation worse off}

Suppose a competitive steady state satisfies $k^*>k_{\mathrm{GR}}$.
Diminishing marginal product makes this equivalent to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:olg-inefficiency}
f'(k^*)<n+\delta
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad R^*<N
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad r^*<n.
\end{equation}
The economy gives up so many goods to maintain its capital stock that a
slightly smaller stock could support more consumption indefinitely. To show
why this is more than a comparison of eventual outcomes, construct a feasible
change from the same initial capital.

\Needspace{25\baselineskip}
\begin{redblock}
\textbf{Question: can consuming more today avoid a sacrifice tomorrow?}
Start at $k_0=k^*>k_{\mathrm{GR}}$. Choose $k^{a}=k^*-\varepsilon$
with $k_{\mathrm{GR}}<k^{a}<k^*$, and set the next and all later
capital intensities to $k^{a}$. At date zero, inherited output and
capital are unchanged, while required next capital falls by $N\varepsilon$
per worker. Hence
\[
\bar c^{a}_0-\bar c^*=N\varepsilon>0.
\]
At all later dates,
\[
\bar c^{a}_t-\bar c^*
=\big[f(k^{a})-(n+\delta)k^{a}\big]
 -\big[f(k^*)-(n+\delta)k^*\big]>0.
\]
The inequality follows because $\bar c(k)$ decreases to the right of
$k_{\mathrm{GR}}$. At each date, a positive gain $\Delta\bar c_t$ can be
split as $\Delta c_{1,t}=\Delta\bar c_t/2$ and
$\Delta c_{2,t}=N\Delta\bar c_t/2$. Both currently living age groups gain,
and every future cohort gains at both ages. This is a feasible Pareto
improvement, including for the initially old, rather than a claim that
higher future consumption compensates losers today.
\end{redblock}

The construction changes allocations through a coordinated transfer and
investment policy; it is not the action of one price-taking saver.
At given prices, each person's saving is optimal. Yet no person chooses
on behalf of the infinite sequence of entrants. Competitive optimization
within each finite life therefore does not impose the dynasty terminal
restriction that rules out this overaccumulation in the Ramsey benchmark.
An overlapping-generations structure permits inefficiency; it does not
make every stationary equilibrium inefficient.

\subsubsection{An explicit log example and the three-model comparison}

In the full-depreciation log/Cobb--Douglas example, substitute
\eqref{eq:olg-log-steady} into the return:
\begin{align}
(k^*)^{1-\alpha}&=\frac{\kappa(1-\alpha)}{N},\\
(k^*)^{\alpha-1}&=\frac{N}{\kappa(1-\alpha)},\\
R^*&=\frac{\alpha N}{\kappa(1-\alpha)}.
\end{align}
All quantities divided below are positive. Thus
\begin{align}
R^*<N
&\Longleftrightarrow\frac{\alpha}{\kappa(1-\alpha)}<1,\\
&\Longleftrightarrow\alpha<\kappa(1-\alpha),\\
&\Longleftrightarrow\alpha(1+\beta)<\beta(1-\alpha),\\
&\Longleftrightarrow\alpha+\alpha\beta<\beta-\alpha\beta,\\
&\Longleftrightarrow\alpha(1+2\beta)<\beta,\\
&\Longleftrightarrow\alpha<\frac{\beta}{1+2\beta}.
\end{align}
\begin{align}
R^*=\alpha(k^*)^{\alpha-1}
&=\frac{\alpha N}{\kappa(1-\alpha)},\\
R^*<N
&\Longleftrightarrow \alpha<\kappa(1-\alpha)
\Longleftrightarrow \alpha<\frac{\beta}{1+2\beta}.
\end{align}
Both efficient and overaccumulating cases are possible by changing the
parameters. This is not an automatic consequence of finite lifetimes.

For illustration, take $\alpha=0.25$, $\beta=0.96$, $N=1.02$, and
$\delta=1$. The formulas give $k^*\simeq0.2562$,
$k_{\mathrm{GR}}\simeq0.1534$, and $R^*\simeq0.6942<N$.
Steady consumption per worker is about $0.4501$ at $k^*$, versus $0.4694$
at the Golden Rule stock. These are hypothetical life-stage parameters,
not annual estimates or a calibration to a particular pension system.
The Pareto argument above supplies the transition comparison; the two
steady consumption numbers alone would not establish it.

For Solow with fixed technology and $n+\delta>0$, the Cobb--Douglas stocks are
\begin{equation}
k^*_{S}=\left(\frac{\sigma}{n+\delta}\right)^{1/(1-\alpha)},
\qquad
k_{\mathrm{GR}}=\left(\frac{\alpha}{n+\delta}\right)^{1/(1-\alpha)}.
\end{equation}
Thus overaccumulation occurs if $\sigma>\alpha$, and is not inevitable.
The stock comparisons use the same positive power $1-\alpha$:
\begin{align}
\sigma(k_S^*)^\alpha&=(n+\delta)k_S^*,\\
(k_S^*)^{1-\alpha}&=\frac{\sigma}{n+\delta},\\
\alpha k_{\mathrm{GR}}^{\alpha-1}&=n+\delta,\\
k_{\mathrm{GR}}^{1-\alpha}&=\frac{\alpha}{n+\delta},\\
k_S^*>k_{\mathrm{GR}}&\Longleftrightarrow\sigma>\alpha.
\end{align}
For the population-weighted Ramsey dynasty in the common discrete benchmark,
stationary consumption and its Euler equation instead give
\begin{equation}
1=\beta R^*,\qquad R^*=1/\beta.
\end{equation}
In the course special case $N=1$, the algebra also reads
\begin{align}
1&=\beta[1-\delta+f'(k^*)],\\
1+\rho&=1-\delta+f'(k^*)\qquad(\beta=1/(1+\rho)),\\
f'(k^*)&=\rho+\delta>\delta=f'(k_{\mathrm{GR}}),\\
k^*&<k_{\mathrm{GR}}\qquad(f''<0).
\end{align}
With population growth restored, compare $R^*=1/\beta$ with
$R_{\mathrm{GR}}=N$, rather than keeping the $N=1$ Golden Rule unchanged.
Under $\beta N<1$, the finite-welfare restriction for this stationary
benchmark, $R^*>N$ and $k^*<k_{\mathrm{GR}}$. This matches the role of the
effective-discount restriction in the previous continuous-time chapter.
The protection comes from the dynasty objective and admissibility conditions,
not simply from the existence of an Euler equation. OLG has an Euler equation
too, but it links two different age consumptions, which need not be equal
even at a stationary equilibrium.

\subsubsection{A planner needs weights across cohorts, not just within a life}

The private discount factor $\beta$ compares the two ages of one person.
A planner also needs a rule for weighing different birth cohorts. To retain
that distinction, let cohort weights be $\omega_t=\chi^t$, with $\chi>0$,
and consider
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\max\;&\omega_{-1}\beta L_{-1}u(c_{2,0})\\
&+\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\omega_tL_t
 \big[u(c_{1,t})+\beta u(c_{2,t+1})\big],
\end{split}
\end{equation}
subject to \eqref{eq:olg-resources}, given $k_0$, and feasible positive
consumption. The first term explicitly includes the initially old; their
young-age utility is already sunk. For the stationary comparisons here,
$\chi N<1$ is a sufficient summability restriction. These weights are a
normative choice, not a preference inferred from household saving.

Let $\mu_t$ be the multiplier on the aggregate date-$t$ resource constraint.
The aggregate resource constraint gives the following derivatives:
\begin{align}
0&=\omega_tL_tu'(c_{1,t})-\mu_tL_t,\\
0&=\beta\omega_{t-1}L_{t-1}u'(c_{2,t})-\mu_tL_{t-1},\\
0&=-\mu_t+\mu_{t+1}[f'(k_{t+1})+1-\delta].
\end{align}
Cancel $L_t$ from the first equation and $L_{t-1}$ from the second. Then
\begin{align}
\mu_t&=\omega_tu'(c_{1,t})
 =\beta\omega_{t-1}u'(c_{2,t}),\\
\mu_t&=R_{t+1}\mu_{t+1}.
\end{align}
Using the age-two condition at $t+1$ recovers the within-cohort Euler equation:
\begin{align}
\mu_{t+1}&=\beta\omega_tu'(c_{2,t+1}),\\
\omega_tu'(c_{1,t})&=R_{t+1}\beta\omega_tu'(c_{2,t+1}),\\
u'(c_{1,t})&=\beta R_{t+1}u'(c_{2,t+1}).
\end{align}
At an interior stationary planner
allocation, however, $\mu_{t+1}/\mu_t=\chi$, so its capital condition is
$R^*=1/\chi$. Changing social cohort weights changes the planner problem.
The ratio follows directly from constant young consumption:
\begin{align}
\frac{\mu_{t+1}}{\mu_t}
 &=\frac{\omega_{t+1}u'(c_1^*)}{\omega_tu'(c_1^*)}=\chi,\\
1&=R^*\frac{\mu_{t+1}}{\mu_t}=R^*\chi,\\
R^*&=1/\chi.
\end{align}
Maximizing $\bar c(k)$ across stationary paths supplies the Golden Rule;
it is not the solution of every weighted lifetime-welfare problem. Moreover,
a planner optimum under one set of weights and a Pareto improvement for all
cohorts are different claims.

\subsection{Population, pensions, and public debt}\label{sec:olg-policy}

The benchmark now gives a way to examine how retirement is financed.
Introduce each arrangement separately. Taxes and benefits below are lump-sum;
there is still no uncertainty, valued government consumption, or endogenous
retirement. A more realistic pension system would need those mechanisms
before making quantitative policy recommendations.

\subsubsection{Population change: a support ratio and a capital effect}

With two age groups, the number of retirees per worker is $1/N$. A lower
$N$ raises that ratio. In the log/Cobb--Douglas capital-only steady state,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial\log k^*}{\partial\log N}=-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}.
\end{equation}
Lower cohort growth spreads saving over fewer future workers, raising
long-run capital per worker, increasing wages and reducing returns. With
$A=1$, this is a comparison of stationary levels; it is not permanently
faster productivity growth. An unexpected change in cohort size also changes
labor supply and the ratio $K/L$ while inherited aggregate $K$ is fixed;
its full transition must track when the affected cohorts enter work.

Real population aging can also reflect longer lives, changing employment,
migration, and retirement ages. Those channels are not all captured by a
change in $N$. The support ratio is a useful first accounting step, not a
complete empirical explanation of aging.

\subsubsection{Pay-as-you-go pensions: current workers finance current retirees}

Let each young person pay $T_t\geq0$ and each old person receive $P_t$.
A balanced pension budget is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:olg-payg-budget}
L_tT_t=L_{t-1}P_t,\qquad P_t=NT_t.
\end{equation}
A cohort paying at $t$ receives the benefit at $t+1$, so its budgets become
\begin{equation}
c_{1,t}+a_t=w_t-T_t,\qquad
c_{2,t+1}=R_{t+1}a_t+P_{t+1}.
\end{equation}
Private assets still satisfy $Nk_{t+1}=a_t$: the tax paid to retirees is not
an additional physical asset.\footnote{For the pension budget and the contrast
with funded accounts, see MIT 14.472, \textit{Public Economics II} (2004),
``Social Security---OLG Settings,''
\href{https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/14-472-public-economics-ii-spring-2004/resources/socisecurolgsett-1/}{lecture notes}.}

Under log utility, the interior saving choice follows from
\begin{align}
\frac{1}{w_t-T_t-a_t}
&=\frac{\beta R_{t+1}}{R_{t+1}a_t+P_{t+1}},\\
R_{t+1}a_t+P_{t+1}
 &=\beta R_{t+1}(w_t-T_t-a_t),\\
R_{t+1}a_t+\beta R_{t+1}a_t
 &=\beta R_{t+1}(w_t-T_t)-P_{t+1},\\
(1+\beta)R_{t+1}a_t
&=\beta R_{t+1}(w_t-T_t)-P_{t+1},\\
a_t&=\kappa(w_t-T_t)
-\frac{P_{t+1}}{(1+\beta)R_{t+1}}.
\label{eq:olg-payg-saving}
\end{align}
Current contributions reduce disposable wages, and expected benefits reduce
the assets needed for retirement. Both lower private saving at given prices.
If this expression is negative, the no-borrowing restriction binds at $a_t=0$;
the interior formula must not be used beyond its feasible range.

A permanent, constant contribution $T$ gives a future benefit $NT$.
At fixed prices its effect on present-value lifetime resources is
\begin{equation}
(w_t-T)+\frac{NT}{R_{t+1}}-w_t
=T\left(\frac{N}{R_{t+1}}-1\right).
\end{equation}
This is positive if $N>R_{t+1}$. The initially old can receive a windfall,
and suitable transfers can improve an overaccumulating allocation. But this
fixed-price comparison is not a proof that every contribution rate benefits
every cohort: equilibrium wages, returns, asset constraints, and the entire
transition must also be checked. In an efficient benchmark, reducing capital
need not create the resources needed to compensate all generations.

\begin{blueblock}
\textbf{A pension's implicit return depends on its funding rule.}
The gross return $N$ above assumes a constant real contribution per worker.
For a constant wage contribution rate $\tau$, $T_t=\tau w_t$ and
$P_{t+1}/T_t=Nw_{t+1}/w_t$. Wage growth then matters as well as cohort size.
Holding benefits fixed instead, \eqref{eq:olg-payg-budget} requires
$T_t=P_t/N$: fewer workers per retiree raise the required contribution.
These are accounting relations, not risk-adjusted comparisons with observed
financial yields. Benefits, wages, and taxes in actual pension systems are
not all fixed or certain.
\end{blueblock}

\subsubsection{Funded pensions: distinguish compulsory from total saving}

Suppose a mandatory contribution $D_t$ is invested in capital and earns the
same return as voluntary assets. Benefits are $R_{t+1}D_t$. The household faces
\begin{equation}
c_{1,t}+a_t=w_t-D_t,\qquad
c_{2,t+1}=R_{t+1}(a_t+D_t).
\end{equation}
Define total retirement assets $A_t^h\equiv a_t+D_t$. Substitution gives
$c_{1,t}+A_t^h=w_t$ and $c_{2,t+1}=R_{t+1}A_t^h$, exactly the original
lifetime budget. If voluntary saving can adjust without a binding constraint,
a household offsets a one-unit compulsory contribution by one unit less
voluntary saving. Total assets, capital, and consumption are unchanged:
\begin{equation}
Nk_{t+1}=A_t^h,\qquad A_t^h=\kappa w_t
\quad\text{under log utility and an interior offset.}
\end{equation}
If $a_t\geq0$ binds, the contribution exceeds desired assets, or the account
has different returns, fees, insurance, or tax treatment, this neutrality need
not hold. A funded account and a transfer to the current old cannot be treated
as the same capital-market flow.

Moving from pay-as-you-go to funding also leaves a transition question:
who finances benefits already promised to existing retirees while workers
build their own accounts? A comparison of eventual fund balances alone
cannot determine the welfare of the transition cohorts.

\subsubsection{Public debt: a retirement asset with a fiscal counterpart}

Now consider government bonds instead of a pension program. Let $B_t$ denote
principal issued at $t-1$, due to its old holders at date $t$. New bonds
$B_{t+1}$ are sold to the young for their principal amount at date $t$.
Deterministic bonds and physical capital must offer the same gross payoff
$R_{t+1}$ if both are held. With no government purchases, a lump-sum tax
$T_t^D$ per young person satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:olg-government-debt}
R_tB_t=L_tT_t^D+B_{t+1}.
\end{equation}
A negative tax is a transfer. This budget identifies who pays the old
bondholders; a bond is not an extra good available for consumption.

Asset clearing and its per-worker version become
\begin{equation}\label{eq:olg-debt-assets}
L_ta_t=K_{t+1}+B_{t+1},\qquad
 a_t=N(k_{t+1}+b_{t+1}),\quad b_t\equiv B_t/L_t.
\end{equation}
The fiscal budget is $T_t^D=R_tb_t-Nb_{t+1}$. Consolidating households and
government still gives \eqref{eq:olg-resources}: public liabilities cancel
public claims, while physical capital remains a productive resource.
Debt changes portfolios and intergenerational payments, not the resource
identity itself. Its financing matters because the young and the old are
different people without offsetting bequests.

For a constant debt stock per worker, $b_t=b$, the tax is
$T_t^D=(R_t-N)b$. If $R_t<N$, new issuance can cover debt service and a
transfer at these model prices; if $R_t>N$, maintaining that debt ratio
requires a positive tax. This does not establish that an arbitrary debt
path is feasible: returns respond to capital, and all future budgets and
household choices must hold.

Under log utility, combine $a_t=\kappa(w_t-T_t^D)$ with asset clearing:
\begin{align}
N(k_{t+1}+b)&=\kappa[w(k_t)-(R(k_t)-N)b],\\
Nk_{t+1}&=\kappa w(k_t)-\kappa R(k_t)b+\kappa Nb-Nb,\\
 &=\kappa w(k_t)-[\kappa R(k_t)+(1-\kappa)N]b.
\end{align}
\begin{equation}
Nk_{t+1}=\kappa w(k_t)
-\big[\kappa R(k_t)+(1-\kappa)N\big]b.
\end{equation}
Near the no-debt log/Cobb--Douglas steady state, a small permanent increase
in $b$ reduces stationary capital. Differentiating the stationary equation
at $b=0$ gives
the following chain. Differentiate before setting $b$ to zero:
\begin{align}
N\frac{dk^*}{db}
 &=\kappa w'(k^*)\frac{dk^*}{db}
   -\kappa R'(k^*)b\frac{dk^*}{db}
   -[\kappa R(k^*)+(1-\kappa)N],\\
\left[N-\kappa w'(k^*)\right]
 \left.\frac{dk^*}{db}\right|_{b=0}
 &=-[\kappa R^*+(1-\kappa)N].
\end{align}
\begin{equation}
\left.\frac{dk^*}{db}\right|_{b=0}
=-\frac{\kappa R^*+(1-\kappa)N}{N-\kappa w'(k^*)}<0,
\qquad N-\kappa w'(k^*)=N(1-\alpha)>0.
\end{equation}
Indeed, $w'(k)=\alpha(1-\alpha)k^{\alpha-1}$ and the no-debt steady
state gives $\kappa(1-\alpha)(k^*)^{\alpha-1}=N$.
Thus $\kappa w'(k^*)=\alpha N$ and the denominator is
$N-\alpha N=N(1-\alpha)$.
This is a local result in the stated example, including its tax-financing
rule, rather than a universal one-for-one displacement of capital by bonds.
Whether the reduction helps welfare depends on initial accumulation and
on how gains and costs are distributed along the transition.

\Needspace{12\baselineskip}
\begin{greenblock}
\textbf{Who holds the asset, and who supplies its payoff?}
Physical capital pays from production and surviving assets. A funded pension
owns such assets on the worker's behalf. A pay-as-you-go pension pays from
current contributors, while government bonds pay from taxes and new issuance.
All can help a person finance retirement, but they need not have the same
effect on productive capital or on different generations' consumption.
Tracing both sides of the balance sheet is the central OLG lesson.
\end{greenblock}

OLG makes retirement finance and population replacement part of the equilibrium,
and shows why individually optimal saving need not guarantee intergenerational
efficiency. It still takes productivity as given and abstracts from realistic
lifetimes, risk, labor choices, and within-cohort inequality. The next growth
chapter changes the source of productivity and long-run growth; later fiscal
chapters can build richer policy problems on the cohort budgets established here.
