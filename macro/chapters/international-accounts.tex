\section{International Accounts and Intertemporal Trade}\label{sec:international-accounts}

\subsection{What changes when an economy opens?}

A household in Ramsey already buys goods, sells labor, and holds assets.
A firm already sells output and buys inputs. More markets alone therefore do
not explain what makes an economy open. The new question is: \emph{which
transactions can cross the boundary of the economy we are studying?}

Imagine a region whose harvest is poor this year but good next year. First
try a familiar explanation: its households borrow from its banks. This can
redistribute purchasing power within the region, but it cannot create food
that no resident currently has. Consolidate residents' balance sheets: one
resident's loan asset cancels another's liability. If the region as a whole
wants more goods than it currently produces, someone outside it must supply
them. That supplier needs payment now, a claim on future delivery, or a transfer.
The missing objects are external goods flows and external claims.

This is a teaching reconstruction of the modeling problem, not a claim about
an author's private thoughts. We begin with one good and two dates so that
neither product variety nor specialization can explain the result. The
intertemporal approach links spending to lifetime resources and the price of
moving resources across time.\footnote{The endowment and production benchmarks
follow the framework in Schmitt-Groh\'e, Uribe, and Woodford (2022),
\href{https://www.columbia.edu/~mu2166/UIM/slides_endowment.pdf}{Chapter 3}
and \href{https://www.columbia.edu/~mu2166/UIM/slides_production.pdf}{Chapter 5}
slides for \textit{International Macroeconomics: A Modern Approach}.
The worked economies, figures, and extensions below are constructed here.}

\Needspace{9\baselineskip}
\begin{blueblock}
\textbf{Openness, comparative advantage, and size answer different questions.}
Openness specifies which cross-border exchanges are feasible. Comparative
advantage explains a source of gains from exchanging different goods.
Country size specifies whether domestic choices affect world prices.
A large country can affect an equilibrium world interest rate even when every
household and firm is competitive. This is distinct from an individual firm's
market power. None of these concepts implies the other two.
\end{blueblock}

The boundary is analytical. Two regions may trade inside a closed national
model; two countries may trade inside a closed world model. A country can
also trade goods extensively while restricting external borrowing. Financial
openness is not synonymous with complete insurance, unrestricted capital
mobility, or the absence of trade costs. We must specify the transactions,
assets, and constraints rather than attach all of them to the word ``open.''

\subsubsection{A first exchange without comparative advantage}

Consider countries $A$ and $B$, each with one representative household. There
is one perishable good, no investment, no government, and initially no debt.
Their endowments at dates 1 and 2 are
\begin{equation}
 (Q_1^A,Q_2^A)=(1,3),\qquad (Q_1^B,Q_2^B)=(3,1).
\end{equation}
Under autarky there is no storage or external exchange, so each consumes its
own endowment. With a gross borrowing return $R=1$, consider this allocation:
\begin{align}
 (C_1^A,C_2^A)&=(2,2), & B_1^A&=1-2=-1,\\
 (C_1^B,C_2^B)&=(2,2), & B_1^B&=3-2=1.
\end{align}
A positive $B_1$ is a claim held at the end of date 1; a negative $B_1$ is debt.
Country $B$ sends one good to $A$ today in exchange for one good tomorrow.
Check both the physical allocation and the promise:
\begin{align}
 C_1^A+C_1^B&=2+2=1+3=Q_1^A+Q_1^B,\\
 C_2^A+C_2^B&=2+2=3+1=Q_2^A+Q_2^B,\\
 B_1^A+B_1^B&=-1+1=0.
\end{align}
For preferences $\log C_1+\log C_2$, each household's utility increases by
\begin{equation}
 [\log2+\log2]-[\log1+\log3]=\log(4/3)>0.
\end{equation}
We will derive the choices and the world return below. The preliminary lesson
is already visible: trade can exchange the \emph{dates} of consumption even
when there are no different goods in which to specialize.

\subsection{Trade across time: the two-period economy}

\subsubsection{Conditions and the order of decisions}

We now study one small economy facing a given external return. The two-country
example will later close that return through world asset clearing.

\Needspace{12\baselineskip}
\begin{modelsetup}[1]
\begin{modelcondition}{01}{Residents, goods, and the analysis boundary}
There is a unit mass of identical resident households. Their aggregate equals
the representative household's allocation. A single homogeneous good is
perishable, freely tradable, and the real numeraire at each date. There are
no transport costs, tariffs, money, exchange rates, production choices,
capital, government, or population growth in this baseline. Endowments
$Q_1,Q_2>0$ are the residents' domestic goods income. Domestic claims cancel
when residents are consolidated; $B_t$ records only their net external asset.
\end{modelcondition}
\end{modelsetup}

\begin{modelsetup}[1]
\begin{modelcondition}{02}{Dates, information, and inherited wealth}
Dates are $t=1,2$. Endowments, preferences, and all returns are known with
certainty before date-1 choices. $B_0$ is the inherited real external claim
before date 1; it pays $R_0B_0$ at date 1, with known $R_0>0$. Negative
$B_0$ requires payment of inherited debt. At date 1, households receive income
and the inherited payoff, then choose consumption $C_1$ and a new position
$B_1$. At date 2 they receive $Q_2+RB_1$, consume $C_2$, and settle all claims.
There is no news arriving after the initial choice in the baseline.
\end{modelcondition}
\end{modelsetup}

\begin{modelsetup}[1]
\begin{modelcondition}{03}{The external asset and the price of time}
One unit of $B_1$ costs one unit of date-1 goods and pays $R=1+r^*>0$ units
of date-2 goods. Residents borrow and lend at this same certain return;
there is no default, spread, transaction cost, or quantity limit. The economy
is small in this asset market: its choices do not change $R$. A negative
net interest rate is allowed provided $R>0$. A single deterministic bond
moves resources across dates; it does not provide insurance against
unmodeled random states. The rest of the world supplies the opposite asset
position and the corresponding net goods deliveries.
\end{modelcondition}
\end{modelsetup}

\begin{modelsetup}[1]
\begin{modelcondition}{04}{Preferences and admissible consumption}
The household chooses $C_1,C_2>0$ to maximize $u(C_1)+\beta u(C_2)$, where
$\beta>0$ and
\[
 u(C)=\begin{cases}(C^{1-\sigma}-1)/(1-\sigma),&\sigma>0,\ \sigma\ne1,\\
 \log C,&\sigma=1.\end{cases}
\]
Thus $u'(C)=C^{-\sigma}>0$ and $u''(C)=-\sigma C^{-\sigma-1}<0$.
The finite-horizon example permits $\beta=1$. Lifetime resources
$\mathcal W=R_0B_0+Q_1+Q_2/R$ must be positive. This condition permits
positive consumption at both dates despite possibly negative inherited wealth.
\end{modelcondition}
\end{modelsetup}

\begin{modelsetup}[1]
\begin{modelcondition}{05}{Budgets, settlement, and equilibrium}
Each household faces
\[
 C_1+B_1=Q_1+R_0B_0,\qquad C_2+B_2=Q_2+RB_1.
\]
No unpaid debt may remain at the final date: $B_2\geq0$. There is no bequest
motive, so an optimum leaves $B_2=0$; this equality is a result of monotonic
utility combined with terminal settlement. Given the external price $R$,
an equilibrium consists of optimal positive consumption and external holdings
satisfying both budgets. Domestic goods absorption need not equal domestic
income: imports fill a shortfall and exports deliver a surplus. World goods
and asset markets clear once the rest of the world is included.
\end{modelcondition}
\end{modelsetup}

The timing separates an inherited stock from a new decision:
\begin{equation*}
 \underbrace{B_0,Q_1,Q_2,R_0,R}_{\text{Known before choice}}
 \ \longrightarrow\ 
 \underbrace{C_1,B_1}_{\text{Date 1}}
 \ \longrightarrow\ 
 \underbrace{Q_2+RB_1=C_2}_{\text{Date 2 settlement}}.
\end{equation*}
Ramsey's marginal trade-off will reappear. What changes is the resource
constraint and the determination of its return: domestic saving can buy a
foreign claim, and investment will no longer have to equal national saving.

\subsubsection{From two budgets to one feasible consumption set}

First impose terminal settlement. If $B_2>0$, reducing it by $\varepsilon>0$
and increasing $C_2$ by the same amount satisfies the final budget and raises
utility. Therefore $B_2=0$ at the optimum. The final budget gives
\begin{align}
 RB_1&=C_2-Q_2,\\
 B_1&=\frac{C_2-Q_2}{R}.
\end{align}
Substitute this position into the first budget, then move its income term:
\begin{align}
 C_1+\frac{C_2-Q_2}{R}&=Q_1+R_0B_0,\\
 C_1+\frac{C_2}{R}&=Q_1+R_0B_0+\frac{Q_2}{R}
 \equiv\mathcal W.\label{eq:open-pv-two}
\end{align}
Here $\mathcal W$ is lifetime wealth in date-1 goods, not current GDP.
For a feasible allocation that leaves a positive terminal asset, the left
side is instead less than $\mathcal W$. Optimization uses the frontier.
Solve the frontier for future consumption:
\begin{align}
 C_2&=R(\mathcal W-C_1),\\
 \frac{dC_2}{dC_1}\bigg|_{\mathcal W,R}&=-R.
\end{align}
One extra good consumed today costs $R$ goods tomorrow. This slope is a price,
not a psychological preference. With $B_0=0$, the endowment point $(Q_1,Q_2)$
lies on the frontier. Autarky restricts the household to that point; external
trade permits movement along the line.

\subsubsection{The consumption choice, by substitution and by multipliers}

Substitution reduces the problem to one variable:
\begin{equation}
 \max_{0<C_1<\mathcal W}
 V(C_1)=u(C_1)+\beta u\bigl(R(\mathcal W-C_1)\bigr).
\end{equation}
Apply the chain rule explicitly:
\begin{align}
 \frac{dV}{dC_1}
 &=u'(C_1)+\beta u'(C_2)\frac{dC_2}{dC_1}\\
 &=u'(C_1)-\beta R u'(C_2)=0,\\
 u'(C_1)&=\beta R u'(C_2).\label{eq:open-euler-two}
\end{align}
The second derivative is
\begin{align}
 \frac{d^2V}{dC_1^2}
 &=u''(C_1)-\beta R u''(C_2)(-R)\\
 &=u''(C_1)+\beta R^2u''(C_2)<0.
\end{align}
Strict concavity and the infinite marginal utility near either zero-consumption
endpoint select a unique interior maximum.

The multiplier route shows the same resource price:
\begin{align}
 \mathcal L&=u(C_1)+\beta u(C_2)
   +\lambda\left(\mathcal W-C_1-\frac{C_2}{R}\right),\\
 \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial C_1}&=u'(C_1)-\lambda=0,\\
 \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial C_2}&=\beta u'(C_2)-\frac\lambda R=0,\\
 \lambda&=u'(C_1)=\beta R u'(C_2).
\end{align}
A marginal unit of lifetime wealth is worth $\lambda$ units of utility.
Date-2 consumption costs only $1/R$ units of that wealth, explaining the
multiplier attached to its first-order condition.

For CRRA preferences, solve the Euler equation and the budget together:
\begin{align}
 C_1^{-\sigma}&=\beta R C_2^{-\sigma},\\
 \left(\frac{C_2}{C_1}\right)^\sigma&=\beta R,\\
 C_2&=gC_1,\qquad g\equiv(\beta R)^{1/\sigma}>0,\\
 C_1+\frac{gC_1}{R}&=\mathcal W,\\
 C_1^*&=\frac{\mathcal W}{D},\qquad
 C_2^*=\frac{g\mathcal W}{D},\qquad D\equiv1+\frac gR,\label{eq:open-crra-choice}\\
 B_1^*&=Q_1+R_0B_0-C_1^*.
\end{align}
When $\beta R=1$, the consumption levels are equal. When $\beta R>1$,
consumption rises: the market reward for waiting outweighs impatience at a
flat path. ``Smoothing'' means avoiding unnecessarily unequal marginal
utilities at the available price, not imposing constant consumption for all
preferences and returns.

\Needspace{9\baselineskip}
\begin{redblock}
\textbf{Borrowing is an allocation, not an extra resource.}
Suppose $Q_1=1$, $Q_2=3$, $B_0=0$, $R=\beta=\sigma=1$.
Then $\mathcal W=4$, $g=1$, $D=2$, and $C_1=C_2=2$.
The first budget requires $B_1=1-2=-1$. The second gives
$C_2=3+1(-1)=2$. Counting the borrowed good as extra income at date 1
while omitting repayment at date 2 would falsely expand lifetime resources.
\end{redblock}

\subsubsection{Close the world market, not just one country's problem}

The given-return assumption describes a small country facing a much larger
rest of the world. For the two equal countries at the start of the chapter,
$R$ is instead an equilibrium price. Set $B_0^j=0$ and $\beta=\sigma=1$ for
$j\in\{A,B\}$. Equations~\eqref{eq:open-crra-choice} give $g=R$, $D=2$:
\begin{align}
 C_1^j&=\frac12\left(Q_1^j+\frac{Q_2^j}{R}\right),\\
 B_1^j&=Q_1^j-C_1^j
       =\frac12\left(Q_1^j-\frac{Q_2^j}{R}\right).
\end{align}
World asset clearing determines the return:
\begin{align}
 0=B_1^A+B_1^B
 &=\frac12\left[Q_1^A+Q_1^B-\frac{Q_2^A+Q_2^B}{R}\right],\\
 R(Q_1^A+Q_1^B)&=Q_2^A+Q_2^B,\\
 R&=\frac{Q_2^A+Q_2^B}{Q_1^A+Q_1^B}=\frac44=1.
\end{align}
Both households take this price as given when choosing. No monopoly is needed
for aggregate endowments to determine it. Date-1 goods clearing follows from
summing the first budgets; date-2 clearing follows from summing the second.
The lending of one country finances the borrowing of the other.

\subsection{Reading the external accounts}

\subsubsection{Name the flows that the model has already produced}

The model tells us what to measure. At date 1, a borrower absorbs more goods
than its endowment. At date 2, it delivers goods and pays interest. Those are
different flows. We now use $Y$ for GDP, $C$ for household consumption, $G$
for government consumption, and $I$ for gross capital formation, including
inventory changes. All expenditure and income measures below use consistent
prices and the same resident boundary. In the endowment benchmark, $Y=Q$
and $G=I=0$.

Let $X$ and $M$ be exports and imports of goods \emph{and services}. Here $M$
is imports, not the money stock used in the earlier monetary chapters. The
expenditure identity and the trade balance are
\begin{align}
 Y&=C+I+G+X-M,\\
 TB&\equiv X-M=Y-C-I-G.\label{eq:open-trade-balance}
\end{align}
A trade deficit says that domestic absorption exceeds domestic production.
It does not identify whether consumption, investment, or government spending
caused that gap, nor whether the allocation is desirable.

Use $NPI$ for net primary income from abroad, and $NCT$ for net current
transfers received. Primary income includes cross-border labor and investment
income; current transfers are not payments for current goods or services.
Then define
\begin{align}
 CA&\equiv TB+NPI+NCT,\label{eq:open-current-account}\\
 GNI&\equiv Y+NPI,\\
 GNDI&\equiv GNI+NCT=Y+NPI+NCT.
\end{align}
We retain the familiar primary/secondary-income terminology used in many
existing datasets. BPM7 calls these earned income and transfer income.
Its integrated framework distinguishes transactions from revaluations and
other changes in positions.\footnote{IMF (2025),
\href{https://data.imf.org/-/media/iData/External-Storage/Documents/5B776E0E552E4881AF24042EAE7D049B/en/1-BPM7-White-Cover.pdf}{\textit{Integrated Balance of Payments and International Investment Position
Manual}, seventh edition}, White Cover, pre-edited version, Chapters 2,
7--9, 12--14, and 19. Statistical residence concerns an institutional unit's
center of predominant economic interest, not simply citizenship. The
identities here suppress measurement discrepancies unless stated otherwise.}
The bridge from production to residents' disposable resources matters:
a debtor may produce enough to cover its domestic absorption and still need
a trade surplus to pay external interest.

Define gross national saving from disposable income, not GDP alone:
\begin{align}
 S&\equiv GNDI-C-G\\
  &=Y+NPI+NCT-C-G,\\
 S-I&=Y-C-I-G+NPI+NCT\\
    &=TB+NPI+NCT=CA.\label{eq:open-saving-investment}
\end{align}
This is an accounting identity. It does not establish that a policy can change
saving while leaving investment, income, prices, and external finance fixed.
For a consolidated household--firm private sector and government, let $T$
be net current taxes paid to government, $Y_d^p$ private disposable income,
and $Y_d^g$ government disposable income. With all current flows allocated
consistently between them,
\begin{align}
 Y_d^p+Y_d^g&=GNDI,\\
 S_p&=Y_d^p-C,\qquad S_g=Y_d^g-G,\\
 S_p+S_g-I&=CA.
\end{align}
In the special case $Y_d^g=T$ and all external income and transfers accrue to
the private sector, $S_p=GNDI-T-C$ and $S_g=T-G$.
A larger fiscal deficit need not imply an equal increase in the current-account
deficit: private saving and investment can respond.

\subsubsection{From the bond budget to the current account}

In the baseline, interest on the inherited claim is $r_0B_0$, where
$r_0=R_0-1$, and there are no current transfers. Subtract $B_0$ from the
first budget's asset equation:
\begin{align}
 B_1-B_0&=Q_1-C_1+(R_0-1)B_0\\
 &=TB_1+r_0B_0=CA_1.\label{eq:open-bond-ca}
\end{align}
Likewise, $r^*=R-1$ is the interest paid at date 2:
\begin{align}
 B_2-B_1&=Q_2-C_2+r^*B_1=CA_2,\\
 B_2=0&\quad\Longrightarrow\quad CA_2=-B_1,\\
 TB_2&=CA_2-r^*B_1=-RB_1.
\end{align}
If $B_1=-1$ and $R=1.1$, final settlement needs $TB_2=1.1$, of which $0.1$
pays interest. The current-account surplus is $CA_2=1.1-0.1=1$, which
reduces principal debt by one. A trade surplus and a current-account surplus
therefore need not have the same size or even the same sign at a given date.

\subsubsection{Transactions, valuation, and the sign of capital flows}

For actual portfolios, let $A^{ext}$ be external financial assets and
$L^{ext}$ external liabilities. Set $NFA=A^{ext}-L^{ext}$. Use the statistical
financial-account sign convention:
\begin{align}
 FA&\equiv\text{net acquisition of external assets}
             -\text{net incurrence of external liabilities},\\
 CA+KA&=FA\qquad\text{(without a measurement discrepancy)}.\label{eq:open-bop-sign}
\end{align}
Here $KA$ is the \emph{capital account}: capital transfers and transactions
in nonproduced nonfinancial assets. It is not a label for ordinary bond or
bank-loan flows. Positive net borrowing normally gives $FA<0$ under this
convention. If a textbook instead defines net financial \emph{inflows}
$F^{in}\equiv-FA$, its equation is $CA+KA+F^{in}=0$.

Now reconcile stocks and flows. Let $VAL$ be net revaluation gains, including
exchange-rate and other price changes, and $OCV$ other changes in volume,
such as an uncompensated write-off. Then
\begin{align}
 NFA_t-NFA_{t-1}&=FA_t+VAL_t+OCV_t\\
 &=CA_t+KA_t+VAL_t+OCV_t.\label{eq:open-iip-reconcile}
\end{align}
This is why adding current accounts does not generally reconstruct a country's
observed net external wealth. The equality $\Delta B=CA$ in
\eqref{eq:open-bond-ca} uses the special assumptions $KA=VAL=OCV=0$ and
one real bond valued at par.

A numerical reconciliation makes the distinction concrete. Suppose opening
assets and liabilities are $100$ and $80$. Residents acquire $5$ of assets
and incur $10$ of liabilities. With no $KA$, the financial and current
accounts are $FA=CA=5-10=-5$. Suppose existing assets gain $12$ in value,
liabilities gain $2$, and there are no other volume changes:
\begin{align}
 A^{ext}_{end}&=100+5+12=117,\\
 L^{ext}_{end}&=80+10+2=92,\\
 \Delta NFA&=(117-92)-(100-80)=5,\\
 CA+VAL&=-5+(12-2)=5.
\end{align}
Net wealth rises despite a current-account deficit. A valuation gain is not
an export receipt or current earned income.

\Needspace{9\baselineskip}
\begin{blueblock}
\textbf{Zero net flows do not mean a closed economy.}
Exports and imports may both be large while $TB=0$. Residents may acquire
foreign assets of 100 while foreigners acquire domestic liabilities of 100,
so $FA=0$ despite large gross transactions. The corresponding stocks can
also be large while $NFA=0$. Net measures reveal an imbalance; they do not
measure the full scale, composition, or risk of cross-border exposure.
\end{blueblock}

\subsection{Income timing and the demand for external finance}

\subsubsection{Temporary income, persistent income, and news}

Hold $R,R_0,B_0,\beta,\sigma$ fixed in the two-period endowment model.
The growth ratio $g$ and denominator $D=1+g/R$ are then fixed. Differentiate
wealth and the consumption rule before interpreting a shock:
\begin{align}
 d\mathcal W&=dQ_1+\frac{dQ_2}{R},\\
 dC_1&=\frac{dQ_1+dQ_2/R}{D},\\
 dC_2&=g\,dC_1,\\
 dB_1&=dQ_1-dC_1\\
 &=\left(1-\frac1D\right)dQ_1-\frac{dQ_2}{RD}.\label{eq:open-income-response}
\end{align}
There is no valuation change and the inherited asset is fixed, so $dCA_1=dB_1$.
For a temporary extra endowment $\Delta>0$ today only,
\begin{align}
 dC_1&=\frac\Delta D,\qquad
 dC_2=\frac{g\Delta}{D},\\
 dB_1&=\frac{D-1}{D}\Delta
       =\frac{g/R}{1+g/R}\Delta>0.
\end{align}
Some of the windfall is lent abroad. Reverse the signs for a temporary
harvest loss: borrowing spreads the loss across both consumption dates.
This is a conditional model of a temporary real-income disturbance, not a
claim that all disaster losses can be insured or financed on these terms.

For income higher by $\Delta$ at \emph{both} dates,
\begin{align}
 dC_1&=\frac{1+1/R}{D}\Delta,\\
 dB_1&=\left[1-\frac{1+1/R}{D}\right]\Delta.
\end{align}
If $\beta R=1$, then $g=1$ and $D=1+1/R$, so $dC_1=dC_2=\Delta$ and
$dB_1=0$. Higher income need not create a larger external surplus when the
household regards it as lasting.

For news at date 1 that only date-2 income will rise,
\begin{align}
 dQ_1&=0,\quad dQ_2=\Delta,\\
 dC_1&=\frac\Delta{RD}>0,\quad
 dC_2=\frac{g\Delta}{RD}>0,\\
 dB_1&=-\frac\Delta{RD}<0.
\end{align}
Consumption and borrowing rise before the income arrives. For example,
a hypothetical country learns with certainty of future resource royalties
that accrue to its residents. In this model their present value can support
spending now. Uncertain extraction, foreign ownership, political risk, and
lenders' limits would each change that conclusion.

\subsubsection{A higher return changes both a price and wealth}

It is tempting to say that a higher interest rate must reduce consumption
and improve the current account because saving becomes more rewarding.
The household also revalues future endowments, and an existing creditor
and debtor are affected differently. For CRRA utility,
\begin{align}
 g&=\beta^{1/\sigma}R^{1/\sigma},\qquad
 \frac{dg}{dR}=\frac{g}{\sigma R},\\
 D&=1+g/R,\qquad
 \frac{dD}{dR}=\frac{g}{R^2}\left(\frac1\sigma-1\right),\\
 \frac{d\mathcal W}{dR}&=-\frac{Q_2}{R^2},\\
 \frac{dC_1}{dR}
 &=\frac{D\,d\mathcal W/dR-\mathcal W\,dD/dR}{D^2}\\
 &=-\frac{Q_2}{R^2D}
   -\frac{\mathcal W g}{R^2D^2}\left(\frac1\sigma-1\right).
\end{align}
For $\sigma>1$, the second term is positive, so the general sign requires
more information. In the log case, $g=\beta R$ and $D=1+\beta$:
\begin{align}
 C_1&=\frac{Q_1+R_0B_0+Q_2/R}{1+\beta},\\
 \frac{dC_1}{dR}&=-\frac{Q_2}{(1+\beta)R^2}<0,\\
 \frac{dB_1}{dR}&=-\frac{dC_1}{dR}>0.
\end{align}
These derivatives hold the inherited payoff $R_0B_0$ fixed. Repricing old
variable-rate debt is a separate experiment.

We can also measure the welfare effect without guessing it from consumption
at one date. Write $V^*=u(C_1)+\beta u(C_2)$ at the optimum. Differentiating
its budget and utility gives
\begin{align}
 \frac{dC_1}{dR}+\frac1R\frac{dC_2}{dR}-\frac{C_2}{R^2}
 &=-\frac{Q_2}{R^2},\\
 \frac{dV^*}{dR}
 &=u'(C_1)\frac{dC_1}{dR}+\beta u'(C_2)\frac{dC_2}{dR}\\
 &=\lambda\left(\frac{dC_1}{dR}+\frac1R\frac{dC_2}{dR}\right)\\
 &=\frac{\lambda(C_2-Q_2)}{R^2}=\frac{\lambda B_1}{R}.
\end{align}
A higher new-loan return benefits a net lender and harms a net borrower
locally. The quantity response and the welfare response answer different
questions.
