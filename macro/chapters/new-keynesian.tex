\subsection{Why can demand change output?}

RBC taught us to ask what changed in production opportunities and how households
reallocated consumption and work. Now consider a different hypothetical sequence.
A restaurant receives fewer bookings after borrowing costs rise. Its kitchen,
recipes, and available workers have not changed. Yet its posted menu may remain
unchanged while meals served and hours worked fall. Which constraint makes this
response possible, and which household choice starts it?

An initial guess is to keep the RBC allocation and attach a price level. That
cannot explain this sequence: a mere change of nominal units leaves its real
choices unchanged. A second guess is to declare output demand-determined. That
describes the outcome but leaves the household's spending and the seller's price
unexplained. We need both decisions before we can assess the guess.

Start with the seller. If its product were identical to every other product,
a higher price would lose all customers. Give each seller a differentiated
product instead. Customers can switch, but substitution is finite; a seller
then chooses a price facing a downward-sloping demand curve. This creates a
markup. It does not yet create delayed adjustment: a monopolist that can freely
reprice still responds immediately to new conditions.

Now let a chosen nominal price survive for an uncertain number of periods.
The seller asks: what wage bill and demand will this price face today, next
quarter, and later if it is still in place? The relevant object is a discounted
stream of profits, not today's markup alone. Households, meanwhile, compare
buying goods today with holding a nominal bond. The expected purchasing power
of its payoff connects the policy rate, expected inflation, and present demand.

This is a teaching reconstruction of the modeling problem, not a claim about
any author's private thought process. The basic framework retains optimization,
market clearing, and model-consistent expectations; it adds differentiated goods
and staggered nominal prices.\footnote{For the framework and policy questions,
see Clarida, Gal\'{\i}, and Gertler (1999),
\href{https://www.nber.org/papers/w7147}{The Science of Monetary Policy: A New
Keynesian Perspective}; Gal\'{\i} (2015), \textit{Monetary Policy, Inflation, and
the Business Cycle}, second edition, Chapters 3--4; the author's
\href{https://crei.cat/people/gali/}{official teaching materials} include the
corresponding chapter slides. The calculations below
state their own conventions and intermediate steps.}

\begin{greenblock}
\textbf{The change from RBC is a constraint on price adjustment.}
A household can still optimize while a firm produces less than it would under
flexible prices. Its inherited price and customers' demand jointly constrain
sales. Lower activity need not mean lower productive capacity. Conversely, a
productivity shock can move the flexible-price benchmark itself; a fall in
observed output alone does not identify a demand shortfall.
\end{greenblock}

\subsection{Economic environment and timing}

A closed, labor-only economy isolates the price-adjustment mechanism. Any fixed
factor remains in place; there is no investment decision. Capital accumulation
can be added later, once spending and price setting are understood.

\Needspace{12\baselineskip}
\begin{modelsetup}[1]
\begin{modelcondition}{01}{People, products, and ownership}
Time is discrete, $t=0,1,\ldots$. There is a unit mass of identical households
and a unit continuum of differentiated goods $i\in[0,1]$. A representative
household consumes the composite $C_t$, supplies total labor $N_t$, and owns an
equal share of every firm. Nominal distributed profits are $D_t$. There is no
foreign trade, physical-capital accumulation, investment, or government purchase.
Population is fixed; hours are chosen. Goods differ even though households
have identical preferences.
\end{modelcondition}
\end{modelsetup}

\begin{modelsetup}[1]
\begin{modelcondition}{02}{The consumption basket and its price}
For $\epsilon>1$, the composite and its unit-expenditure price are
\[
 C_t=\left[\int_0^1 C_{it}^{(\epsilon-1)/\epsilon}\,di
       \right]^{\epsilon/(\epsilon-1)},\qquad
 P_t=\left[\int_0^1 P_{it}^{1-\epsilon}\,di\right]^{1/(1-\epsilon)}.
\]
Households take all prices as given and allocate expenditure across goods.
The substitution elasticity is $\epsilon$, not $(\epsilon-1)/\epsilon$.
The latter is the exponent inside the aggregator. The same basket defines
aggregate output $Y_t$. Demand for each good clears its own market:
$Y_{it}=C_{it}$; aggregate clearing is $Y_t=C_t$ in composite units.
\end{modelcondition}
\end{modelsetup}

\begin{modelsetup}[1]
\begin{modelcondition}{03}{Preferences, hours, and optional liquidity services}
Households choose contingent plans to maximize
\[
 E_0\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\beta^t\xi_t
 \left[v(C_t)-\frac{\chi N_t^{1+\varphi}}{1+\varphi}
                 +\omega v_m(M_t/P_t)\right].
\]
Here $0<\beta<1$, $\sigma>0$, $\chi>0$, $\varphi\geq0$, and $\xi_t>0$.
For $\sigma\ne1$, $v(C)=(C^{1-\sigma}-1)/(1-\sigma)$; for $\sigma=1$,
$v(C)=\log C$. Similarly $v_m(m)=(m^{1-\nu_m}-1)/(1-\nu_m)$ for
$\nu_m>0$, $\nu_m\ne1$, and $v_m(m)=\log m$ for $\nu_m=1$.
Thus $v'=C^{-\sigma}$ and $v_m'=m^{-\nu_m}$. Labor is measured in hours
with an interior solution $N_t>0$; this specification has no finite time-endowment
bound. The course normalization is $\chi=\omega=1$ when money is included.
For $\omega>0$, end-of-period real balances give separable liquidity services.
For the moneyless core, omit both that term and the household money choice;
do not impose a money FOC with $\omega=0$.
\end{modelcondition}
\end{modelsetup}

\begin{modelsetup}[1]
\begin{modelcondition}{04}{Nominal accounts, assets, and government}
$W_t$ is the common nominal wage. A bond bought at $t$ for $Q_t$ pays one unit
of currency at $t+1$; $B_t$ is its face value. Write
$R_t^n=Q_t^{-1}=1+i_t$, where $i_t$ is the net nominal interest rate. The budget is
\[
 P_t C_t+M_t+Q_tB_t
 =W_tN_t+D_t+T_t+M_{t-1}+B_{t-1}.
\]
Initial money and maturing bonds are given. $T_t$ is a lump-sum nominal transfer,
positive when received. In the monetary version, the government issues
non-interest-bearing money, has no purchases or net bond supply, and sets
$T_t=M_t-M_{t-1}$ in aggregate. Private bonds clear at zero aggregate holdings.
Profits include the return on any fixed factor. Liquidity services use no goods;
these accounts therefore imply $C_t=Y_t$. Moneyless accounts omit $M,T$.
\end{modelcondition}
\end{modelsetup}

\begin{modelsetup}[1]
\begin{modelcondition}{05}{Technology, cost, and the labor market}
Each firm has technology $Y_{it}=A_tN_{it}^{1-\alpha}$, with common $A_t>0$
and $0\leq\alpha<1$. If $\alpha>0$, an omitted fixed factor has been normalized
to one at each firm; it cannot be traded or accumulated here. If $\alpha=0$,
output is linear in labor. Labor is homogeneous and mobile across firms;
competitive labor trading gives a common $W_t$, with $N_t=\int_0^1N_{it}\,di$.
It does not require equal firm employment. Each firm takes $W_t,A_t,P_t,Y_t$
as given, hires labor to meet its product demand, and minimizes the wage bill
conditional on that demand. Differing relative prices can imply differing
sales, hours, and marginal costs, even at the common wage.
\end{modelcondition}
\end{modelsetup}

\begin{modelsetup}[1]
\begin{modelcondition}{06}{Market power and the price that cannot be changed}
The goods market is monopolistically competitive. A firm's demand is
$Y_{it}=(P_{it}/P_t)^{-\epsilon}Y_t$, so its flexible-price gross markup is
$\mu=\epsilon/(\epsilon-1)>1$. Independently across firms and of its age and state, a firm can
reset its nominal price with probability $1-\theta$, $0<\theta<1$; with
probability $\theta$ it retains the previous nominal price. There is no baseline
indexation. Resetting firms face identical current and future aggregate conditions
and choose the same reset price $P_t^{\#}$; firms that cannot reset need not have
that price. A chosen price survives $k$ further periods with probability
$\theta^k$; expected duration is $1/(1-\theta)$ periods.
\end{modelcondition}
\end{modelsetup}

\begin{modelsetup}[1]
\begin{modelcondition}{07}{Information, shocks, and within-period timing}
At $t$, everyone observes aggregate states and current innovations. Firms learn
whether they may reset, then resetters choose $P_t^{\#}$; households allocate
spending, firms meet demand, labor is supplied, and asset positions are carried
to $t+1$. These choices are jointly determined in equilibrium. Expectations are
$E_tX=E[X\mid\mathcal I_t]$ under the model; future innovations are not observed.
For the examples, $a_t=\log(A_t/\bar A)$ and $q_t=\log(\xi_t/\bar\xi)$ follow
$a_t=\rho_a a_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t^a$ and
$q_t=\rho_q q_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t^q$, with $|\rho_a|,|\rho_q|<1$ and
mean-zero innovations independent of past information. Full observation of the
current state does not remove uncertainty about the future.
\end{modelcondition}
\end{modelsetup}

\begin{modelsetup}[1]
\begin{modelcondition}{08}{Price dispersion and aggregate feasibility}
The exact labor requirement is
\[
 N_t=(Y_t/A_t)^{1/(1-\alpha)}\Delta_t,\qquad
 \Delta_t=\int_0^1(P_{it}/P_t)^{-\epsilon/(1-\alpha)}\,di.
\]
Thus the symmetric production function $Y_t=A_tN_t^{1-\alpha}$ is exact only
when $\Delta_t=1$. Unequal relative prices use more labor for the same composite
output. The local analysis starts from a symmetric zero-inflation reference
with all prices equal, $\bar\Delta=1$, $\bar\xi=1$, and constant productivity.
Initial inherited price vintages must be specified. The shock experiment starts
from the symmetric reference; arbitrary initial dispersion cannot be silently
set to zero.
\end{modelcondition}
\end{modelsetup}

\begin{modelsetup}[1]
\begin{modelcondition}{09}{Policy closure and the reference interest rate}
Choose one nominal closure. Under a quantity rule, an exogenous money path and
money-market clearing determine the interest rate jointly with spending and
prices. Under an interest-rate rule, the authority specifies $R_t^n$, usually
as a reaction to inflation and the output gap; money, if present, accommodates
household demand. These are alternative experiments. At the zero-inflation
reference, $\bar R^n=1/\beta$, $\bar i=1/\beta-1>0$.
The interior money-demand calculation assumes $i_t>0$. The baseline interest rule
has no binding lower bound; large shocks near that bound require a different
solution. Transfers finance money issuance without distortionary taxes.
\end{modelcondition}
\end{modelsetup}

\begin{modelsetup}[1]
\begin{modelcondition}{10}{Admissibility, discounting, and local scope}
Choices satisfy feasibility, household no-Ponzi restrictions, and the associated
transversality condition on assets valued at marginal utility. In the moneyless
case, one convenient terminal condition is
$\lim_{T\to\infty}E_0[\beta^T\lambda_T Q_TB_T]=0$,
where $\lambda_t=\xi_t C_t^{-\sigma}/P_t$; with money, include $M_T$ in
terminal expenditure. The pricing sums below must converge. Firms use their
owners' marginal-utility valuation of profits; no borrowing or working-capital
constraint is added. We study a local, interior, bounded equilibrium around
the stated deterministic reference. Linearization drops second-order products,
not economically necessary equilibrium conditions. Local uniqueness must be
checked after the policy rule is specified.
\end{modelcondition}
\end{modelsetup}

\begin{blueblock}
\textbf{Two conventions for the Calvo probability.}
Here $\theta$ always means \emph{keep} the price. In the first course derivation,
$\theta_{\mathrm{adjust}}=1-\theta$; in its later vintage-price derivation,
$\theta_{\mathrm{keep}}=\theta$. We use $P^{\#}$ for the price actually chosen
at a reset and reserve a separate target below. Flexible pricing is the limit
$\theta=0$; it says nothing about the substitution elasticity $\epsilon$.
\end{blueblock}

\subsection{The household: first the basket, then the date of purchase}

\subsubsection{Expenditure minimization: deriving demand and the price index}

Fix a date and suppress $t$. Let $r=(\epsilon-1)/\epsilon$ and
$\mathcal C=(\int C_i^r\,di)^{1/r}$. To buy the required composite $C$, solve
\begin{align}
 \min_{\{C_i\}}\int_0^1P_iC_i\,di
 &\quad\text{subject to }\mathcal C\geq C,\\
 \mathcal L&=\int_0^1P_iC_i\,di+\Lambda(C-\mathcal C).
\end{align}
The multiplier is a cost per extra unit of composite. The chain rule gives
\begin{align}
 \frac{\partial\mathcal C}{\partial C_i}
 &=\frac1r\left(\int C_j^r\,dj\right)^{1/r-1}r C_i^{r-1}\\
 &=\left(\int C_j^r\,dj\right)^{1/(\epsilon-1)} C_i^{-1/\epsilon}
 =\mathcal C^{1/\epsilon}C_i^{-1/\epsilon},\\
 0=\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial C_i}
 &=P_i-\Lambda(C/C_i)^{1/\epsilon},\\
 \frac{P_i}{\Lambda}&=(C_i/C)^{-1/\epsilon},\qquad
 C_i=(P_i/\Lambda)^{-\epsilon}C.
\end{align}
Insert this demand into the binding composite constraint:
\begin{align}
 C^r&=\int[(P_i/\Lambda)^{-\epsilon}C]^r\,di\\
 &=C^r\Lambda^{\epsilon-1}\int P_i^{1-\epsilon}\,di,\\
 1&=\Lambda^{\epsilon-1}\int P_i^{1-\epsilon}\,di,\\
 \Lambda&=\left(\int P_i^{1-\epsilon}\,di\right)^{1/(1-\epsilon)}=P.
\end{align}
Check the bill rather than assuming that the multiplier is the index:
\begin{align}
 \int P_iC_i\,di
 &=CP^\epsilon\int P_i^{1-\epsilon}\,di
 =CP^\epsilon P^{1-\epsilon}=PC.\label{eq:nk-ces-bill}
\end{align}
Thus the household's many purchases can be represented in its budget by $PC$.
A seller that raises its price relative to the index loses customers with
elasticity $-\partial\log C_i/\partial\log(P_i/P)=\epsilon$.

\subsubsection{Choosing a basket with a given budget}

Alternatively, fix expenditure $Z$ and choose the largest affordable basket:
\begin{align}
 \max_{\{C_i\}}\mathcal C&\quad\text{subject to }\int P_iC_i\,di\leq Z,\\
 \mathcal L&=\mathcal C+\ell\left(Z-\int P_iC_i\,di\right),\\
 0&=(\mathcal C/C_i)^{1/\epsilon}-\ell P_i,\\
 C_i&=(\ell P_i)^{-\epsilon}\mathcal C.
\end{align}
Substitute into the basket, then into the bill:
\begin{align}
 1&=\ell^{-(\epsilon-1)}\int P_i^{1-\epsilon}\,di,\\
 \ell&=\left(\int P_i^{1-\epsilon}\,di\right)^{1/(\epsilon-1)}=1/P,\\
 Z&=\int P_i(P_i/P)^{-\epsilon}\mathcal C\,di=P\mathcal C,\\
 \mathcal C&=Z/P,\qquad C_i=(P_i/P)^{-\epsilon}Z/P.
\end{align}
One problem fixes the basket and minimizes its bill; the other fixes the bill
and maximizes the basket. The price index makes their answers consistent.
$Z$ is expenditure available at this date, not necessarily an exogenous lifetime
endowment: the next stage determines how much to spend.

\subsubsection{A finite number of goods and the units of the basket}

For $J$ goods, write the basket using a mean rather than a sum. This choice
changes the units of the composite and its price index. Define
\begin{align}
 C&=\left(\frac1J\sum_{j=1}^J C_j^r\right)^{1/r},\qquad
 \text{bill}=\sum_{j=1}^JP_jC_j,\\
 \frac{\partial C}{\partial C_j}
 &=\frac1J C^{1/\epsilon}C_j^{-1/\epsilon},\\
 P_j&=\frac{P}{J}(C/C_j)^{1/\epsilon},\qquad
 C_j=(JP_j/P)^{-\epsilon}C.
\end{align}
Recover the dual price without importing the continuum normalization:
\begin{align}
 1&=\frac1J\sum_{j=1}^J(JP_j/P)^{1-\epsilon}\\
  &=J^{-\epsilon}P^{\epsilon-1}\sum_{j=1}^JP_j^{1-\epsilon},\\
 P&=J\left(\frac1J\sum_{j=1}^JP_j^{1-\epsilon}\right)^{1/(1-\epsilon)},\\
 \sum_jP_jC_j&=C(P/J)^\epsilon\sum_jP_j^{1-\epsilon}=PC.
\end{align}
If all $P_j=p$, the index is $P=Jp$ and each $C_j=C$; buying one unit of
this normalized basket buys one unit of every good. If the course's exponent is
$1/(1+n)$, equate it to $r$:
\begin{align}
 \frac1{1+n}&=\frac{\epsilon-1}{\epsilon},\qquad
 n=\frac1{\epsilon-1},\qquad
 \epsilon=\frac{1+n}{n},\\
 P&=J\left(\frac1J\sum_jP_j^{-1/n}\right)^{-n}.
\end{align}
This explains the extra $J$ in the finite-good formulas. It is a units choice;
it should not be inserted into the unit-mass continuum formulas.

\subsubsection{Intertemporal choice with money and nominal bonds}

Return to time subscripts. Let $m_t^r=M_t/P_t$ denote real money in this
subsection; later $\hat m_t$ denotes the log deviation of \emph{nominal} money.
Using resources minus expenditures keeps the multiplier positive:
\begin{align}
 \mathcal L=E_0\sum_{t=0}^\infty\beta^t\Big\{&\xi_t
 [v(C_t)-\chi N_t^{1+\varphi}/(1+\varphi)+\omega v_m(m_t^r)]\notag\\
 &+\lambda_t[W_tN_t+D_t+T_t+M_{t-1}+B_{t-1}
                  -P_tC_t-M_t-Q_tB_t]\Big\}.
\end{align}
The current-date derivatives are
\begin{align}
 0&=\xi_t C_t^{-\sigma}-\lambda_tP_t,
 &\lambda_t&=\xi_t C_t^{-\sigma}/P_t,\\
 0&=-\xi_t\chi N_t^\varphi+\lambda_tW_t,
 &W_t/P_t&=\chi C_t^\sigma N_t^\varphi.\label{eq:nk-labor}
\end{align}
For $B_t$, include its cost now and payoff next period. For $M_t$, include
its liquidity service now and its carried nominal balance next period:
\begin{align}
 0&=-\beta^t\lambda_tQ_t+\beta^{t+1}E_t\lambda_{t+1},\\
 Q_t&=\beta E_t(\lambda_{t+1}/\lambda_t)\\
 &=\beta E_t\left[\frac{\xi_{t+1}}{\xi_t}
       \left(\frac{C_{t+1}}{C_t}\right)^{-\sigma}\frac{P_t}{P_{t+1}}\right],
       \label{eq:nk-euler-exact}\\
 0&=\beta^t[\xi_t\omega(m_t^r)^{-\nu_m}/P_t-\lambda_t]
              +\beta^{t+1}E_t\lambda_{t+1},\\
 1&=\frac{\xi_t\omega(m_t^r)^{-\nu_m}}{P_t\lambda_t}
                  +\beta E_t(\lambda_{t+1}/\lambda_t)\\
  &=\omega C_t^\sigma(m_t^r)^{-\nu_m}+Q_t,\\
 \omega C_t^\sigma(m_t^r)^{-\nu_m}
 &=1-Q_t=1-\frac1{1+i_t}=\frac{i_t}{1+i_t}.\label{eq:nk-money-exact}
\end{align}
The cost of money is the interest forgone on the bond. A larger $i_t$ makes
liquidity more costly; a larger purchase basket makes liquidity more useful.
The marginal utility of money must be divided by marginal utility of consumption;
$1-Q_t$ is not unscaled $u_m$.

The old budget buys a bond for one unit today and receives $1+i_t$ tomorrow.
It is equivalent, but its quantity is a different object. Set $H_t=Q_tB_t$:
\begin{align}
 B_t&=R_t^nH_t,\\
 P_tC_t+M_t+H_t
 &=W_tN_t+D_t+T_t+M_{t-1}+R_{t-1}^nH_{t-1}.
\end{align}
Do not use the face-value budget with the unit-price bond's payoff. Households
own firms, so omitting distributed profits would also break aggregate accounting:
\begin{align}
 D_t&=\int(P_{it}Y_{it}-W_tN_{it})\,di=P_tY_t-W_tN_t,\\
 P_tC_t&=W_tN_t+D_t+(M_t-M_{t-1})+M_{t-1}-M_t=P_tY_t.
\end{align}

\begin{blueblock}
\textbf{Common wage, different quantities.}
Competitive labor trading equates wages across uses; it does not equate
$N_{it}$ across firms. Likewise, a continuum of goods is an indexing device,
not proof of competitive pricing. The product market's demand elasticity and
pricing choice determine its market structure.
\end{blueblock}

\subsubsection{Money demand and interest-rate conventions}

Let hats denote log deviations from the deterministic reference. From
\eqref{eq:nk-money-exact},
\begin{align}
 \log\omega+\sigma\log C_t-\nu_m(\log M_t-\log P_t)
 &=\log i_t-\log(1+i_t),\\
 \sigma\hat c_t-\nu_m(\hat m_t-\hat p_t)
 &=f(i_t)-f(\bar i),\qquad f(i)=\log i-\log(1+i),\\
 f'(i)&=1/i-1/(1+i)=1/[i(1+i)],\\
 f(i_t)-f(\bar i)&\simeq\frac{i_t-\bar i}{\bar i(1+\bar i)},\\
 \hat m_t-\hat p_t
 &\simeq\frac\sigma{\nu_m}\hat c_t
   -\frac1{\nu_m\bar i(1+\bar i)}(i_t-\bar i).
\end{align}
The course's $\hat i_t=\log(i_t/\bar i)\simeq(i_t-\bar i)/\bar i$ gives
\begin{align}
 \hat m_t-\hat p_t&=\frac\sigma{\nu_m}\hat c_t
             -\frac1{\nu_m(1+\bar i)}\hat i_t.
\end{align}
For the Euler equation and policy rule we instead use the log \emph{gross}
rate deviation $j_t=\log(R_t^n/\bar R^n)$. Since
\begin{align}
 j_t&\simeq(i_t-\bar i)/(1+\bar i),\\
 \hat i_t&\simeq(1+\bar i)j_t/\bar i,\\
 \hat m_t-\hat p_t&=\alpha_m\hat y_t-\eta j_t,
 \qquad\alpha_m=\sigma/\nu_m,\quad\eta=1/(\nu_m\bar i),
 \label{eq:nk-money-linear}
\end{align}
where $C_t=Y_t$. An income coefficient of one additionally requires
$\sigma=\nu_m$. From now on local equations use equality signs for their
first-order versions; they do not become exact nonlinear identities.

Near $i=0$, the log of the opportunity cost is singular and the semi-elasticity
becomes large. A zero-bound experiment cannot be obtained by blindly extending
this local money-demand line through zero.

\subsection{Labor costs and price setting}

\subsubsection{Cost minimization and the flexible-price markup}

First find the cheapest labor input for a required $Y_i$:
\begin{align}
 \min_{N_i}WN_i&\quad\text{subject to }AN_i^{1-\alpha}\geq Y_i,\\
 \mathcal L_i&=WN_i+MC_i^n(Y_i-AN_i^{1-\alpha}),\\
 0&=W-MC_i^n(1-\alpha)AN_i^{-\alpha},\\
 MC_i^n&=\frac{W}{(1-\alpha)AN_i^{-\alpha}}
        =\frac{W}{MPN_i}.\label{eq:nk-cost-foc}
\end{align}
The multiplier is the marginal cost of an extra unit of output, not the total
wage bill. Solve the production constraint and differentiate its cost directly:
\begin{align}
 N_i&=(Y_i/A)^{1/(1-\alpha)},\\
 TC_i^n(Y_i)&=WA^{-1/(1-\alpha)}Y_i^{1/(1-\alpha)},\\
 \frac{dTC_i^n}{dY_i}
 &=\frac{W}{1-\alpha}A^{-1/(1-\alpha)}Y_i^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)}=MC_i^n.
\end{align}
For a flexible-price firm, demand implies
\begin{align}
 Y_i&=(P_i/P)^{-\epsilon}Y,
 &\frac{dY_i}{dP_i}&=-\epsilon Y_i/P_i,\\
 \frac{d(P_iY_i-TC_i^n)}{dP_i}
 &=Y_i+P_i\frac{dY_i}{dP_i}-MC_i^n\frac{dY_i}{dP_i}\\
 &=Y_i[1-\epsilon+\epsilon MC_i^n/P_i]=0,\\
 (\epsilon-1)P_i&=\epsilon MC_i^n,
 &P_i&=\mu MC_i^n,\qquad\mu=\frac\epsilon{\epsilon-1}.
\end{align}
For $\alpha=0$, $MC_i^n=W/A$ is common across firms. For $\alpha>0$,
a lower relative price raises sales, hours, and the firm's marginal cost.
This feedback will matter for the Phillips-curve slope.

\begin{redblock}
\textbf{Try two separate limits.}
Set $\theta=0$: every firm can reset, but $\mu>1$ remains. Set
$\epsilon\to\infty$: the markup tends to one, but this does not grant firms
permission to reprice. Flexible prices and perfect competition remove different
restrictions; a steady price in an unchanging economy proves neither one.
\end{redblock}

\subsubsection{Choosing a price that may remain in place}

Let $s=t+k$. Conditional on a reset price surviving, sales are
$Y_{s|t}=(P_t^{\#}/P_s)^{-\epsilon}Y_s$. The household values one nominal unit
at $s$ relative to one at $t$ by the stochastic discount factor
\begin{align}
 \mathcal Q_{t,s}
 &=\beta^k\frac{\lambda_s}{\lambda_t}
 =\beta^k\frac{\xi_s}{\xi_t}
       \left(\frac{C_s}{C_t}\right)^{-\sigma}\frac{P_t}{P_s}.
\end{align}
This is a valuation kernel, not a certain discount rate: it puts greater value
on profits in states where household marginal utility is high. Firm ownership
makes that valuation relevant even though bonds have zero net supply.

A resetter maximizes the expected discounted profits earned while this price
is still in place:
\begin{align}
 \max_{P_t^{\#}}E_t\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\theta^k\mathcal Q_{t,t+k}
 \big[P_t^{\#}Y_{t+k|t}-TC_{t+k}^n(Y_{t+k|t})\big].
\end{align}
Its own future reset choices do not depend on the price chosen now, so the
objective can omit profits after the next reset. The probability $\theta^k$
is separate from both the discount factor and the demand elasticity. Holding
aggregate conditions fixed, differentiate each surviving-price profit:
\begin{align}
 \frac{\partial Y_{s|t}}{\partial P_t^{\#}}
 &=-\epsilon(P_t^{\#}/P_s)^{-\epsilon-1}Y_s/P_s
 =-\epsilon Y_{s|t}/P_t^{\#},\\
 \frac{\partial[P_t^{\#}Y_{s|t}-TC_s^n(Y_{s|t})]}{\partial P_t^{\#}}
 &=Y_{s|t}+P_t^{\#}(-\epsilon Y_{s|t}/P_t^{\#})
                     -MC_{s|t}^n(-\epsilon Y_{s|t}/P_t^{\#})\\
 &=Y_{s|t}[1-\epsilon+\epsilon MC_{s|t}^n/P_t^{\#}].
\end{align}
Set the sum of these derivatives to zero and move terms explicitly:
\begin{align}
 0&=E_t\sum_{k\geq0}\theta^k\mathcal Q_{t,s}Y_{s|t}
             [1-\epsilon+\epsilon MC_{s|t}^n/P_t^{\#}],\\
 (\epsilon-1)E_t\sum_{k\geq0}\theta^k\mathcal Q_{t,s}Y_{s|t}
 &=\frac\epsilon{P_t^{\#}}E_t\sum_{k\geq0}
                  \theta^k\mathcal Q_{t,s}Y_{s|t}MC_{s|t}^n,\\
 P_t^{\#}&=\mu\frac{E_t\sum_{k\geq0}\theta^k\mathcal Q_{t,s}Y_{s|t}MC_{s|t}^n}
                        {E_t\sum_{k\geq0}\theta^k\mathcal Q_{t,s}Y_{s|t}}.
 \label{eq:nk-reset-implicit}
\end{align}
For diminishing returns, this remains an implicit equation because
$MC_{s|t}^n$ depends on the chosen reset price. A long-lived price balances
lost margin at a low price against lost sales at a high one across dates and
states, rather than setting today's markup in every future state.

\subsubsection{The optimal reset price}

Substitute the discount factor and demand into each weight:
\begin{align}
 \theta^k\mathcal Q_{t,s}Y_{s|t}
 &=(\beta\theta)^k\frac{\xi_s}{\xi_t}
       C_s^{-\sigma}C_t^\sigma\frac{P_t}{P_s}
                    (P_t^{\#})^{-\epsilon}P_s^\epsilon Y_s\\
 &=(P_t^{\#})^{-\epsilon}\frac{P_tC_t^\sigma}{\xi_t}
            (\beta\theta)^k F_s,\\
 F_s&=\xi_s C_s^{-\sigma}Y_sP_s^{\epsilon-1}
     =\xi_sY_s^{1-\sigma}P_s^{\epsilon-1}.
\end{align}
The first factor is known at $t$, identical in numerator and denominator, and
cancels. The result at time zero and at a general reset date is, respectively,
\begin{align}
 P_0^{\#}
 &=\mu\frac{E_0\sum_{k\geq0}(\beta\theta)^k F_k MC_{k|0}^n}
                 {E_0\sum_{k\geq0}(\beta\theta)^k F_k},\\
 P_t^{\#}
 &=\mu\frac{E_t\sum_{k\geq0}(\beta\theta)^k F_{t+k}MC_{t+k|t}^n}
                 {E_t\sum_{k\geq0}(\beta\theta)^k F_{t+k}}.
 \label{eq:nk-reset-ratio}
\end{align}
Both sums use $\beta^k\theta^k$. A common $\beta^t$ from dating all values at
zero would cancel; it would not justify dropping $\beta^k$ from one sum.

Define a reference marginal cost evaluated at the average demand quantity $Y_s$:
\begin{align}
 MC_s^n&=\frac{W_s}{1-\alpha}A_s^{-1/(1-\alpha)}Y_s^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)},\\
 MC_{s|t}^n
 &=MC_s^n(Y_{s|t}/Y_s)^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)}\\
 &=MC_s^n(P_t^{\#}/P_s)^{-\zeta},\qquad
 \zeta=\frac{\epsilon\alpha}{1-\alpha},\quad
 \Omega=\frac1{1+\zeta}=\frac{1-\alpha}{1-\alpha+\epsilon\alpha}.
 \label{eq:nk-own-cost}
\end{align}
Inserting this expression into the ratio and multiplying by $(P_t^{\#})^\zeta$
removes the implicit own-price term:
\begin{align}
 (P_t^{\#})^{1+\zeta}
 &=\mu\frac{E_t\sum_{k\geq0}(\beta\theta)^kF_sMC_s^nP_s^\zeta}
                 {E_t\sum_{k\geq0}(\beta\theta)^kF_s}.
\end{align}
For $\alpha=0$, $\zeta=0$, $\Omega=1$, and marginal cost is $W_s/A_s$.
For $\theta=0$, only $k=0$ remains in \eqref{eq:nk-reset-ratio}:
$P_t^{\#}=\mu MC_{t|t}^n$. All resetters make the same decision at a date;
this does not make prices of nonresetting firms identical to theirs.

\begin{blueblock}
\textbf{An infinite sum does not make expectations unnecessary.}
Known current factors may leave $E_t$. Unknown future wages, demand, and prices
stay inside it, even with infinitely many terms. Under uncertainty,
$E_t(XY)$ is generally not $E_tX\,E_tY$, and a ratio of expected sums is not an
expectation of date-specific ratios. The first-order approximation below
removes products of deviations; the exact problem does not.
\end{blueblock}

\subsection{From individual prices to aggregate inflation}

\subsubsection{The reset price near a steady state}

Let $\hat p_t=\log(P_t/\bar P)$, $\hat p_t^{\#}=\log(P_t^{\#}/\bar P)$,
and $\hat{mc}_t^n=\log(MC_t^n/\overline{MC}^n)$.
A symmetric zero-inflation reference has
\begin{align}
 \bar P&=\mu\overline{MC}^n,\qquad
 \bar F=\bar Y^{1-\sigma}\bar P^{\epsilon-1},\qquad
 S=\sum_{k\geq0}(\beta\theta)^k=\frac1{1-\beta\theta}.
\end{align}
Write \eqref{eq:nk-reset-ratio} as a product equality, then divide by its
steady factor $\bar P\bar F$. The left side is
\begin{align}
 \mathcal H_L&=\frac{P_t^{\#}}{\bar P}
             E_t\sum_{k\geq0}(\beta\theta)^k\frac{F_s}{\bar F}.
\end{align}
For clarity, the individual derivatives behind its Taylor expansion are
\begin{align}
 \frac{d\xi}{\bar\xi}&\simeq q,&
 \frac{dY^{1-\sigma}}{\bar Y^{1-\sigma}}&=(1-\sigma)\frac{dY}{\bar Y},&
 \frac{dP^{\epsilon-1}}{\bar P^{\epsilon-1}}&=(\epsilon-1)\frac{dP}{\bar P},\\
 \frac{dF_s}{\bar F}&=q_s+(1-\sigma)\hat y_s+(\epsilon-1)\hat p_s
                  \equiv\hat f_s.
\end{align}
Thus constant terms and first-order terms on both sides are
\begin{align}
 \mathcal H_L&\simeq S+S\hat p_t^{\#}
                  +E_t\sum_{k\geq0}(\beta\theta)^k\hat f_s,\\
 \mathcal H_R&=\frac\mu{\bar P}E_t\sum_{k\geq0}(\beta\theta)^k
                                    \frac{F_s}{\bar F}MC_{s|t}^n\\
 &\simeq S+E_t\sum_{k\geq0}(\beta\theta)^k
                         [\hat f_s+\hat{mc}_{s|t}^n].
\end{align}
If both sides are additionally divided by $P_{t-1}/\bar P$, as in the course,
each gains the same first-order term $-S\hat p_{t-1}$; it cancels too.
Equating the two expansions shows the cancellation, rather than assuming it:
\begin{align}
 S+S\hat p_t^{\#}+E_t\sum_k(\beta\theta)^k\hat f_s
 &=S+E_t\sum_k(\beta\theta)^k\hat f_s
       +E_t\sum_k(\beta\theta)^k\hat{mc}_{s|t}^n,\\
 S\hat p_t^{\#}&=E_t\sum_k(\beta\theta)^k\hat{mc}_{s|t}^n,\\
 \hat p_t^{\#}&=(1-\beta\theta)E_t\sum_k(\beta\theta)^k
                                      \hat{mc}_{s|t}^n.
\end{align}
Now use the own-price effect from \eqref{eq:nk-own-cost}:
\begin{align}
 \hat{mc}_{s|t}^n&=\hat{mc}_s^n-\zeta(\hat p_t^{\#}-\hat p_s),\\
 \hat p_t^{\#}&=(1-\beta\theta)E_t\sum_k(\beta\theta)^k
       [\hat{mc}_s^n+\zeta\hat p_s]-\zeta\hat p_t^{\#},\\
 (1+\zeta)\hat p_t^{\#}
 &=(1-\beta\theta)E_t\sum_k(\beta\theta)^k
                  [\hat{mc}_s^n+\zeta\hat p_s].
\end{align}
Let $\hat{mc}_s=\hat{mc}_s^n-\hat p_s$ be real marginal-cost deviation.
Since $\hat{mc}_s^n+\zeta\hat p_s=(1+\zeta)\hat p_s+\hat{mc}_s$,
\begin{align}
 \hat p_t^{\#}&=(1-\beta\theta)E_t\sum_{k\geq0}(\beta\theta)^k
                     [\hat p_{t+k}+\Omega\hat{mc}_{t+k}].
 \label{eq:nk-reset-linear}
\end{align}
A positive marginal-cost deviation makes a resetter choose a higher price.
Diminishing returns damp that response: raising its own price loses sales and
reduces its own marginal cost, so the desired change is smaller than a
calculation that treats its marginal cost as fixed would imply.

Define $p_t^{\mathrm{tar}}=\hat p_t+\Omega\hat{mc}_t$ as the local pricing
target in this weighted sum. Separate its first term and reindex $h=k-1$:
\begin{align}
 \hat p_t^{\#}
 &=(1-\beta\theta)p_t^{\mathrm{tar}}
  +(1-\beta\theta)E_t\sum_{k\geq1}(\beta\theta)^k p_{t+k}^{\mathrm{tar}}\\
 &=(1-\beta\theta)p_t^{\mathrm{tar}}
  +\beta\theta E_t\left[(1-\beta\theta)
       E_{t+1}\sum_{h\geq0}(\beta\theta)^h p_{t+1+h}^{\mathrm{tar}}\right]\\
 &=(1-\beta\theta)(\hat p_t+\Omega\hat{mc}_t)
                      +\beta\theta E_t\hat p_{t+1}^{\#}.
 \label{eq:nk-reset-recursion}
\end{align}
The second equality uses iterated expectations. It does not remove forecasting
from the pricing problem.

\subsubsection{Reset prices and inherited prices}

A fraction $1-\theta$ chooses $P_t^{\#}$ and the remaining fraction keeps its
old price distribution. Independent selection leaves that distribution
representative of the previous period's distribution, so
\begin{align}
 P_t^{1-\epsilon}
 &=(1-\theta)(P_t^{\#})^{1-\epsilon}+\theta P_{t-1}^{1-\epsilon},\\
 \Pi_t^{1-\epsilon}
 &=(1-\theta)(P_t^{\#}/P_{t-1})^{1-\epsilon}+\theta,
 \qquad\Pi_t=P_t/P_{t-1}.
\end{align}
The Taylor expansion of $z^{1-\epsilon}$ at $z=1$ is
$1+(1-\epsilon)(z-1)\simeq1+(1-\epsilon)\log z$.
Apply it to both ratios:
\begin{align}
 1+(1-\epsilon)\pi_t
 &=(1-\theta)[1+(1-\epsilon)(\hat p_t^{\#}-\hat p_{t-1})]+\theta,\\
 \pi_t&=(1-\theta)(\hat p_t^{\#}-\hat p_{t-1}),\\
 \hat p_t&=(1-\theta)\hat p_t^{\#}+\theta\hat p_{t-1},
 \qquad\pi_t=\log\Pi_t=\hat p_t-\hat p_{t-1}.
 \label{eq:nk-price-aggregation}\\
 r_t^{\#}\equiv\hat p_t^{\#}-\hat p_t
 &=\frac{\pi_t}{1-\theta}-\pi_t
 =\frac\theta{1-\theta}\pi_t.
\end{align}
Inflation is a change in the log price level, not the same variable as that
level. Likewise, the reset price differs from both.

Subtract $\hat p_t$ from \eqref{eq:nk-reset-recursion}, remembering that
$\hat p_{t+1}^{\#}-\hat p_t=r_{t+1}^{\#}+\pi_{t+1}$:
\begin{align}
 r_t^{\#}&=(1-\beta\theta)\Omega\hat{mc}_t
                +\beta\theta E_t(r_{t+1}^{\#}+\pi_{t+1}),\\
 \frac\theta{1-\theta}\pi_t
 &=(1-\beta\theta)\Omega\hat{mc}_t
  +\beta\theta E_t\left[\frac\theta{1-\theta}\pi_{t+1}+\pi_{t+1}\right]\\
 &=(1-\beta\theta)\Omega\hat{mc}_t
                  +\frac{\beta\theta}{1-\theta}E_t\pi_{t+1},\\
 \pi_t&=\beta E_t\pi_{t+1}+\lambda_p\Omega\hat{mc}_t,
 \qquad\lambda_p=\frac{(1-\theta)(1-\beta\theta)}\theta.
 \label{eq:nk-pc-cost}
\end{align}
This is the marginal-cost New Keynesian Phillips curve. It is a local pricing
condition, not an assumed correlation between inflation and unemployment.

\subsubsection{Inflation as a sequence of price changes}

The reset price depends on future price levels, whereas inflation records
the changes between those levels. Writing each price difference as a sum of
inflation rates makes that connection explicit. Subtract $\hat p_{t-1}$ directly in \eqref{eq:nk-reset-linear}:
\begin{align}
 \frac{\pi_t}{1-\theta}
 &=H_t+(1-\beta\theta)E_t\sum_{k\geq0}(\beta\theta)^k
                        (\hat p_{t+k}-\hat p_{t-1}),\\
 H_t&=(1-\beta\theta)\Omega E_t\sum_{k\geq0}(\beta\theta)^k
                         \hat{mc}_{t+k}.
\end{align}
Put $b=\beta\theta$. The price differences telescope into inflation:
\begin{align}
 \hat p_{t+k}-\hat p_{t-1}&=\sum_{h=0}^k\pi_{t+h},\\
 \sum_{k\geq0}b^k\sum_{h=0}^kE_t\pi_{t+h}
 &=\pi_t+b(\pi_t+E_t\pi_{t+1})
       +b^2(\pi_t+E_t\pi_{t+1}+E_t\pi_{t+2})+\cdots\\
 &=\sum_{h\geq0}E_t\pi_{t+h}\sum_{k\geq h}b^k
 =\frac1{1-b}\sum_{h\geq0}b^hE_t\pi_{t+h}.
\end{align}
The rearrangement requires convergent expected discounted sums. Define
$J_t=\sum_{h\geq0}b^hE_t\pi_{t+h}$ and
$V_t=H_t+J_t=\pi_t/(1-\theta)$. Separating their first terms gives
\begin{align}
 H_t&=(1-b)\Omega\hat{mc}_t+bE_tH_{t+1},\\
 J_t&=\pi_t+bE_tJ_{t+1},\\
 \frac{\pi_t}{1-\theta}
 &=\pi_t+(1-b)\Omega\hat{mc}_t+bE_tV_{t+1}\\
 &=\pi_t+(1-b)\Omega\hat{mc}_t
                  +\frac{b}{1-\theta}E_t\pi_{t+1},\\
 \theta\pi_t&=(1-\theta)(1-b)\Omega\hat{mc}_t+bE_t\pi_{t+1},\\
 \pi_t&=\lambda_p\Omega\hat{mc}_t+\beta E_t\pi_{t+1}.
\end{align}
The discount powers and expectations stay attached to every future term.
The cancellation shows how the expected sequence of price changes enters
today's inflation condition.

\subsubsection{Price vintages and the Phillips curve}

The same inflation condition can be understood through the prices firms inherit.
Today's price index combines newly chosen prices with prices set at earlier
dates. If inherited history is generated by the same Calvo mechanism, iterating
\eqref{eq:nk-price-aggregation} backward yields
\begin{align}
 \hat p_t&=(1-\theta)\hat p_t^{\#}+\theta\hat p_{t-1}\\
 &=(1-\theta)\hat p_t^{\#}
       +\theta(1-\theta)\hat p_{t-1}^{\#}+\theta^2\hat p_{t-2}\\
 &=(1-\theta)\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\theta^h\hat p_{t-h}^{\#}
                                     +\theta^H\hat p_{t-H}\\
 &=(1-\theta)\sum_{h\geq0}\theta^h\hat p_{t-h}^{\#}.
\end{align}
The last equality needs a bounded inherited history so the last term vanishes.
The exact CES-vintage expression precedes this log approximation; averaging
price levels arithmetically is not its exact counterpart.

Use the aggregate identity at $t$ and $t+1$:
\begin{align}
 (1-\theta)\hat p_t^{\#}&=\hat p_t-\theta\hat p_{t-1},\\
 (1-\theta)E_t\hat p_{t+1}^{\#}&=E_t\hat p_{t+1}-\theta\hat p_t.
\end{align}
Multiply the reset recursion by $1-\theta$ and substitute those two identities:
\begin{align}
 \hat p_t-\theta\hat p_{t-1}
 &=(1-\theta)(1-\beta\theta)
       (\hat p_t+\Omega\hat{mc}_t)
                 +\beta\theta(E_t\hat p_{t+1}-\theta\hat p_t),\\
 [1+\beta\theta^2-(1-\theta)(1-\beta\theta)]\hat p_t
       -\theta\hat p_{t-1}
 &=(1-\theta)(1-\beta\theta)\Omega\hat{mc}_t
                         +\beta\theta E_t\hat p_{t+1}.
\end{align}
Expand the coefficient instead of leaving the cancellation to the reader:
\begin{align}
 1+\beta\theta^2-(1-\theta-\beta\theta+\beta\theta^2)
 &=\theta+\beta\theta,\\
 \theta(1+\beta)\hat p_t-\theta\hat p_{t-1}
 &=(1-\theta)(1-\beta\theta)\Omega\hat{mc}_t
                       +\beta\theta E_t\hat p_{t+1},\\
 \theta(\hat p_t-\hat p_{t-1})
 &=(1-\theta)(1-\beta\theta)\Omega\hat{mc}_t
                    +\beta\theta(E_t\hat p_{t+1}-\hat p_t),\\
 \pi_t&=\lambda_p\Omega\hat{mc}_t+\beta E_t\pi_{t+1}.
\end{align}
The two routes agree because their pricing target, timing, and aggregation
are now the same. Changing the target coefficient changes the economic model,
not merely the speed of the derivation.

\subsection{Natural output and inflationary pressure}

\subsubsection{Price dispersion and aggregate production}

Use demand and technology firm by firm, then aggregate labor:
\begin{align}
 N_{it}&=(Y_{it}/A_t)^{1/(1-\alpha)}\\
 &=(Y_t/A_t)^{1/(1-\alpha)}
                       (P_{it}/P_t)^{-\epsilon/(1-\alpha)},\\
 N_t&=(Y_t/A_t)^{1/(1-\alpha)}\Delta_t,\\
 Y_t&=A_t(N_t/\Delta_t)^{1-\alpha}.
\end{align}
Let $h=\epsilon/(1-\alpha)$. Calvo selection also implies
\begin{align}
 \Delta_t&=(1-\theta)(P_t^{\#}/P_t)^{-h}
                   +\theta\Pi_t^h\Delta_{t-1}.
\end{align}
At the symmetric reference the first-order deviation satisfies
\begin{align}
 \hat\Delta_t&=-(1-\theta)h r_t^{\#}
                         +\theta h\pi_t+\theta\hat\Delta_{t-1}\\
 &=-\theta h\pi_t+\theta h\pi_t+\theta\hat\Delta_{t-1}
 =\theta\hat\Delta_{t-1}.
\end{align}
Starting at $\Delta_{-1}=1$, first-order dispersion is zero. It generally
reappears at second order and uses real resources. Thus for this local analysis,
\begin{align}
 \hat y_t&=a_t+(1-\alpha)\hat n_t,\qquad
 \hat n_t=(\hat y_t-a_t)/(1-\alpha).
 \label{eq:nk-local-production}
\end{align}
Relative prices matter for efficiency even when their dispersion drops out of
the first-order three-equation system.

\subsubsection{Marginal cost and the flexible-price allocation}

Divide \eqref{eq:nk-cost-foc} by $P_t$ and evaluate cost at the reference
quantity. From household labor supply,
\begin{align}
 mc_t&=\frac{W_t/P_t}{(1-\alpha)A_tN_t^{-\alpha}}
       \quad\text{to first order around symmetry},\\
 \hat{mc}_t
 &=\hat w_t-\hat p_t-a_t+\alpha\hat n_t\\
 &=\sigma\hat c_t+\varphi\hat n_t-a_t+\alpha\hat n_t\\
 &=\sigma\hat y_t+(\varphi+\alpha)
                   \frac{\hat y_t-a_t}{1-\alpha}-a_t\\
 &=\left[\sigma+\frac{\varphi+\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right]\hat y_t
              -\left[\frac{\varphi+\alpha}{1-\alpha}+1\right]a_t\\
 &=\gamma\hat y_t-\frac{1+\varphi}{1-\alpha}a_t,
 \qquad\gamma=\frac{\sigma(1-\alpha)+\varphi+\alpha}{1-\alpha}.
 \label{eq:nk-mc-output}
\end{align}
Here $\hat w_t$ is the nominal wage's log deviation. The intermediate expression
is exact under symmetry; its use with sticky prices is a first-order one.

Define $Y_t^n$ by allowing all firms to reprice at the current technology,
while retaining preferences, market power, and other real distortions.
Symmetry then gives $P_{it}=P_t$ and
\begin{align}
 1&=\mu mc_t^{\mathrm{flex}},\qquad mc_t^{\mathrm{flex}}=1/\mu>0,\\
 \hat{mc}_t^{\mathrm{flex}}
 &=\log[(1/\mu)/(1/\mu)]=0.
\end{align}
The qualifier $\mathrm{flex}$ identifies the natural allocation; uppercase
$MC_t^n$ still denotes nominal marginal cost. Zero \emph{deviation} does not
mean zero marginal cost.

To see the level, combine flexible-price labor supply, technology, and markup:
\begin{align}
 \frac1\mu&=\frac\chi{1-\alpha}(Y_t^n)^\sigma
           (N_t^n)^{\varphi+\alpha}/A_t,\\
 N_t^n&=(Y_t^n/A_t)^{1/(1-\alpha)},\\
 \frac1\mu&=\frac\chi{1-\alpha}
    (Y_t^n)^\gamma A_t^{-(1+\varphi)/(1-\alpha)},\\
 \gamma\log Y_t^n
 &=\log(1-\alpha)-\log\chi-\log\mu
                         +\frac{1+\varphi}{1-\alpha}\log A_t,\\
 \hat y_t^n&=\psi_a a_t,\qquad
 \psi_a=\frac{1+\varphi}{\sigma(1-\alpha)+\varphi+\alpha}.
 \label{eq:nk-natural-output}
\end{align}
Subtract its reference-deviation equation from \eqref{eq:nk-mc-output}:
\begin{align}
 0&=\gamma\hat y_t^n-\frac{1+\varphi}{1-\alpha}a_t,\\
 \hat{mc}_t&=\gamma(\hat y_t-\hat y_t^n)=\gamma x_t,\\
 \pi_t&=\beta E_t\pi_{t+1}+\kappa x_t,
 \qquad x_t=\hat y_t-\hat y_t^n,\quad
 \kappa=\lambda_p\Omega\gamma>0.
 \label{eq:nk-pc-gap}
\end{align}
The coefficient $\gamma$ measures how real marginal cost responds to the output
gap. With decreasing returns, $\Omega$ also tracks the effect of a resetter's
own sales on its marginal cost. It equals one under linear production.

\subsubsection{Desired prices under linear production}

With linear production, the desired relative price has a particularly direct
connection to wages and productivity. Set $\alpha=0$ and write:
\begin{align}
 \frac{P_{it}^d}{P_t}&=\mu\frac{W_t/P_t}{A_t}
               =\mu\chi\frac{Y_t^\sigma N_t^\varphi}{A_t},\\
 N_t&=\int_0^1(Y_{jt}/A_t)\,dj
      =\frac{Y_t}{A_t}\int_0^1(P_{jt}/P_t)^{-\epsilon}\,dj.
\end{align}
Under flexible-price symmetry the integral is one. Around the symmetric
reference its first-order change is zero by the price-index restriction.
Consequently,
\begin{align}
 \log(P_{it}^d/P_t)
 &=\log(\mu\chi)+(\sigma+\varphi)\log Y_t
                              -(1+\varphi)\log A_t
                              \quad\text{under symmetry},\\
 \hat p_t^d-\hat p_t
 &=(\sigma+\varphi)\hat y_t-(1+\varphi)a_t
   =(\sigma+\varphi)x_t\quad\text{locally},\\
 \hat y_t^n&=\frac{1+\varphi}{\sigma+\varphi}a_t.
\end{align}
This gives the same target and natural output as the profit-based derivation
when $\alpha=0$. The common-wage household supplies \emph{aggregate} labor.
Changing the price of a single measure-zero firm does not replace $N_t$
by that firm's $N_{it}$ in the household's labor condition.

\begin{blueblock}
\textbf{The $1+\epsilon\varphi$ denominator and labor supply}
If one substitutes $N_{it}=(P_{it}/P_t)^{-\epsilon}Y_t/A_t$ in place of
aggregate $N_t$, the algebra gives
\[
 (1+\epsilon\varphi)(\hat p_{it}^d-\hat p_t)
       =(\sigma+\varphi)\hat y_t-(1+\varphi)a_t.
\]
The extra denominator is a consequence of that substitution. It does not follow
from the stated common competitive labor market. A separate model with an
appropriate firm-level wage or real rigidity may produce a different target
coefficient; its conditions must be supplied first. Under the common-wage
assumption, aggregate labor and a firm's own employment remain distinct.
\end{blueblock}

\subsubsection{Natural output and efficient output}

For the goods-and-hours planner with symmetric production, solve
\begin{align}
 \max_{N_t>0}\ \xi_t\left[
 v(A_tN_t^{1-\alpha})-\frac{\chi N_t^{1+\varphi}}{1+\varphi}\right].
\end{align}
The chain rule makes the consumption benefit of one more hour explicit:
\begin{align}
 0&=\xi_t\left[v'(Y_t^e)(1-\alpha)A_t(N_t^e)^{-\alpha}
                                    -\chi(N_t^e)^\varphi\right],\\
 (Y_t^e)^{-\sigma}(1-\alpha)A_t(N_t^e)^{-\alpha}
 &=\chi(N_t^e)^\varphi,\\
 \chi(Y_t^e)^\sigma(N_t^e)^{\varphi+\alpha}/[(1-\alpha)A_t]&=1.
\end{align}
Compare the natural condition, whose right side is $1/\mu$:
\begin{align}
 \gamma\log Y_t^e-\gamma\log Y_t^n&=\log\mu,\\
 \log Y_t^e-\log Y_t^n&=\frac{\log\mu}{\gamma}>0.
\end{align}
This comparison uses the moneyless core, or conditions on the separate liquidity
service allocation; it does not solve a planner problem of choosing unlimited
costless money services. Market power depresses natural output relative to the
goods-and-hours efficient level. A subsidy that exactly offsets the markup can
remove this wedge, but that subsidy is not part of the baseline conditions.
A zero natural output gap therefore need not maximize welfare. The policy
chapter develops the objective and instruments; here the benchmark must first
be identified correctly.

A restaurant can have spare tables because demand is weak, yet an inefficiently
high markup can also restrict its flexible-price output. These are different
comparisons. Likewise, $Y^n$ is a model counterfactual, not a measured smooth
trend or a technical maximum capacity.

\subsection{Dynamic IS: household timing and the natural real rate}

Multiply \eqref{eq:nk-euler-exact} by $R_t^n$. With
$\beta\bar R^n=1$, its first-order expansion is
\begin{align}
 1&=\beta R_t^n E_t\left[
  \frac{\xi_{t+1}}{\xi_t}(C_{t+1}/C_t)^{-\sigma}e^{-\pi_{t+1}}\right],\\
 1&\simeq E_t\big[(1+j_t)
    \{1+q_{t+1}-q_t-\sigma(\hat c_{t+1}-\hat c_t)-\pi_{t+1}\}\big],\\
 0&=j_t+E_tq_{t+1}-q_t
           -\sigma(E_t\hat c_{t+1}-\hat c_t)-E_t\pi_{t+1},\\
 \hat y_t&=E_t\hat y_{t+1}
      -\frac1\sigma(j_t-E_t\pi_{t+1})
      +\frac1\sigma(q_t-E_tq_{t+1}).\label{eq:nk-dis-level}
\end{align}
Products such as $j_t\pi_{t+1}$ are second order and have been dropped.
The equality is between conditional expected marginal returns. It is not an
exact claim that expected real returns equal a ratio of expected consumption.

At the flexible-price allocation, define the natural log real-rate deviation
$r_t^n$ by the same consumption Euler condition:
\begin{align}
 r_t^n&=\sigma(E_t\hat y_{t+1}^n-\hat y_t^n)+q_t-E_tq_{t+1},\\
 \hat y_t^n&=E_t\hat y_{t+1}^n-r_t^n/\sigma
                             +(q_t-E_tq_{t+1})/\sigma.
\end{align}
Subtract this equation from \eqref{eq:nk-dis-level} term by term:
\begin{align}
 \hat y_t-\hat y_t^n
 &=E_t(\hat y_{t+1}-\hat y_{t+1}^n)
       -\frac1\sigma[j_t-E_t\pi_{t+1}-r_t^n],\\
 x_t&=E_tx_{t+1}-\frac1\sigma(j_t-E_t\pi_{t+1}-r_t^n).
 \label{eq:nk-dis-gap}
\end{align}
Under the processes stated in the conditions,
\begin{align}
 r_t^n&=\sigma\psi_a(\rho_a-1)a_t+(1-\rho_q)q_t.
\end{align}
The natural rate is not automatically constant when natural output changes.
A temporarily high productivity level implies expected consumption declines
as productivity reverts; the real return consistent with that spending path
is below its reference. A temporary increase in the weight on present utility
instead raises the rate required to make households defer consumption.

\begin{greenblock}
\textbf{The relevant policy stance is a real-rate gap.}
When $j_t-E_t\pi_{t+1}>r_t^n$, households wish to postpone consumption relative
to the flexible-price path. With prices still in place, firms meet lower demand
by selling less and hiring fewer hours. The wage and marginal cost fall; firms
that reset then choose lower prices. A lower nominal rate alone does not prove
an expansionary stance if expected inflation or the natural rate fell further.
This is a hypothetical mechanism, not a diagnosis of a particular recession.
\end{greenblock}

\subsection{Fixed-duration contracts and staggered adjustment}

Suppose firms know exactly how long their prices will remain in place. Divide
them into three equal cohorts that take turns resetting: one third resets every
date, and each reset price lasts exactly three periods. The duration is known; future aggregate
conditions remain uncertain. There are three cohorts of firms, not just three
strategic firms whose individual decisions move the price index.

With the same demand and cost function, the reset problem now sums only
$k=0,1,2$. The exact FOC becomes
\begin{align}
 P_t^{(3)}&=\mu\frac{E_t\sum_{k=0}^{2}\mathcal Q_{t,t+k}
                               Y_{t+k|t}MC_{t+k|t}^n}
                         {E_t\sum_{k=0}^{2}\mathcal Q_{t,t+k}Y_{t+k|t}}.
\end{align}
There is no survival factor $\theta^k$: survival for these three dates is certain.
The earlier Taylor cancellation, applied to the finite sum, gives
\begin{align}
 (1+\zeta)\hat p_t^{(3)}\sum_{k=0}^2\beta^k
 &=E_t\sum_{k=0}^2\beta^k
                  [(1+\zeta)\hat p_{t+k}+\hat{mc}_{t+k}],\\
 \hat p_t^{(3)}
 &=\frac{p_t^{\mathrm{tar}}+\beta E_tp_{t+1}^{\mathrm{tar}}
                        +\beta^2E_tp_{t+2}^{\mathrm{tar}}}{1+\beta+\beta^2}.
\end{align}
Only with the additional no-discounting finite-horizon simplification $\beta=1$
is this an arithmetic average divided by three. Aggregate pricing is
\begin{align}
 P_t^{1-\epsilon}
 &=\frac13\big[(P_t^{(3)})^{1-\epsilon}
              +(P_{t-1}^{(3)})^{1-\epsilon}+(P_{t-2}^{(3)})^{1-\epsilon}\big],\\
 \hat p_t&=\frac13(\hat p_t^{(3)}+\hat p_{t-1}^{(3)}+\hat p_{t-2}^{(3)}),\\
 \pi_t&=\frac13(\hat p_t^{(3)}-\hat p_{t-3}^{(3)}).
\end{align}
The previous reset price drops out exactly after three dates. Under Calvo,
its survival probability instead declines geometrically and has no fixed end.
Both mechanisms stagger adjustment, but these different vintage laws do not
produce the same Phillips curve. Knowing the duration of a contract is not
perfect foresight about demand over its life.\footnote{See Taylor (1980),
\textit{Aggregate Dynamics and Staggered Contracts}, Journal of Political
Economy 88(1), 1--23, and Calvo (1983), \textit{Staggered Prices in a
Utility-Maximizing Framework}, Journal of Monetary Economics 12(3), 383--398.
The three-period price example here is a teaching construction.}

\subsection{Monetary policy and equilibrium}

The household and firm problems deliver two core equations:
\begin{align}
 \pi_t&=\beta E_t\pi_{t+1}+\kappa x_t,\\
 x_t&=E_tx_{t+1}-\frac1\sigma(j_t-E_t\pi_{t+1}-r_t^n).
\end{align}
They do not yet determine the policy instrument. Specifying its rule supplies
another equilibrium relation; merely adding another unknown does not close
the system. We must also specify initial states, shock processes, and the
bounded or terminal behavior selecting admissible paths.

\subsubsection{Equilibrium with an exogenous money supply}

With money, add the household condition and price-level accounting:
\begin{align}
 \hat m_t-\hat p_t&=\alpha_m(x_t+\hat y_t^n)-\eta j_t,\\
 \hat p_t&=\hat p_{t-1}+\pi_t.
\end{align}
A quantity policy additionally specifies $\hat m_t$; for example,
\begin{align}
 \hat m_t&=\rho_m\hat m_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t^m,\qquad |\rho_m|<1.
\end{align}
This example is a stationary \emph{level} deviation of money, not an assumption
about money growth. Given inherited $\hat p_{t-1}$ and money history, this is a
closed forward-looking system in $x_t,\pi_t,j_t,\hat p_t$ and the exogenous
state. Closure alone does not prove a unique bounded rational-expectations
solution. Its root conditions depend on the chosen money rule; the Taylor-rule
proof below applies to a different closure.

The identities $\pi_t=\hat p_t-\hat p_{t-1}$ and $MV=PY$ do not specify a
money process. The latter defines velocity $V=PY/M$. Setting velocity constant
adds a restriction and generally conflicts with the interest-elastic money
demand derived here. Technology, preferences, and policy innovations can all
be exogenous; the word ``real'' is not a synonym for ``exogenous.''

\subsubsection{Equilibrium under an interest-rate rule}

Let the authority respond to current inflation and the natural output gap,
with a constant zero inflation target:
\begin{align}
 j_t&=\phi_\pi\pi_t+\phi_x x_t+\nu_t,\qquad
 \phi_\pi,\phi_x\geq0,\\
 \nu_t&=\rho_\nu\nu_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t^\nu,\qquad 0\leq\rho_\nu<1.
 \label{eq:nk-rule}
\end{align}
The coefficients describe systematic responses; $\nu_t$ is a deviation from
that rule. A change in a coefficient changes the rule itself and is not the
same experiment as a one-time innovation. The rule is written in log gross-rate
deviations; $\phi_x$ multiplies the output gap, not output without qualification.
A rule can instead respond to output relative to its steady reference.
The distinction matters when technology moves:
\begin{align}
 j_t&=\phi_\pi\pi_t+\phi_x\hat y_t+\nu_t\notag\\
 &=\phi_\pi\pi_t+\phi_x x_t+(\nu_t+\phi_x\hat y_t^n).
\end{align}
Thus the level-output rule uses the same gap-system matrix but an effective
forcing term $\nu_t+\phi_x\hat y_t^n$. In the monetary experiment below,
$\hat y_t^n=0$ and the two conventions coincide. For a productivity shock they
must be distinguished. Our baseline rule responds to the natural gap.

It does not automatically track the natural rate. One could instead add
$r_t^n$ to its right side, but that would be a different rule.

If money services are present, recover $\hat m_t$ from
\eqref{eq:nk-money-linear} after solving the core. With separable money utility,
that recovery does not feed back into the goods and hours equations. If money
is omitted, there is no money equation to solve. In neither case may an
independent fixed money stock be added while keeping the same rate rule.

\subsubsection{Output and inflation as a joint dynamic system}

Substitute the interest rule into dynamic IS, then substitute the Phillips
curve for current inflation:
\begin{align}
 \sigma x_t&=\sigma E_tx_{t+1}-\phi_\pi\pi_t-\phi_xx_t-\nu_t
                                      +E_t\pi_{t+1}+r_t^n,\\
 (\sigma+\phi_x)x_t
 &=\sigma E_tx_{t+1}-\phi_\pi(\beta E_t\pi_{t+1}+\kappa x_t)
                                  +E_t\pi_{t+1}+r_t^n-\nu_t,\\
 D x_t&=\sigma E_tx_{t+1}+(1-\beta\phi_\pi)E_t\pi_{t+1}+r_t^n-\nu_t,
 \qquad D=\sigma+\phi_x+\kappa\phi_\pi>0,\\
 x_t&=\frac\sigma D E_tx_{t+1}
    +\frac{1-\beta\phi_\pi}{D}E_t\pi_{t+1}+\frac{r_t^n-\nu_t}{D}.
\end{align}
Use this result in current inflation and put terms over the same denominator:
\begin{align}
 \pi_t&=\beta E_t\pi_{t+1}
      +\frac\kappa D[\sigma E_tx_{t+1}
        +(1-\beta\phi_\pi)E_t\pi_{t+1}+r_t^n-\nu_t],\\
 \beta D+\kappa(1-\beta\phi_\pi)
 &=\beta\sigma+\beta\phi_x+\beta\kappa\phi_\pi
                             +\kappa-\kappa\beta\phi_\pi\\
 &=\kappa+\beta(\sigma+\phi_x),\\
 \pi_t&=\frac{\kappa\sigma}{D}E_tx_{t+1}
    +\frac{\kappa+\beta(\sigma+\phi_x)}D E_t\pi_{t+1}
    +\frac\kappa D(r_t^n-\nu_t).
\end{align}
Thus, for $z_t=(x_t,\pi_t)'$,
\begin{align}
 z_t&=\mathcal A E_tz_{t+1}+b(r_t^n-\nu_t),\label{eq:nk-matrix}\\
 \mathcal A&=\frac1D
 \begin{bmatrix}\sigma&1-\beta\phi_\pi\\
 \kappa\sigma&\kappa+\beta(\sigma+\phi_x)\end{bmatrix},
 \qquad b=\frac1D\begin{bmatrix}1\\\kappa\end{bmatrix}.
\end{align}
The lower shock coefficient is $\kappa/D$, not $1/D$. Both rows must represent
the same substitution; this coefficient is easy to lose when compressing steps.

\subsubsection{When is the equilibrium unique?}

Conditional on exogenous states, $x_t$ and $\pi_t$ are both jump variables in
this baseline. There is no inherited capital stock fixing one of them. Since
\eqref{eq:nk-matrix} maps future expectations \emph{back} to the present, a
unique bounded fundamental solution requires its two eigenvalues inside the
unit circle. Equivalently, its inverse, the forward transition matrix, needs
two roots outside it. This is the relevant root count for the local system;
changing the direction of the transition reverses the root test.\footnote{The
general linear rational-expectations root-counting result is Blanchard and
Kahn (1980), \textit{The Solution of Linear Difference Models under Rational
Expectations}, Econometrica 48(5), 1305--1311. Here we also derive the
special two-variable criterion rather than invoking the name alone.}

Compute trace $T$ and determinant $d$ directly:
\begin{align}
 T&=\frac{\sigma+\kappa+\beta(\sigma+\phi_x)}D,\\
 d&=\frac{\sigma[\kappa+\beta(\sigma+\phi_x)]
                   -\kappa\sigma(1-\beta\phi_\pi)}{D^2}\\
  &=\frac{\sigma\beta(\sigma+\phi_x+\kappa\phi_\pi)}{D^2}
   =\frac{\sigma\beta}{D}.
\end{align}
The characteristic polynomial is $\ell^2-T\ell+d=0$. To check its roots without
presuming they are real, set $\ell=(1+s)/(1-s)$. A root inside the unit disk
corresponds to $\operatorname{Re}s<0$, since
\begin{align}
 \left|\frac{1+s}{1-s}\right|<1
 &\iff |1+s|^2<|1-s|^2\iff 4\operatorname{Re}s<0.
\end{align}
Multiplying the transformed polynomial by $(1-s)^2$ gives
\begin{align}
 0&=(1+s)^2-T(1+s)(1-s)+d(1-s)^2\\
  &=(1+T+d)s^2+2(1-d)s+(1-T+d).
\end{align}
Here $1+T+d>0$. For real roots, negative sum and positive product put both
on the negative half-line; for a conjugate pair, the real part is half their
sum. The remaining conditions are therefore
\begin{align}
 1-d&>0,&1-T+d&>0,&1+T+d&>0.
\end{align}
The first and third hold for the stated nonnegative policy coefficients:
\begin{align}
 D-\sigma\beta&=\sigma(1-\beta)+\phi_x+\kappa\phi_\pi>0,\\
 D(1-T+d)
 &=D-\sigma-\kappa-\beta(\sigma+\phi_x)+\sigma\beta\\
 &=\kappa(\phi_\pi-1)+(1-\beta)\phi_x.
\end{align}
Hence the strict local determinacy condition is
\begin{align}
 \kappa(\phi_\pi-1)+(1-\beta)\phi_x&>0.
 \label{eq:nk-determinacy}
\end{align}
With $\phi_x=0$, this is $\phi_\pi>1$: an inflation increase induces a larger
nominal-rate increase, raising the real rate rather than validating higher
demand. Equality leaves a unit root and does not pass the strict test.

For completeness, iteration under these conditions gives
\begin{align}
 z_t&=\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\mathcal A^h b
                 E_t(r_{t+h}^n-\nu_{t+h})+\mathcal A^HE_tz_{t+H},\\
 z_t&=\sum_{h\geq0}\mathcal A^h bE_t(r_{t+h}^n-\nu_{t+h}).
\end{align}
The terminal term vanishes for bounded conditional first moments as
$\mathcal A^H\to0$. In this solution class, any difference between two solutions
has only that vanishing terminal term, proving uniqueness. Stationary shocks
with finite moments and convergent forecasts suffice here. This is local
uniqueness, not a claim of global uniqueness with lower bounds or nonlinear
policy rules.

\begin{blueblock}
\textbf{Why the Ramsey root picture cannot be copied unchanged.}
Ramsey has an inherited capital stock and a consumption choice. This labor-only
NK core has two current jump variables and exogenous inherited shocks. The
technique of selecting admissible paths is related; the state count and the
matrix direction must be checked anew. The inherited nominal price level still
matters for levels, but it drops out of this inflation-targeting gap system.
\end{blueblock}

\subsection{The effects of a monetary policy shock}

\subsubsection{Solving for output and inflation}

Hold technology and the preference shifter at their reference, so
$\hat y_t^n=r_t^n=0$. For the AR(1) rule disturbance, try
\begin{align}
 x_t&=a_\nu\nu_t,\qquad \pi_t=b_\nu\nu_t,\\
 E_t\nu_{t+1}&=\rho_\nu\nu_t,
 \quad E_t\varepsilon_{t+1}^\nu=0,\\
 E_tx_{t+1}&=a_\nu\rho_\nu\nu_t,
 \quad E_t\pi_{t+1}=b_\nu\rho_\nu\nu_t.
\end{align}
In the Phillips curve, equality for every possible state $\nu_t$ requires
\begin{align}
 b_\nu\nu_t&=\beta b_\nu\rho_\nu\nu_t+\kappa a_\nu\nu_t,\\
 (1-\beta\rho_\nu)b_\nu&=\kappa a_\nu,\qquad
 a_\nu=\frac{1-\beta\rho_\nu}{\kappa}b_\nu.
\end{align}
In dynamic IS, substitute both coefficients and the rate rule:
\begin{align}
 a_\nu\nu_t&=a_\nu\rho_\nu\nu_t
 -\frac1\sigma[\phi_\pi b_\nu\nu_t+\phi_xa_\nu\nu_t
                           +\nu_t-b_\nu\rho_\nu\nu_t],\\
 \sigma a_\nu&=\sigma\rho_\nu a_\nu-\phi_xa_\nu
                                     -(\phi_\pi-\rho_\nu)b_\nu-1,\\
 [\sigma(1-\rho_\nu)+\phi_x]a_\nu
                       +(\phi_\pi-\rho_\nu)b_\nu&=-1,\\
 \left\{[\sigma(1-\rho_\nu)+\phi_x]
       \frac{1-\beta\rho_\nu}{\kappa}+\phi_\pi-\rho_\nu\right\}b_\nu&=-1.
\end{align}
Define the full denominator before writing the answer:
\begin{align}
 D_\nu&=[\sigma(1-\rho_\nu)+\phi_x](1-\beta\rho_\nu)
                                     +\kappa(\phi_\pi-\rho_\nu),\\
 \Lambda_\nu&=D_\nu^{-1},\\
 b_\nu&=-\kappa\Lambda_\nu,\qquad
 a_\nu=-(1-\beta\rho_\nu)\Lambda_\nu,\\
 x_t&=-(1-\beta\rho_\nu)\Lambda_\nu\nu_t,\qquad
 \pi_t=-\kappa\Lambda_\nu\nu_t.\label{eq:nk-shock-solution}
\end{align}
Under \eqref{eq:nk-determinacy}, $D_\nu>0$ for $0\leq\rho_\nu<1$.
To check the sign, put $d_\rho=1-\rho_\nu>0$ and expand:
\begin{align}
 D_\nu&=[\sigma d_\rho+\phi_x](1-\beta+\beta d_\rho)
                             +\kappa(\phi_\pi-1+d_\rho)\\
 &=\underbrace{(1-\beta)\phi_x+\kappa(\phi_\pi-1)}_{>0}
   +d_\rho[\sigma(1-\beta)+\beta\phi_x+\kappa]
   +\sigma\beta d_\rho^2>0.
\end{align}
A positive policy-rule disturbance reduces the output gap and inflation in
this experiment. Its persistence changes both the duration and the initial
response because anticipated future conditions already affect today's choices.

\subsubsection{The paths of output, inflation, and interest rates}

Substitute the shock process into the output solution, then identify the lagged
solution rather than treating a jump variable as predetermined:
\begin{align}
 x_t&=-(1-\beta\rho_\nu)\Lambda_\nu
                       (\rho_\nu\nu_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t^\nu)\\
 &=\rho_\nu[-(1-\beta\rho_\nu)\Lambda_\nu\nu_{t-1}]
                      -(1-\beta\rho_\nu)\Lambda_\nu\varepsilon_t^\nu\\
 &=\rho_\nu x_{t-1}-(1-\beta\rho_\nu)\Lambda_\nu\varepsilon_t^\nu,\\
 \pi_t&=-\kappa\Lambda_\nu(\rho_\nu\nu_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t^\nu)
       =\rho_\nu\pi_{t-1}-\kappa\Lambda_\nu\varepsilon_t^\nu.
\end{align}
These are implied equilibrium paths under the stated single-shock experiment;
$x_{t-1}$ is not an additional independent initial condition.

The first-order expected real-rate deviation is $r_t=j_t-E_t\pi_{t+1}$.
Use dynamic IS and then the solved coefficients:
\begin{align}
 r_t&=\sigma(E_tx_{t+1}-x_t)
     =\sigma(\rho_\nu-1)a_\nu\nu_t\\
 &=\sigma(1-\rho_\nu)(1-\beta\rho_\nu)\Lambda_\nu\nu_t,\\
 j_t&=r_t+E_t\pi_{t+1}\\
 &=[\sigma(1-\rho_\nu)(1-\beta\rho_\nu)-\rho_\nu\kappa]
                                               \Lambda_\nu\nu_t.
 \label{eq:nk-rate-response}
\end{align}
The real rate rises for a positive disturbance. The nominal rate's sign also
depends on falling expected inflation; a positive rule intercept is not a
universal guarantee that the realized nominal rate rises.

\subsubsection{Price levels and the demand for money}

Consider one innovation $\varepsilon_0^\nu=e>0$, zero subsequent innovations,
and zero prehistory. Then $\nu_t=\rho_\nu^t e$. Accumulate inflation to recover
the price level:
\begin{align}
 \hat p_t-\hat p_{-1}
 &=\sum_{h=0}^t\pi_h
 =-\kappa\Lambda_\nu e\sum_{h=0}^t\rho_\nu^h\\
 &=-\kappa\Lambda_\nu e\frac{1-\rho_\nu^{t+1}}{1-\rho_\nu}.
\end{align}
Let $J_\nu=[\sigma(1-\rho_\nu)(1-\beta\rho_\nu)
-\rho_\nu\kappa]\Lambda_\nu$. Money demand now determines money supply:
\begin{align}
 \hat m_t&=\hat p_t+\alpha_mx_t-\eta j_t\\
 &=\hat p_{-1}-\kappa\Lambda_\nu e
                \frac{1-\rho_\nu^{t+1}}{1-\rho_\nu}
       -\alpha_m(1-\beta\rho_\nu)\Lambda_\nu\rho_\nu^te
       -\eta J_\nu\rho_\nu^te,\\
 \frac{\partial\hat m_t}{\partial e}
 &=-\kappa\Lambda_\nu\frac{1-\rho_\nu^{t+1}}{1-\rho_\nu}
       -\alpha_m(1-\beta\rho_\nu)\Lambda_\nu\rho_\nu^t
       -\eta J_\nu\rho_\nu^t.\label{eq:nk-money-response}
\end{align}
The derivative is with respect to the \emph{initial innovation}, keeping
prehistory fixed. A derivative with respect to current $\nu_t$ holding today's
price level fixed is a different experiment because the price level records
past inflation. The three terms represent the price-level path, transactions,
and the opportunity cost of liquidity. Their joint sign must be checked, not
presumed from $MV=PY$.

As $t\to\infty$, $x_t,\pi_t,j_t,r_t$ return to zero while
\begin{align}
 \hat p_\infty-\hat p_{-1}
 &=\hat m_\infty-\hat p_{-1}
 =-\frac{\kappa\Lambda_\nu}{1-\rho_\nu}e.
\end{align}
An inflation-targeting rule need not undo the accumulated price-level change.
A price-level target would change that result. With $\rho_\nu=0$, the gap and
inflation effects last one period, but the price-level change remains; delayed
peaks do not arise automatically from Calvo staggering alone in this baseline.

\subsubsection{A numerical policy experiment}

Choose illustrative quarterly parameters, without estimation:
\begin{align}
 \beta&=0.99,\quad\sigma=\varphi=1,\quad\alpha=1/3,
 \quad\epsilon=6,\quad\theta=0.75,\\
 \phi_\pi&=1.5,\quad\phi_x=0.125,\quad\rho_\nu=0.5,
 \quad\nu_m=2,\quad e=0.001.
\end{align}
Compute the pricing slope and shock denominator:
\begin{align}
 \lambda_p&=\frac{0.25(1-0.99\times0.75)}{0.75}=0.0858333,\\
 \Omega&=\frac{2/3}{2/3+6/3}=0.25,\qquad
 \gamma=\frac{2/3+1+1/3}{2/3}=3,\\
 \kappa&=0.0858333\times0.25\times3=0.064375,\\
 D_\nu&=[1(1-0.5)+0.125](1-0.99\times0.5)
                           +0.064375(1.5-0.5)=0.38,\\
 x_0&=-\frac{0.505}{0.38}(0.001)=-0.001328947,\\
 \pi_0&=-\frac{0.064375}{0.38}(0.001)=-0.000169408,\\
 r_0&=\frac{0.5\times0.505}{0.38}(0.001)=0.000664474,\\
 j_0&=r_0+0.5\pi_0=0.000579770.
\end{align}
The ten-basis-point log-rate intercept innovation raises the nominal rate by
about $5.80$ basis points and the real rate by $6.64$ basis points on impact.
Output falls about $0.133\%$ and quarterly log inflation by $1.69$ basis points.
These are unannualized, local model responses, not estimated policy effects.

For money, $\bar i=1/0.99-1$, so $\alpha_m=0.5$ and $\eta=49.5$:
\begin{align}
 \hat m_0&=\pi_0+0.5x_0-49.5j_0\simeq-0.0295325,\\
 \hat p_\infty&=-\frac{0.064375}{0.38(1-0.5)}(0.001)
                  \simeq-0.000338816.
\end{align}
Money's larger log response reflects the selected liquidity semi-elasticity;
it is not an additional exogenous monetary shock. First-order accuracy is
local, especially for this large money-demand semi-elasticity.

\subsection{Expectations, persistence, and the model's limits}

\subsubsection{Current inflation depends on a path of gaps}

Iterate the Phillips curve forward without suppressing expectations:
\begin{align}
 \pi_t&=\kappa x_t+\beta E_t\pi_{t+1}\\
 &=\kappa x_t+\beta E_t[\kappa x_{t+1}+\beta E_{t+1}\pi_{t+2}]\\
 &=\kappa x_t+\kappa\beta E_tx_{t+1}+\beta^2E_t\pi_{t+2}\\
 &=\kappa\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\beta^hE_tx_{t+h}+\beta^HE_t\pi_{t+H}.
\end{align}
If inflation has bounded conditional first moments, the final term vanishes:
\begin{align}
 \pi_t&=\kappa\sum_{h\geq0}\beta^hE_tx_{t+h}.
\end{align}
A resetter cares about future costs while its price survives. This is why
news about future demand can affect pricing now. It does not mean that agents
know future innovations, or that changing expectations without an equilibrium
mechanism is a free policy instrument.

A productivity improvement illustrates why the benchmark matters. Under
\eqref{eq:nk-rule}, let $\nu=q=0$ and $a_t$ be the only AR(1) state. Set
$R_a=\sigma\psi_a(\rho_a-1)$, so $r_t^n=R_a a_t$.
Try $x_t=A_a a_t$ and $\pi_t=B_a a_t$. Direct substitution gives
\begin{align}
 (1-\beta\rho_a)B_a&=\kappa A_a,\\
 [\sigma(1-\rho_a)+\phi_x]A_a+(\phi_\pi-\rho_a)B_a&=R_a,\\
 \left\{[\sigma(1-\rho_a)+\phi_x]
       \frac{1-\beta\rho_a}{\kappa}+\phi_\pi-\rho_a\right\}B_a&=R_a.
\end{align}
Multiplying by $\kappa$ gives the denominator and the coefficients below:

\begin{align}
 D_a&=[\sigma(1-\rho_a)+\phi_x](1-\beta\rho_a)
                                  +\kappa(\phi_\pi-\rho_a),\\
 x_t&=\frac{(1-\beta\rho_a)R_a}{D_a}a_t,\qquad
 \pi_t=\frac{\kappa R_a}{D_a}a_t,\\
 \hat y_t&=\psi_a a_t+x_t.
\end{align}
For $0\leq\rho_a<1$, $R_a<0$: this fixed-intercept rule lets a positive
productivity shock open a negative natural gap and reduce inflation, while
natural output rises. One cannot infer the sign of total output from the gap
alone. A rule that also tracks $r_t^n$ admits $x_t=\pi_t=0$ for this shock
under the same determinacy conditions. The technology is the same; the policy
response changes how closely demand follows the moving benchmark.

\subsubsection{Price indexation and inflation persistence}

What makes past inflation affect current pricing directly? One possibility
is indexation: a nonresetting firm updates its price using the previous
period's aggregate inflation at rate $\iota\in[0,1]$:
\begin{align}
 P_{it}&=P_{i,t-1}\Pi_{t-1}^{\iota}\quad\text{if it does not reset}.
\end{align}
This changes the price contract, not the monetary shock. Introduce a common
log index $I_t-I_{t-1}=\iota\pi_{t-1}$ and adjusted log prices
$\tilde p_{it}=\log(P_{it}/\bar P)-I_t$.
For a nonresetter,
\begin{align}
 \tilde p_{it}-\tilde p_{i,t-1}
 &=[\log P_{it}-\log P_{i,t-1}]-(I_t-I_{t-1})\\
 &=\iota\pi_{t-1}-\iota\pi_{t-1}=0.
\end{align}
In these adjusted units the contract is fixed again. Relative prices and real
marginal costs are unchanged by subtracting a common index. The previous
first-order reset and aggregation calculations therefore apply to adjusted
inflation $\tilde\pi_t=\pi_t-\iota\pi_{t-1}$:
\begin{align}
 \tilde\pi_t&=\beta E_t\tilde\pi_{t+1}+\kappa x_t,\\
 \pi_t-\iota\pi_{t-1}
 &=\beta E_t(\pi_{t+1}-\iota\pi_t)+\kappa x_t,\\
 (1+\beta\iota)\pi_t
 &=\beta E_t\pi_{t+1}+\iota\pi_{t-1}+\kappa x_t,\\
 \pi_t&=\frac\beta{1+\beta\iota}E_t\pi_{t+1}
       +\frac\iota{1+\beta\iota}\pi_{t-1}
       +\frac\kappa{1+\beta\iota}x_t.
\end{align}
For $\iota=0$ this returns to the baseline. With indexation, lagged inflation
is inherited and the equilibrium root count changes. Simply adding
$\pi_{t-1}$ with coefficient one to the old Phillips curve would omit both
the changed contract and the normalization of its other coefficients.

\begin{blueblock}
\textbf{Sticky information asks a different question.}
Calvo firms observe the current state but may be unable to change their prices.
With sticky information, firms may revise information or plans only occasionally;
prices can follow plans made with older information. Mankiw and Reis (2002)
study that alternative. It is not obtained by deleting $E_t$, calling rational
expectations perfect foresight, or adding an arbitrary lag.\footnote{Mankiw
and Reis, \href{https://www.nber.org/papers/w8290}{Sticky Information versus
Sticky Prices}, working paper 2001, published in the Quarterly Journal of
Economics 117(4), 2002, 1295--1328.}
\end{blueblock}

\subsubsection{Which mechanisms are still missing?}

A weaker spending path with unchanged technology is a demand mechanism here;
more expensive inputs or a changing markup need additional wedges. The
constant-markup baseline has no independent cost-push term $u_t$. Adding
$\pi_t=\beta E_t\pi_{t+1}+\kappa x_t+u_t$ can be useful, but $u_t$ must
be tied to a changed cost, markup, or wage-setting condition before interpreting
it structurally. A technology shock already affects natural output and the
natural rate; it is not automatically an extra $u_t$ in the natural-gap equation.

There is no unemployment stock, job search, borrowing constraint, bank balance
sheet, investment, imported input, or household income distribution in this
chapter. Hours fall through an interior labor choice and product demand, not
through a matching failure. Financial stress cannot be represented merely by
renaming the policy innovation. Likewise, a central bank setting a negative
unconstrained rate in the local solution may be infeasible at a lower bound.
These omissions suggest the next models and policy extensions rather than
invalidating the mechanism just isolated.

\Needspace{10\baselineskip}
\subsection{IS--LM and AD--AS with microfoundations}
\label{sec:nk-islm-adas}

The familiar diagrams are useful here because their ingredients have just
been derived. We can now see which household or firm choice supplies each
slope, which expectations are being held fixed, and which policy closure makes
an LM curve relevant. To connect the dynamic model to the familiar diagrams,
first examine current decisions conditional on a given future outlook, then
ask how that outlook changes in equilibrium.

\subsubsection{Intertemporal consumption and the IS curve}

In the intermediate model, a common starting point is
\begin{align}
 Y&=C(Y-T)+I(i)+G.
\end{align}
Behavioral aggregate functions close that equation. Their signs can be useful
and empirically informed without being derived from a household optimization
problem. They need not be arbitrary, but they alone do not specify how
expectations or a new policy regime alter behavior.

In the present model there is no investment or government purchase. Demand
comes entirely from intertemporal consumption. Keep the future outlook fixed
for a \emph{conditional current-period comparison} and define
\begin{align}
 d_t&=E_t\hat y_{t+1}+(q_t-E_tq_{t+1})/\sigma,\qquad
 e_t=E_t\pi_{t+1}.
\end{align}
Then \eqref{eq:nk-dis-level} is
\begin{align}
 \hat y_t&=d_t-\frac1\sigma(j_t-e_t),\\
 j_t&=e_t+\sigma d_t-\sigma\hat y_t,\qquad
 \left.\frac{\partial j_t}{\partial\hat y_t}\right|_{d_t,e_t}=-\sigma.
 \label{eq:nk-static-is}
\end{align}
This is downward-sloping in $(\hat y,j)$ space because a higher real return
makes households defer consumption. It is a current slice of the dynamic
Euler condition. It is not a derivation of the original $I(i)$ term, since
this economy has no capital. Deriving an investment schedule would require
adding capital, its return, and any adjustment costs.

The household saves in a bond, yet bonds have zero net supply. There is no
contradiction: when every household wants to postpone purchases at the quoted
rate, current income and prices must adjust until desired holdings clear.
Individual desired saving and realized aggregate saving are different objects.

\subsubsection{Money demand and the LM curve}

With a fixed current money stock and price level, money-market clearing is
\begin{align}
 \hat m_t-\hat p_t&=\alpha_m\hat y_t-\eta j_t,\\
 \eta j_t&=\alpha_m\hat y_t-(\hat m_t-\hat p_t),\\
 j_t&=\frac{\alpha_m}{\eta}\hat y_t
                 -\frac1\eta(\hat m_t-\hat p_t),\qquad
 \left.\frac{\partial j_t}{\partial\hat y_t}\right|_{\hat m_t,\hat p_t}
       =\frac{\alpha_m}{\eta}>0.\label{eq:nk-static-lm}
\end{align}
More purchases increase desired liquidity. With unchanged real money, a higher
interest rate discourages enough liquidity holding to clear its market.
The slope now follows from money utility and the opportunity cost of holding
cash; it is not an identity from $MV=PY$.

If policy instead fixes a nominal rate in the current comparison, the money
stock adjusts to demand and this independent upward-sloping LM restriction is
replaced by the policy-rate relation. If policy follows a reaction rule,
its slope depends on the variables included in that rule. Describing both
experiments as the same ``money shift'' conceals the policy assumption.

\subsubsection{The same diagram, different behavioral foundations}

Figure~\ref{fig:islm-microfoundations} uses the familiar IS--LM construction
for the same conditional experiment: increase current real money while holding
the current price and the spending outlook fixed. To make the left panel
concrete, take a simple intermediate specification. With expected inflation
fixed and its constant absorbed into the investment intercept, let
\begin{align}
 C&=C_0+c(Y-T),\qquad I=I_0-b i,\qquad M/P=kY-hi,\\
 &\hspace{1em}0<c<1,\qquad b,k,h>0.\notag
\end{align}
The goods-market condition and its inverse IS curve are
\begin{align}
 Y&=C_0+cY-cT+I_0-bi+G,\\
 (1-c)Y&=C_0-cT+I_0+G-bi,\\
 i&=\frac{C_0-cT+I_0+G}{b}-\frac{1-c}{b}Y.
\end{align}
Money clearing gives the corresponding LM curve:
\begin{align}
 hi&=kY-M/P,\qquad i=\frac{k}{h}Y-\frac1h M/P.
\end{align}
The right panel instead uses the already derived
\eqref{eq:nk-static-is} and \eqref{eq:nk-static-lm}. The inverse IS slope
is $-(1-c)/b$ on the left and $-\sigma$ on the right; the LM slope is
$k/h$ on the left and $\alpha_m/\eta$ on the right. The curves can have the
same shape even though different choices and parameters generate their slopes.

The price-setting condition has not yet been imposed on both intersections.
In the complete NK equilibrium, the current price and expectations must also
be consistent with the Phillips curve. Allowing the price level to vary is
precisely the next step from IS--LM to AD--AS below.

\subsubsection{Aggregate demand under a money-supply rule}

Hold $d_t,e_t,\hat m_t$ fixed. Substitute \eqref{eq:nk-static-lm} into
\eqref{eq:nk-static-is}, preserving the real-rate subtraction:
\begin{align}
 \hat y_t
 &=d_t-\frac1\sigma\left[
    \frac{\alpha_m}{\eta}\hat y_t
           -\frac{\hat m_t-\hat p_t}{\eta}-e_t\right],\\
 \sigma\eta\hat y_t
 &=\sigma\eta d_t-\alpha_m\hat y_t+\hat m_t-\hat p_t+\eta e_t,\\
 (\sigma\eta+\alpha_m)\hat y_t
 &=\sigma\eta d_t+\hat m_t-\hat p_t+\eta e_t,\\
 \hat p_t&=\hat m_t+\sigma\eta d_t+\eta e_t
                         -(\sigma\eta+\alpha_m)\hat y_t.
 \label{eq:nk-static-ad}
\end{align}
This AD curve slopes down in output--price-level space. A higher current price
level reduces real money, raises the interest rate at given money, and postpones
purchases at the fixed future outlook. That familiar mechanism survives, with
explicit assumptions supplying the fixed outlook and the money-demand slope.

\subsubsection{Price setting and aggregate supply}

Keep inherited $\hat p_{t-1}$, expected next inflation $e_t$, and natural
output $\hat y_t^n$ fixed. Since $\pi_t=\hat p_t-\hat p_{t-1}$,
\begin{align}
 \hat p_t-\hat p_{t-1}&=\beta e_t+\kappa(\hat y_t-\hat y_t^n),\\
 \hat p_t&=\hat p_{t-1}+\beta e_t
                                 +\kappa(\hat y_t-\hat y_t^n).
 \label{eq:nk-static-as}
\end{align}
This AS curve slopes up in the same output--price-level space. Higher demand
relative to the flexible-price allocation raises marginal cost; resetters
raise prices. The slope is a pricing equilibrium relation, not a physical
limit on how much output the economy can ever produce.

Equate \eqref{eq:nk-static-ad} and \eqref{eq:nk-static-as}, then collect terms:
\begin{align}
 \hat m_t+\sigma\eta d_t+\eta e_t-(\sigma\eta+\alpha_m)\hat y_t
 &=\hat p_{t-1}+\beta e_t+\kappa\hat y_t-\kappa\hat y_t^n,\\
 (\sigma\eta+\alpha_m+\kappa)\hat y_t
 &=\hat m_t+\sigma\eta d_t+(\eta-\beta)e_t
                            -\hat p_{t-1}+\kappa\hat y_t^n,\\
 \hat y_t&=\frac{\hat m_t+\sigma\eta d_t+(\eta-\beta)e_t
                         -\hat p_{t-1}+\kappa\hat y_t^n}
                        {\sigma\eta+\alpha_m+\kappa}.
\end{align}
For this conditional comparison, more money raises output and the price
level. In a full policy experiment, however, future output, expected inflation,
and often the money path change together. The intercepts must be recomputed
from the dynamic equilibrium rather than held fixed by habit.

\subsubsection{Aggregate demand under an interest-rate rule}

For the actual rule used in our shock experiment, substitute
\eqref{eq:nk-rule} into gap IS and rearrange:
\begin{align}
 \sigma x_t&=\sigma E_tx_{t+1}
       -\phi_\pi\pi_t-\phi_xx_t-\nu_t+E_t\pi_{t+1}+r_t^n,\\
 (\sigma+\phi_x)x_t
 &=\sigma E_tx_{t+1}-\phi_\pi\pi_t-\nu_t+E_t\pi_{t+1}+r_t^n.
\end{align}
At a fixed outlook and $\phi_\pi>0$, this is downward-sloping in
$(x_t,\pi_t)$ space; the Phillips curve
$\pi_t=\beta E_t\pi_{t+1}+\kappa x_t$ slopes up. This is an
IS--policy-rule representation of demand, often called IS--MP.
Its vertical axis is inflation, whereas \eqref{eq:nk-static-ad} uses the
price level. Changing between them requires the inherited price level;
renaming the axis alone does not perform that transformation.

The distinction is also visible in the familiar interest-rate--output plane.
Rewrite gap IS with the nominal rate on the vertical axis:
\begin{align}
 j_t&=E_t\pi_{t+1}+r_t^n+\sigma E_tx_{t+1}-\sigma x_t.
 \label{eq:nk-diagram-is}
\end{align}
To draw a policy relation in that same plane, first substitute the Phillips
curve into the Taylor rule:
\begin{align}
 j_t&=\phi_\pi[\beta E_t\pi_{t+1}+\kappa x_t]+\phi_x x_t+\nu_t,\\
 j_t&=\beta\phi_\pi E_t\pi_{t+1}
                   +(\phi_x+\phi_\pi\kappa)x_t+\nu_t.
 \label{eq:nk-diagram-mp}
\end{align}
At a given inflation outlook, the MP slope is
$\phi_x+\phi_\pi\kappa$: policy responds directly to activity and indirectly
to the inflation associated with that activity. A fixed current rate would
instead give a horizontal policy line. Neither is an independent money-demand
LM restriction when the authority accommodates the rate it sets.

discipline here is that its intercept contains an explicit conditional forecast
whose value must eventually agree with the equilibrium path.

intercept shifts MP up, raises the real rate, and lowers the output gap. The
policy rule and price setting determine the current nominal rate jointly;
money, if present, is supplied to meet the resulting demand. This is why an
interest-rule experiment should be drawn with MP rather than an independently
shifted LM. In a full dynamic experiment the future outlook also responds,
which is already incorporated in the solved impulse responses earlier.

\begin{redblock}
\textbf{A useful diagram test.}
Before shifting a curve, state its axes, what is held fixed, the policy
instrument, and the source of the disturbance. Then ask which expectations
also change in equilibrium. A current-period intersection helps organize a
mechanism; the dynamic equations and terminal conditions determine whether
that intersection belongs to an admissible rational-expectations path.
\end{redblock}

sales, hours, marginal cost, inflation, and money. The next chapter changes
which nominal contract is slow to adjust: the wage. That distinction matters
for labor allocation and for the trade-offs policy can face.
