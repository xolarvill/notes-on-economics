\section{Endogenous Growth and Resource Allocation}\label{sec:endogenous-growth}

Intermediate macroeconomics introduces AK, education, and innovation as ways
to explain sustained growth. This chapter asks what closes those explanations:
who accumulates the relevant stock, what it costs, which return guides the
choice, and whether that return is the one society receives. We reuse Ramsey's
optimization tools rather than treating endogenous growth as an unrelated list
of production functions.

\subsection{From intermediate macroeconomics and Ramsey to a growth engine}

\subsubsection{What the familiar diagrams leave to be explained}

A machine, a trained worker, and a usable design can all raise output, but they
are not accumulated in the same way. Building equipment uses goods. Training
uses time and other resources. Developing a design can be costly even when
another firm can subsequently use the same instructions without taking them
away from the first. These differences affect both the resource constraint
and the incentive to invest.

It is tempting to put each item into a production function and declare growth
explained. Instead, ask which scarcity remains after accumulation. If machines
are added while skills or usable methods remain fixed, returns may fall. If
skills and equipment can grow together, the relevant return might not fall.
If ideas can be shared, the social gain from discovery may exceed what the
inventor can charge. We need to identify that mechanism before choosing the
law of motion or discussing policy.

The familiar intermediate-level statements are therefore starting points.
``Saving can affect growth'' needs a technology that sustains returns and a
rule determining saving. ``Education raises productivity'' needs a skill
accumulation technology and a time allocation. ``Research creates ideas''
needs a resource cost and a reason someone pays it. The models below fill
these particular gaps; they do not repeat the Solow derivation.

\subsubsection{Ramsey endogenizes saving, not automatically long-run growth}

With fixed technology and population, the Ramsey benchmark has
\begin{equation}
\dot k=f(k)-c-\delta k,\qquad
\frac{\dot c}{c}=\frac{f'(k)-\delta-\rho}{\theta}.
\end{equation}
Saving is chosen optimally. Yet with diminishing marginal product, accumulating
$k$ lowers its return. At the stationary optimum,
$f'(k^*)=\delta+\rho$ and consumption stops growing. In the earlier version
with exogenous labor-efficiency growth, consumption per person can grow, but
that long-run technology growth rate is still supplied from outside the model.

The next question is thus about the return to \emph{all reproducible inputs},
not whether households have an Euler equation. Keep the household problem,
change the accumulation opportunities, and check what replaces the stationary
capital target. OLG changed who lives, saves, and receives transfers. Here we
return explicitly to a continuing Ramsey household to isolate a different
change: the mechanism that makes productive capacity grow.

\Needspace{10\baselineskip}
\begin{blueblock}
\textbf{Endogenous saving and endogenous growth are different claims.}
An optimal saving decision can lead to a stationary economy. Conversely, an
AK equation with an imposed saving share can generate a growth rate without
explaining saving. To obtain both, combine a specified accumulation technology
with household optimization, market or planner closure, and the appropriate
terminal conditions. Changing notation from an exogenous $g$ to a function
called $g$ does not provide those mechanisms.
\end{blueblock}

The following models are separate experiments. Technology coefficients such
as $\eta$ and $\alpha$ are defined within each specification; a training
efficiency, a production elasticity, and a research efficiency are not assumed
to be the same empirical parameter.

\subsection{AK: retain Ramsey's choice, change the return}

\Needspace{12\baselineskip}
\subsubsection{Complete conditions for the one-stock benchmark}

\begin{modelsetup}[1]
\begin{modelcondition}{01}{Time, population, goods, and states}
Time is continuous. A closed economy has one good, no government in the
baseline, no uncertainty, and a fixed population normalized to one. A
continuing household owns the given stock $k(0)=k_0>0$ and firms.
Consequently aggregate and per-person quantities coincide in this benchmark.
There is no age structure, leisure choice, or exogenous technology growth.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{02}{Technology and accumulation}
Production is linear in a reproducible stock:
\[
y=Bk,\qquad B>0\text{ constant},\qquad
\dot k=(B-\delta)k-c,\quad \delta\geq0.
\]
The stock can be interpreted as a broad productive composite only if its
components can be reproduced with the assumed technology. Goods can be
converted into the stock at unit cost. Strictly diminishing returns and the
Solow Inada limits are deliberately replaced; this is not Cobb--Douglas
with the same fixed factor left implicit.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{03}{Preferences and household choice}
The household chooses a positive consumption path and feasible $k(t)\geq0$:
\[
\max\int_0^\infty e^{-\rho t}u(c(t))\,dt,\qquad
u'(c)=c^{-\theta},\quad\rho>0,\quad\theta>0.
\]
Use $u(c)=c^{1-\theta}/(1-\theta)$ for $\theta\ne1$ and $u(c)=\log c$ for
$\theta=1$. Saving is the residual of output after consumption, not an
independently imposed share.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{04}{Markets, ownership, and returns}
In the competitive interpretation, firms rent the productive stock at
$q=B$ and earn zero economic profit after paying it. There is no separate
productive labor input in this one-stock technology. The household owns the
stock, receives the net return $r=B-\delta$, and can adjust investment without
costs. Without externalities or other wedges, its budget coincides with the
aggregate resource constraint and implements the planner allocation.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{05}{Admissibility and the growing solution}
The planner's transversality condition is
\[
\lim_{t\to\infty}e^{-\rho t}c(t)^{-\theta}k(t)=0.
\]
For the interior positive-growth case studied below, impose
\[
g\equiv\frac{B-\delta-\rho}{\theta}>0,\qquad
\xi\equiv\rho-(1-\theta)g>0.
\]
The second restriction ensures finite allocation-dependent utility and the
terminal condition along the proposed balanced growth path. For $\theta<1$
it restricts how productive accumulation can be relative to discounting;
a formal Euler solution alone need not be a well-posed optimum.
\end{modelcondition}
\end{modelsetup}

\subsubsection{The same Hamiltonian now gives a constant growth rate}

The current-value Hamiltonian and its control and costate conditions are
\begin{align}
\mathcal H&=u(c)+\mu[(B-\delta)k-c],\\
\mu&=c^{-\theta},\qquad
\dot\mu=[\rho-(B-\delta)]\mu.
\end{align}
Since $\dot\mu/\mu=-\theta\dot c/c$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eg-ak-growth}
\frac{\dot c}{c}=\frac{B-\delta-\rho}{\theta}=g.
\end{equation}
The marginal product no longer falls as the stock rises. Waiting remains
rewarded at the same rate, so the optimal consumption growth rate need not
fade toward zero. This is the change in technology doing the work; the
intertemporal preference condition is still Ramsey's.

The Euler equation gives $c(t)=c_0e^{gt}$ but not $c_0$. Set
$a\equiv B-\delta$ and integrate $\dot k=ak-c_0e^{gt}$, using
$\xi=a-g=\rho-(1-\theta)g>0$:
\begin{align}
k(t)&=e^{at}\left[k_0-c_0\int_0^t e^{-(a-g)v}\,dv\right],\\
&=\left(k_0-\frac{c_0}{\xi}\right)e^{at}
  +\frac{c_0}{\xi}e^{gt}.
\end{align}
If $c_0>\xi k_0$, the first term eventually makes capital negative. If
$c_0<\xi k_0$, capital grows at the faster rate $a$ and its limiting discounted
utility value is positive: the household leaves valuable resources unused.
Feasibility and transversality therefore select
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eg-ak-path}
c_0=\xi k_0,\qquad k(t)=k_0e^{gt},\qquad c(t)=\xi k(t).
\end{equation}
Indeed, along this path the terminal expression is proportional to
$e^{-[\rho-(1-\theta)g]t}=e^{-\xi t}$ and vanishes.

\Needspace{9\baselineskip}
\begin{greenblock}
\textbf{A balanced growth path replaces the stationary capital target.}
There is no finite positive stationary $k$ on this growing solution.
Consumption jumps to the feasible optimal ratio $c/k=\xi$ for the inherited
stock, then both grow at $g$. The saving share is an outcome,
$s^*=(\delta+g)/B=1-\xi/B$, rather than Solow's input. Under the stated
positive-growth conditions it lies between zero and one.
\end{greenblock}

\subsubsection{A level change, a return change, and a policy wedge}

A one-time increase in initial capital, holding $B$ and preferences fixed,
raises the levels of capital and consumption but leaves $g$ unchanged.
A permanent change in $B$ changes the return and hence $g$; comparing the
new paths requires the new initial consumption ratio as well as the slope.
The absence of a transition in the ratio is a special feature of linear
technology and these preferences, not a general result for growth models.

For example, $B=0.10$, $\delta=0.02$, $\rho=0.04$, and $\theta=2$ imply
$g=0.02$, $c/k=0.06$, and $s^*=0.40$. Raising $k_0$ by 10\% raises the whole
path by 10\%. Raising $B$ to $0.12$ instead gives $g=0.03$ and $c/k=0.07$.
These are illustrative model numbers, not empirical return estimates.

A tax rate $\tau$ on net capital income, rebated as a lump-sum transfer,
leaves aggregate resources unchanged but changes the household's marginal
return. Each price-taking household treats its rebate as given, as in
Section~\ref{sec:ramsey-taxation}. For an interior admissible growing equilibrium,
\begin{equation}
g_\tau=\frac{(1-\tau)(B-\delta)-\rho}{\theta},\qquad
\frac{c}{k}=B-\delta-g_\tau.
\end{equation}
The finite-welfare restriction $\rho-(1-\theta)g_\tau>0$ must still hold.
This is a permanent growth effect under the AK assumption. The corresponding
Ramsey tax experiment with diminishing returns changed stationary levels;
that difference comes from the accumulation technology. An empirical claim
that a tax changes trend growth would require evidence for the relevant
mechanism, not this formula alone.

\subsubsection{Private diminishing returns can coexist with aggregate AK}

The one-stock competitive benchmark need not contain a market failure. An
alternative construction does: suppose firm $i$ has
\begin{equation}
y_i=B k_i^\alpha(\bar k\ell_i)^{1-\alpha},\qquad 0<\alpha<1,
\end{equation}
where $\bar k=K/L$ is an economy-wide knowledge spillover associated with
capital, taken as given by each small firm. Labor satisfies $\sum_i\ell_i=1$.
Firms have constant returns in their private inputs $k_i,\ell_i$, and take
factor prices and $\bar k$ as given. In a symmetric allocation with the same
capital per worker across firms, aggregate output is $Y=BK$.

Differentiate the firm's own technology before imposing symmetry. Then
\begin{equation}
q_D=\alpha B,\qquad w=(1-\alpha)BK,\qquad
r_D=\alpha B-\delta.
\end{equation}
Factor payments still exhaust output. A planner who internalizes how $K$
raises the common spillover instead obtains marginal product $B$. With the
same household preferences and appropriate admissibility restrictions,
\begin{equation}
g_D=\frac{\alpha B-\delta-\rho}{\theta},\qquad
 g_P=\frac{B-\delta-\rho}{\theta}.
\end{equation}
\Needspace{12\baselineskip}
\begin{blueblock}
\textbf{An aggregate AK equation does not identify the private return.}
Where both growing solutions are feasible, $g_D<g_P$. These are two different
interpretations of the same aggregate $Y=BK$: either $B$ is privately earned,
or part of the social return is external. Aggregate linearity alone cannot
tell us which policy problem is present. Learning during investment or shared
production experience can motivate a spillover, but its size has to be
specified and measured rather than inferred from the AK notation.
\end{blueblock}

\subsection{Human capital: explain the reproducible composite}

\subsubsection{Why adding a second stock is not enough}

The augmented Solow model added physical and human capital with diminishing
returns to their joint accumulation. Its balanced growth still depended on
exogenous technology. To change that result, give skill formation its own
technology: time spent learning today affects the ability to produce and learn
tomorrow. The question is now how much time to divert from current output.
This motivates a two-sector Uzawa--Lucas-style benchmark. We omit average-skill
externalities to isolate the private accumulation mechanism.\footnote{Robert
E. Lucas Jr. (1988), ``On the Mechanics of Economic Development,''
\textit{Journal of Monetary Economics}, 22(1), 3--42, Section 4.
The simplified model here is not the full externality specification in that
paper. \url{https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-3932(88)90168-7}.}

\Needspace{12\baselineskip}
\begin{modelsetup}[1]
\begin{modelcondition}{01}{Household, stocks, and the scope of the experiment}
Keep the continuous-time, fixed-population household and CRRA preferences
above, with $K_0,H_0>0$. $H$ is a stock of skill in the continuing household,
not hours worked or the number of school years. The household chooses
consumption $C>0$ and a work fraction $v\in[0,1]$. The remaining fraction
$1-v$ is used for learning; leisure is omitted. There is no uncertainty,
knowledge spillover to other households, or public subsidy in this benchmark.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{02}{Production and the goods constraint}
Goods production is
\[
Y=K^\alpha(vH)^{1-\alpha},\qquad 0<\alpha<1,\qquad
\dot K=Y-C-\delta K.
\]
Goods can be consumed or accumulated as physical capital. Current production
uses only the skill-weighted time assigned to work. The technology is constant
returns in $K,H$ at fixed $v$, despite a diminishing marginal product of $K$
when $H$ is fixed. The household/planner controls the two uses of its resources.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{03}{Learning technology and its cost}
Skills accumulate according to
\[
\dot H=[\eta(1-v)-\delta_H]H,\qquad \eta>0,\quad\delta_H\geq0.
\]
Learning uses time, so its resource cost is forgone production, even though
there is no separate goods expenditure in this stripped-down specification.
A given learning fraction adds skill in proportion to the existing stock.
Skill depreciation is explicit; finite careers, cohort replacement, and
education quality differences are not separately modeled. Indefinite aggregate
skill accumulation is an assumption about this continuing household, not an
assertion that one person can study forever.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{04}{Optimality, boundaries, and balanced growth}
This is a planner/household production problem with no externality. The
Hamiltonian conditions below apply to an interior allocation $0<v<1$,
$K,H,C>0$, with terminal conditions
\[
\lim_{t\to\infty}e^{-\rho t}\lambda(t)K(t)=0,\qquad
\lim_{t\to\infty}e^{-\rho t}\mu(t)H(t)=0.
\]
For a positive-growth balanced path require
$g=(\eta-\delta_H-\rho)/\theta>0$ and
$\rho-(1-\theta)g>0$. They imply the interior work fraction derived below;
if the implied fraction lies outside $[0,1]$, the interior solution cannot
be used. Capital and skill are both inherited; an arbitrary initial ratio
need not already be on the balanced growth path.
\end{modelcondition}
\end{modelsetup}

\subsubsection{Time allocation and the two shadow values}

Write the current-value Hamiltonian as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal H=u(C)+\lambda[Y-C-\delta K]
 +\mu[\eta(1-v)-\delta_H]H.
\end{equation}
The consumption condition is $\lambda=C^{-\theta}$. The work-time condition
and the physical-capital costate equation are
\begin{align}
\lambda(1-\alpha)Y/v&=\mu\eta H,\label{eq:eg-human-time}\\
\dot\lambda/\lambda&=\rho+\delta-\alpha Y/K.
\end{align}
An extra unit of work time produces goods now; an extra unit of learning time
produces skills valued at $\mu$. The first equation compares those two uses
of time in the same utility units.

For the skill stock,
\begin{equation}
\dot\mu=\rho\mu-\lambda(1-\alpha)Y/H
 -\mu[\eta(1-v)-\delta_H].
\end{equation}
Use \eqref{eq:eg-human-time} to replace
$\lambda(1-\alpha)Y/H$ by $\mu\eta v$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eg-human-costate}
\frac{\dot\mu}{\mu}=\rho-\eta+\delta_H.
\end{equation}
The return to skill includes its contribution to further skill formation.
Looking only at its current wage contribution would miss that part of the
accumulation opportunity.

\subsubsection{Balanced growth and the work--learning split}

On a balanced path, $K,H,C$ grow at the same rate $g$ and $v$ is constant.
Equation~\eqref{eq:eg-human-time} then makes $\mu/\lambda$ constant, because
$Y/H$ is constant. Therefore $\dot\mu/\mu=\dot\lambda/\lambda=-\theta g$.
Using \eqref{eq:eg-human-costate} and the skill law gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eg-human-growth}
g=\frac{\eta-\delta_H-\rho}{\theta},\qquad
v^*=1-\frac{g+\delta_H}{\eta}
=\frac{\rho-(1-\theta)g}{\eta}.
\end{equation}
The stated restrictions ensure $0<v^*<1$. The physical Euler condition and
resource constraint also require
\begin{equation}
\alpha Y/K=\rho+\delta+\theta g,\qquad
C/K=Y/K-\delta-g>0.
\end{equation}
Since $Y/K=(vH/K)^{1-\alpha}$, the balanced stock ratio is
\begin{equation}
(K/H)^*=v^*\left(\frac{\alpha}{\rho+\delta+\theta g}\right)^{1/(1-\alpha)}.
\end{equation}
These equations characterize a balanced path, not the complete transition
from arbitrary $K_0/H_0$. Consumption and time allocation can adjust on impact;
the two stocks cannot be chosen afresh to match this ratio.

\Needspace{10\baselineskip}
\begin{redblock}
\textbf{Where did diminishing returns go?}
Adding machines while holding skill fixed still lowers their marginal product.
But scaling $K$ and $H$ together at a fixed work fraction scales output by
the same factor. The learning technology also reproduces $H$ linearly.
Persistent growth uses those joint reproduction opportunities, rather than
assuming that machines alone have escaped diminishing returns. If learning
became progressively harder as $H$ rose, this conclusion would need to be
rederived.
\end{redblock}

More schooling time at a given $H$ raises immediate skill accumulation while
reducing current goods production. A one-time higher $H_0$ is different from
a permanent improvement in training productivity $\eta$. School attendance,
actual learning, skill depreciation, and the use of skills at work are not
interchangeable measures. The model specifies one way those margins interact;
it does not imply that every extra school year permanently raises a country's
growth rate. Shared ideas introduce another issue: unlike a person's time,
the same design can be used in many places at once.

\subsection{Ideas: from a research share to innovation incentives}

\subsubsection{Nonrival use and costly invention are different margins}

A trained engineer cannot supply the same hour to two laboratories at once.
A design can guide production in both laboratories without being depleted by
its first use. That is nonrivalry. Whether others are allowed to use it without
paying is a separate question of excludability. The distinction motivates
Romer's innovation framework.\footnote{Paul M. Romer (1990), ``Endogenous
Technological Change,'' \textit{Journal of Political Economy}, 98(5), Part 2,
S71--S102. \url{https://doi.org/10.1086/261725}.}

If every additional use can be supplied at negligible cost, competitive pricing
of use alone may not recover the original discovery cost. Patent rents,
research contracts, public funding, or other institutions could supply the
reward. We specify one arrangement instead of assuming that a positive research
share appears without someone bearing its cost.

The worked model adds varieties of a differentiated input. It uses flow inputs
and labor-based research to keep the accounting transparent; it is a
Romer-style teaching model, not a literal reproduction of all sectors in the
1990 paper.\footnote{For expanding-variety constructions and alternative
research technologies, see Daron Acemoglu, MIT, \textit{Advanced Economic Growth},
Lecture 2 (September 10, 2007),
\href{https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/inline-files/Advanced\%20Economic\%20Growth\%20-\%20Lecture\%202\%2C\%20Review\%20of\%20Endogenous\%20Growth\%2C\%20Explaining\%20Variety\%20Models.pdf}{lecture notes}.}

\Needspace{12\baselineskip}
\begin{modelsetup}[1]
\begin{modelcondition}{01}{Household, population, states, and ownership}
Time is continuous and the population $L>0$ is constant. A continuing household
supplies labor inelastically, owns firms and patents, and chooses consumption
and financial saving under the earlier CRRA objective. Total consumption is
$C$; since $L$ is constant, using $C$ rather than $C/L$ does not change optimal
growth. The inherited state is the number of designs $A(0)>0$. There is no
aggregate uncertainty, physical storage stock, population growth, or leisure
choice in this worked model.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{02}{Final production and intermediate resource use}
Competitive final-good firms use production labor $L_Y$ and a continuum of
input flows indexed by their designs:
\[
Y=L_Y^{1-\alpha}\int_0^A x_j^\alpha\,dj,\qquad 0<\alpha<1.
\]
Each unit of $x_j$ costs one unit of the final good to produce and is fully
used within the period. It is not a durable capital stock. Final goods left
for consumption satisfy $C=Y-\int_0^A x_j\,dj$. There are no government
purchases or other goods uses. Gross output and net consumption must not
be confused when calculating the cost of innovation.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{03}{Exclusive production rights and pricing}
Each design has a perpetual patent with one producer of its input, no expiry,
and no creative destruction. The owner chooses the input price taking final
firms' demand as given; marginal production cost is one. Designs are symmetric
but their producers have monopoly power over their own inputs. Final-good
firms and factor markets are competitive. Patents protect production rights;
existing knowledge is available to researchers without a separate royalty.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{04}{Research, free entry, and labor clearing}
Research employs $L_R$ workers and produces designs at
\[
\dot A=\eta A L_R,\qquad \eta>0,\qquad L_Y+L_R=L.
\]
Each research firm takes the common stock $A$, wage $w$, and value $V$ per
new patent as given. Positive research with free entry requires
$\eta A V=w$: the value of new designs per worker equals its wage cost.
Knowledge does not depreciate. The linear dependence on $A$ and the absence
of congestion are substantive accumulation assumptions, not definitions.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{05}{Asset pricing, budgets, and the interior path}
A patent pays flow profit $\pi$. With no risk or displacement,
$rV=\pi+\dot V$, where $r$ is the instantaneous net financial return.
Household financial wealth is $W=AV$ and obeys
\[
\dot W=rW+wL-C,\qquad
\frac{\dot C}{C}=\frac{r-\rho}{\theta}.
\]
Impose solvency and
$\lim_{t\to\infty}e^{-\rho t}C(t)^{-\theta}W(t)=0$.
The interior balanced path below requires $\alpha\eta L>\rho$ and
$\rho-(1-\theta)g_D>0$. Planner comparisons also require the stated planner
interiority and finite-welfare restrictions. If profitable research does not
cover its cost, a zero-research corner replaces the interior formula.
\end{modelcondition}
\end{modelsetup}

\subsubsection{Demand, the markup, wages, and patent profits}

Final firms maximize $Y-wL_Y-\int_0^A p_jx_j\,dj$.
Demand for input $j$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
p_j=\alpha L_Y^{1-\alpha}x_j^{\alpha-1},\qquad
x_j=L_Y(\alpha/p_j)^{1/(1-\alpha)}.
\end{equation}
The input owner maximizes $(p_j-1)x_j(p_j)$. Since demand has price elasticity
$1/(1-\alpha)>1$, its marginal-revenue condition gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eg-idea-markup}
p_D=1/\alpha,\qquad x_D=\zeta_D L_Y,\qquad
\zeta_D\equiv\alpha^{2/(1-\alpha)}.
\end{equation}
Equivalently, differentiating profit in $x$ gives
$\alpha^2L_Y^{1-\alpha}x^{\alpha-1}=1$. Labor's marginal product and profit
per patent are therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eg-idea-wage-profit}
w=(1-\alpha)A\zeta_D^\alpha,\qquad
\pi=\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\zeta_D L_Y.
\end{equation}
Every design can be used alongside the same production labor. Consequently
$Y=A L_Y\zeta_D^\alpha$ and total intermediate cost is $A L_Y\zeta_D$.
Net consumption is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eg-idea-net-output}
C=A L_Y(\zeta_D^\alpha-\zeta_D)>0.
\end{equation}
The production firm's wage payment and the input owners' profits sum to this
net product. A larger set of designs expands productive possibilities; the
markup limits how intensively each is used.

\subsubsection{Free entry and Ramsey pricing determine research effort}

On a balanced path with constant $L_Y,L_R$, wages grow with $A$, while profit
\emph{per patent} is constant. Free entry then makes patent value constant:
\begin{equation}
V=\frac{w}{\eta A}
=\frac{(1-\alpha)\zeta_D^\alpha}{\eta}.
\end{equation}
Asset pricing reduces to $r=\pi/V$. Since
$\zeta_D^{1-\alpha}=\alpha^2$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eg-idea-return}
r=\eta L_Y\frac{\zeta_D^{1-\alpha}}{\alpha}
=\alpha\eta L_Y.
\end{equation}
Consumption grows at the rate of designs on this path. Combine the household
Euler equation, research technology, and labor clearing:
\begin{align}
g_D=\eta L_R&=\frac{\alpha\eta(L-L_R)-\rho}{\theta},\\
\eta(\theta+\alpha)L_R&=\alpha\eta L-\rho,\\
L_R^D&=\frac{\alpha\eta L-\rho}{\eta(\theta+\alpha)},\qquad
 g_D=\frac{\alpha\eta L-\rho}{\theta+\alpha}.
\label{eq:eg-idea-growth}
\end{align}
The research share has now been derived from incentives, not inserted into
$\dot A/A$ as a given number. Under the stated restriction it lies strictly
between zero and $L$. If $\alpha\eta L\leq\rho$, the interior expression
would be nonpositive; the no-research corner has $L_R=0$, rather than negative
research employment.

\Needspace{13\baselineskip}
\begin{blueblock}
\textbf{Research uses labor even when no research goods appear in the budget.}
New patents cost $V\dot A=wL_R$. That expenditure pays research workers,
whose time is no longer available to final production. Household saving buys
new equity: with constant $V$, $\dot W=V\dot A$. Indeed,
$rW+wL-C=A\pi+w(L_Y+L_R)-(A\pi+wL_Y)=wL_R$.
The financial transaction and the labor opportunity cost describe the same
allocation. Treating all $L$ as production labor while also employing $L_R$
in research would manufacture resources that the economy does not have.
\end{blueblock}

This mechanism connects to laboratories, software development, and design
work: people must be paid before there is a product to sell, and the expected
claim on future profits helps finance that cost. Patent duration, imitation,
entry barriers, or the ability to sell a design would change that claim.
Those margins are deliberately fixed here; the formula is not a measured
return to every kind of research.

\subsubsection{The planner's research problem is another AK problem}

A planner does not need the monopoly markup to finance discovery. At given
$A,L_Y$, it first maximizes net output,
$L_Y^{1-\alpha}\int x_j^\alpha dj-\int x_jdj$.
The optimal input use satisfies
\begin{equation}
\alpha L_Y^{1-\alpha}x_P^{\alpha-1}=1,\qquad
x_P=\zeta_P L_Y,\quad \zeta_P\equiv\alpha^{1/(1-\alpha)}>\zeta_D.
\end{equation}
Let $D_P\equiv\zeta_P^\alpha-\zeta_P>0$. Its remaining resource constraint is
$C=D_PA(L-L_R)$. Solve it for $L_R$ and substitute into research:
\begin{equation}
\dot A=\eta LA-\frac{\eta}{D_P}C.
\end{equation}
Define a transformed state $Z\equiv D_PA/\eta$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eg-idea-planner-map}
\dot Z=\eta LZ-C.
\end{equation}
This is exactly the one-stock AK resource equation, with net return $\eta L$.
$Z$ is a planner's transformed state, not the market patent wealth $AV$.
Reusing the earlier optimization, rather than maximizing growth as a separate
objective, gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eg-idea-planner-growth}
g_P=\frac{\eta L-\rho}{\theta},\qquad L_R^P=g_P/\eta,
\end{equation}
provided $0<g_P<\eta L$ and $\rho-(1-\theta)g_P>0$.
With $\alpha\eta L>\rho$ and both interior admissible paths,
$g_P>g_D$. Private innovation rewards omit part of the value of future knowledge,
and monopoly pricing also restricts input use. The growth comparison belongs
to this specified welfare problem; it does not establish that any intervention
raising measured research must improve welfare.

A subsidy to invention and a policy changing input markups address different
margins. Their financing, targeting, and effect on entry would have to be
modeled before proposing an optimal instrument. In settings with duplication,
displacement of existing innovators, or other external effects, the welfare
comparison would need to be redone. Nonrivality alone does not identify one
universally correct patent or subsidy policy.

\subsection{What changes when ideas become harder to find?}

\subsubsection{The scale prediction is an assumption worth testing}

The worked model has $\dot A/A=\eta L_R$: a permanently larger research
workforce supports a permanently faster growth rate. In particular,
\eqref{eq:eg-idea-growth} makes the scale $L$ matter. This follows from linear
research productivity in existing knowledge and no congestion. It is not
implied by the definition of a nonrival idea.

Jones (1995) questioned the scale predictions of early R\&D models using the
historical evidence available in that paper.\footnote{Charles I. Jones (1995),
``R\&D-Based Models of Economic Growth,'' \textit{Journal of Political Economy},
103(4), 759--784. The comparison concerns that paper's models and evidence,
not a claim that current data settle every innovation mechanism.
\url{https://doi.org/10.1086/262002}.}
One way to investigate the restriction is to change the research technology:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eg-semi-technology}
\dot A=\eta A^\phi L_R^\lambda,\qquad
\phi<1,\quad 0<\lambda\leq1.
\end{equation}
These are new assumptions. The preceding optimal research-share and patent
formulas must not be carried over unchanged.

\subsubsection{A conditional balanced-growth calculation}

For this calculation, allow labor to grow at exogenous rate $n\geq0$ and
hold the research share $s_R=L_R/L$ constant and positive. This is a
technology experiment; it does not yet solve the new household and entry
problem. Since
\begin{equation}
g_A\equiv\dot A/A=\eta A^{\phi-1}(s_RL)^\lambda,
\end{equation}
a positive constant $g_A$ on a balanced path requires
\begin{equation}
0=\frac{d\log g_A}{dt}=(\phi-1)g_A+\lambda n,
\qquad g_A=\frac{\lambda n}{1-\phi}.
\end{equation}
For $n>0$, the corresponding balanced-path relation is
\begin{equation}
A^{1-\phi}=\frac{\eta s_R^\lambda}{g_A}L^\lambda.
\end{equation}
A permanent increase in the research share changes this level relation and
the transition, while the balanced growth rate in this specification is
unchanged. Research remains a costly productive activity; a level effect
is not evidence that research is useless.

When $n=0$ and $L_R$ is constant, integrate
\eqref{eq:eg-semi-technology} directly:
\begin{equation}
A(t)^{1-\phi}=A(0)^{1-\phi}
 +(1-\phi)\eta L_R^\lambda t.
\end{equation}
New ideas keep arriving, but their percentage addition to a growing stock
shrinks and $g_A$ tends to zero. This illustrates why a model can endogenize
research decisions or technological change without making every permanent
research policy change alter the asymptotic growth rate.

\subsection{Allocation: more output from the same stocks}

\subsubsection{A distinct question from inventing or accumulating inputs}

Two economies with the same total capital and available production methods
need not use them equally well. Capital might be tied to a less productive
firm while a more productive firm cannot finance expansion. This changes
aggregate productivity without creating another machine or design. Separate
that allocation margin from the growth engines already studied.

The example below is a static resource-allocation problem, not a new balanced
growth model. It isolates a mechanism used in research on misallocation.
Hsieh and Klenow (2009) apply a richer framework to manufacturing
establishments; the simple numbers below are not their empirical estimates.
\footnote{Chang-Tai Hsieh and Peter J. Klenow (2009), ``Misallocation and
Manufacturing TFP in China and India,'' \textit{Quarterly Journal of Economics},
124(4), 1403--1448. \url{https://doi.org/10.1162/qjec.2009.124.4.1403}.}

\Needspace{12\baselineskip}
\begin{modelsetup}[1]
\begin{modelcondition}{01}{Firms, fixed resources, and technology}
At one date there are two firms with fixed labor and other complementary
inputs. They produce the same good at price one:
\[
y_i=z_i k_i^\alpha,\qquad z_i>0,\quad0<\alpha<1,\quad i=1,2.
\]
The total available stock is $k_1+k_2=K>0$, with $k_i\geq0$.
There is no investment, research, uncertainty, or adjustment cost in this
one-date experiment. The $z_i$ and the complementary inputs are held fixed.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{02}{Efficient allocation and the private problem}
A planner maximizes $y_1+y_2$ subject to the fixed stock. In an undistorted
competitive rental market, firms maximize $z_i k_i^\alpha-qk_i$ at the common
rental price $q$. Households own the stock and firms. Labor costs and incomes
can be suppressed because their quantities are fixed here; they cannot be
inferred to be absent from a full growth economy.
\end{modelcondition}
\begin{modelcondition}{03}{A precisely specified allocation wedge}
For the distorted comparison, firm $i$ faces user cost $(1+\tau_i)q$, with
$1+\tau_i>0$. It chooses capital to maximize
$z_i k_i^\alpha-(1+\tau_i)qk_i$. Think of a rental tax rebated lump-sum to
owners, so the payment itself is not a resource loss. Different effective
financing costs could motivate a wedge, but this simple tax experiment does
not derive a credit constraint. Market clearing still requires $k_1+k_2=K$.
\end{modelcondition}
\end{modelsetup}

\subsubsection{Equalize marginal products, not capital quantities}

With multiplier $\nu$ on the capital stock, the planner's interior conditions
are
\begin{equation}
\alpha z_1k_1^{\alpha-1}=\nu
=\alpha z_2k_2^{\alpha-1}.
\end{equation}
Rearrange the ratio, then use resource clearing:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eg-efficient-allocation}
\frac{k_1}{k_2}=\left(\frac{z_1}{z_2}\right)^{1/(1-\alpha)},\qquad
k_i=K\frac{z_i^{1/(1-\alpha)}}{z_1^{1/(1-\alpha)}+z_2^{1/(1-\alpha)}}.
\end{equation}
Substituting into output and collecting terms gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eg-aggregate-allocation}
Y^*=K^\alpha
\left[z_1^{1/(1-\alpha)}+z_2^{1/(1-\alpha)}\right]^{1-\alpha}.
\end{equation}
Capital goes to the firm with the higher marginal product until the marginal
products match. Equal capital would be efficient only if the technologies
and complementary inputs make that allocation satisfy the same condition.

Distorted private choices instead satisfy
\begin{equation}
\frac{\alpha z_i k_i^{\alpha-1}}{1+\tau_i}=q,\qquad
\frac{k_1}{k_2}=
\left[\frac{z_1/(1+\tau_1)}{z_2/(1+\tau_2)}\right]^{1/(1-\alpha)}.
\end{equation}
Both firms optimize, but their physical marginal products differ when their
wedges differ. Removing the wedge changes where capital works; it does not
change the given total stock.

For $\alpha=1/2$, $z_1=2$, $z_2=1$, and $K=1$, the efficient allocation is
$(k_1,k_2)=(0.8,0.2)$ and $Y^*=\sqrt5\simeq2.2361$. If
$\tau_1=1,\tau_2=0$, both adjusted productivities are one, so market clearing
gives $(0.5,0.5)$ and $Y=3/\sqrt2\simeq2.1213$. The output loss is about
5.13\% of the efficient level, despite identical total capital and unchanged
firm technologies. This is a hypothetical accounting comparison, not an
estimate of the loss from a specific financial institution.

\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{images/endogenous_allocation.pdf}
\par\medskip\RaggedRight
\noindent\textit{Allocation changes output without changing total capital.}
The stock is one throughout. Moving capital toward the more productive firm
raises total output until marginal products match at $k_1=0.8$. The peak

\Needspace{10\baselineskip}
\begin{greenblock}
\textbf{A productivity level gain is not automatically a permanent growth gain.}
Reallocating the existing stock can raise output now. Repeating that comparison
with an unchanged efficient allocation does not create ever faster growth.
To affect trend growth, the allocation change must also alter a continuing
mechanism such as research entry, learning, or the return to reproducible
inputs. A measured productivity residual can reflect allocation as well as
available technology; identifying its source requires more evidence.
\end{greenblock}

Observed differences in marginal revenue products are not by themselves a
complete diagnosis of financing distortions. Market power, adjustment costs,
risk, prices, product quality, and measurement can matter. A wedge model
provides a disciplined counterfactual once those choices are specified; it
does not turn every cross-firm gap into a causal estimate of misallocation.

\Needspace{24\baselineskip}
\subsection{How to read an endogenous-growth claim}

The same Ramsey household has appeared with different accumulation
opportunities. The AK problem made the marginal return constant; the skill
model explained one way reproducible inputs can grow together; the idea model
made invention and its rewards explicit. The allocation experiment changed
the use of existing resources rather than supplying another growth engine.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{p{0.22\linewidth}p{0.32\linewidth}p{0.32\linewidth}}
\hline
\tableheading{Mechanism} & \tableheading{What is determined} & \tableheading{What remains assumed}\\
\hline
Ramsey benchmark & Optimal consumption and saving & Technology growth, if present\\
AK & Growth from the chosen saving path & A non-diminishing reproducible return\\
Skill accumulation & Work--learning split and balanced growth & A linear skill-formation opportunity\\
Innovation & Research effort, patent value, and growth & Research productivity, rights, and market structure\\
Semi-endogenous experiment & Conditional technology growth & Research share and labor growth in the calculation\\
Static allocation & Output from given stocks & Technologies, complementary inputs, and wedges\\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Endogenous does not mean parameter-free. Preferences, learning productivity,
research technology, and legal rules remain specified primitives. The claim
is that the accumulation or innovation choice, and the resulting growth,
have been determined inside the stated environment.

Ask which stock grows, what resources it requires, whose marginal return
supports the choice, and which restriction keeps that return from fading.
Then distinguish a higher level, a faster transition, and a permanently higher
growth rate. None is unimportant, but they are different conclusions.

These models isolate growth mechanisms rather than explaining every stage
of development. Adoption, institutions, openness, demography, and distribution
can change the relevant constraints and returns. The open-economy and policy
parts will add those links. The next chapter compares the growth mechanisms
through concrete economic situations and brings them together before we turn
to fluctuations.
